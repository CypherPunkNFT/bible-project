## Decisions and limits

### Talents — Matthew 25:14–30

**Observation:** entrusted property; different initial sums; identical commendation in verses 21 and 23; the accusation of harshness belongs to the servant. **Interpretation:** faithful service during the master's absence, with readiness made active. Henry's treatment of verses 24–27 explains the reply as exposing inconsistency within the servant's own excuse; this avoids adopting the accusation as the narrator's description of God. O’Donnell independently connects the story to readiness. **Application:** use what is entrusted without making financial success a measure of Christian worth. **Limit:** application to natural abilities is not the lexical meaning of talent; no modern monetary equivalent is supplied. Judgment is retained rather than softened into a productivity lesson.

### Minas — Luke 19:11–27

**Observation:** Luke states the immediate-kingdom expectation; departure, return and reckoning follow; the unproductive servant and the hostile citizens receive different outcomes. **Interpretation followed:** Henry's royal-return/stewardship reading fits the stated introduction and Luke 19:38. **Material alternative:** Bolt reads the ruler as oppressive and the third servant as a protester; the ruler's violence and Luke 22:25–27 make that contrast worth considering. The note explains why 19:11 and the reckoning favor the main reading, while refusing to equate every royal action with Jesus. **Application:** faithful responsibility, never a mandate for disciples to punish unbelief; Luke 6:27–36 governs their conduct toward enemies. **Limits:** the alternatives remain disputed. Bolt's page includes an AD 4 date for Archelaus and a statement about ten minas each; neither is adopted. Luke's ten minas distributed among ten servants and reports about one mina control the wording. No historical reconstruction is needed for this note.

### Royal wedding and garment — Matthew 22:1–14

**Observation:** both bad and good are gathered; the king inspects the guests; the garment is not explicitly interpreted. **Interpretation:** generous invitation requires a fitting response. Henry's treatment joins Christ's righteousness with Christian renewal; O’Donnell emphasizes faith expressed in deeds. These are related emphases, not manufactured opposites. **Application:** transformed response to grace. **Limits:** no supplied-garment custom is asserted; the scene does not establish church dress rules, condemn poverty or provide population statistics about salvation. The severity of exclusion remains visible.

### Shrewd manager — Luke 16:1–13

**Observation:** threatened loss of position, reduced debts, praise for shrewdness, followed by demands for faithfulness and exclusive service to God. **Interpretation:** urgent preparation for the future is the stable comparison. Henry takes the reductions as further dishonesty. Bock tentatively prefers commission surrender, partly to explain the praise, while acknowledging alternatives. **Limit:** commission, interest and principal reconstructions are not narrated facts; the study does not resolve the bookkeeping. **Application:** generous stewardship without treating either fraud or purchased salvation as the lesson. Luke 12:32–34 supplies an explicitly identified wider connection between the kingdom's gift and generosity.

### Midnight friend — Luke 11:5–13

**Observation:** a request, an objection, then teaching about asking and the Father's good gift. Repeated visits are not narrated. **Interpretation:** Henry stresses perseverance; Bolt stresses boldness. The note attributes both and distinguishes a reading of importunity from extra events added to the plot. **Application:** confident prayer for another's need. **Limits:** the good Father is not exhausted into compliance; the Holy Spirit is the gift named in Luke. Henry's imaginative details about the household are not treated as textual facts. No claim that lexical debate is settled, and no guarantee of every requested outcome.

### Rich man and Lazarus — Luke 16:19–31

**Observation:** need at the gate, reversal, fixed gulf, living brothers, Moses and the prophets. **Interpretation:** Henry treats the warning about life after death seriously; Bock discusses the intermediate state and reads wealth in Luke's wider context, including Zacchaeus. The notes distinguish real eschatological warning from a complete literal map and distinguish poverty from saving merit. **Application:** hear Scripture and respond to the neighbor now. **Limits:** the story's naming of Lazarus does not establish identity with John 11 or certify a historical report. The note does not settle every doctrinal dispute about the intermediate state.

### Sheep and goats — Matthew 25:31–46

**Observation:** the King identifies care for his brethren with care for himself; the inheritance and judgment both matter. **Interpretation followed:** disciples, including vulnerable messengers, supported by Matthew 12:49–50 and 10:40–42 and the O’Donnell treatment. **Material alternative/application:** Benedict XVI's section 15 extends the identification pastorally to those in need generally. It is explicitly described as a broader application, not presented as if the encyclical supplies an argument about Matthew's terminology. **Grace and deeds:** Henry treats acts of love as evidence rather than purchase; Ephesians 2:8–10 is labeled a wider theological connection, not a substitute for Matthew. **Application:** concrete care, with Luke 10 preventing an excuse to neglect outsiders. **Limit:** this is a reasoned study reading, not a declaration of denominational consensus.
