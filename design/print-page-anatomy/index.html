Academic studies/The Bible: formation & transmission/05

Printed editions · Page layers · Printers and editors

From manuscriptto printed Bible

How the Bible went from hand-copied leaves to the editions on scholars’ desks today — told through the pages themselves. Take six landmark printed pages apart layer by layer, follow twenty-five editions and translations on one timeline, and meet the patrons, editors, printers and correctors behind each.

Why it matters. Every Bible you read rests on choices made on these pages: which manuscripts to trust, which language goes in the main column, what is pushed to the margin. The press fixed those choices in thousands of identical copies — and those copies became the text that later readers translated, argued over and revised.

The argument behind the change

Did Erasmus make a promise?

A familiar account says he promised to insert the clause if a Greek copy could be found. H. J. de Jonge revisited the supposed evidence in 1980. Inspect the wording he quotes from Erasmus’s reply to Edward Lee.

Si mihi contigisset unum exemplar

“If a single copy had come into my hands…”

May 1520 reply · quoted in de Jonge, p. 385
Study rendering of the opening words
A past condition, not a future pledge.

The continuation says what Erasmus would have done. De Jonge reads it as a defense of his earlier editions. That weakens the promise story; it does not reveal every motive behind the 1522 decision.

Confessional pressure made this more than a question of wording. The clause had been used in arguments about the Trinity. McDonald’s later monograph places the controversy in that wider social history.

03Make a book

From a hand to a repeatable impression

Copying, selecting a reading, and reproducing a page are separate acts. A new technology changes their scale without making them the same task.

Before the press

A parchment book began with prepared skins, ruled lines and gatherings. A scribe copied; corrections, decorated initials and binding involved further work. This is a European parchment workflow, not a recipe for every manuscript tradition.

Getty — The Making of a Medieval Book (opens in a new tab)

Inside the print shop

At Plantin’s workshop, compositors assembled cast metal letters into lines and pages; press workers inked the setting and pulled impressions. Correctors checked proofs. One setting can repeat a mistake as readily as a correct reading.

The exercise models this mechanism, not an event in a particular Bible. Movable type in Asia predates Gutenberg’s European workshop.

Museum Plantin-Moretus — The printing workshop (opens in a new tab)Library of Congress — Printing in Asia (opens in a new tab)
Who made the letters?

Punchcutters shaped letters in reverse. A punch formed a matrix used to cast reusable metal type. Plantin worked with specialists including Claude Garamond, Guillaume Le Bé and Robert Granjon. Their craft helped make multilingual printing possible.

From the exercise to the objects

One edition.
Different copies.

Mari Agata compared digital images of Gutenberg Bibles to identify changes made during printing. Her dissertation summary reports 117 changes on 88 pages, excluding spacing differences. Some changes improved the setting; others introduced errors. These are results from her studied copies, not a count of every difference in every surviving Bible.

Collation means systematic comparison. A bound volume can combine sheets from different states of the press: “corrected copy” is often too simple a description.

04 · Read the architecture

A page is more than its main text

Open a printed Bible and the eye goes straight to the large type in the middle. But most pages carry several kinds of writing at once: headings, a translation beside the original, scribal notes, cross-references and, in modern editions, a band of evidence at the foot.

Each layer was put there by someone, for a reason: a rubricator’s red initial, a Masorete’s note, a Latin column for comparison, an editor’s list of manuscripts that read differently.

Why it matters

Mistake one layer for another and the argument goes wrong. A Latin column is not a second ancient witness; a cross-reference is not a variant. Telling the layers apart is the first skill in reading any edition.

What the translators said / 1611

Why leave alternatives in the margin?

“variety of Translations is profitable”

The King James translators’ preface defends alternative senses where wording is difficult. Its theological confidence coexists with acknowledged linguistic uncertainty. This is their stated policy; testing its practice requires examining individual verses.

A marginal alternative may concern how to translate a word, or which source reading to follow. Those questions need different evidence.

The Translators to the Reader — 1611 preface, modernized transcription (opens in a new tab)Modernized transcription of the 1611 preface via an 1833 reprint.

Cambridge, Massachusetts / 1663 → 1685

Behind the name “Eliot Bible”

John Eliot’s Massachusett Bible depended on Indigenous linguistic and printing skill. Job Nesutan assisted translation; James Printer, a Nipmuc compositor, worked on setting and correction. The revised 1685 edition is another reminder that a translation’s history continues after its first printing.

Bridwell Library — Eliot Bible, revised edition (1685) (opens in a new tab)Harvard Peabody Museum — Literacy and the Printing Press (opens in a new tab)
Language knowledge→Roman-letter setting→Informed correction

The missionary project served colonial conversion. Much later, the Bible and other records became resources for Wôpanâak language reclamation. A book’s later use can differ profoundly from its makers’ purpose.

06Look beneath the line

What changes beneath the line?

A critical apparatus is a compact record of textual evidence. It can change even when the reading text above it does not. That makes the foot of a page a place of argument.

Habakkuk 3:1 · selected phraseBHS / Elliger, 1970

לַחֲבַקּוּק הַנָּבִיא

of Habakkuk the prophet

At the foot of the page

No note at this phrase.

The absence of an entry tells us what this apparatus reports. It cannot establish that every witness agrees.

Normalized teaching excerpt and paraphrased apparatus comparison, following Prinsloo (2022). Not a facsimile or full apparatus.

Follow the reasoning

The largest type is only one layer of evidence.

The modern Biblia Hebraica series changed its base: Kittel’s first two editions used Ben Hayyim’s printed tradition; the third turned to the Leningrad Codex under Paul Kahle’s influence. BHS and BHQ continue with that manuscript as their reference text.

German Bible Society — History of Biblia Hebraica (opens in a new tab)

Keeping the reference text stable makes other readings easier to locate. It also asks something of the reader: separate what the base manuscript has from what the editor considers earlier.

The Habakkuk example, reported by Prinsloo, shows a change in the reporting of evidence. It does not demonstrate a newly discovered manuscript or a newly changed Hebrew sentence.
