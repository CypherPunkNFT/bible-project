04 · Read the architecture

A page is more than its main text

Open a printed Bible and the eye goes straight to the large type in the middle. Yet almost every page carries several kinds of writing at once: a heading, a translation set beside the original, notes on how the text was copied, references to other passages, and in modern editions a dense band of evidence at the foot.

Each of those layers was put there by someone, for a reason. A rubricator painted the red initial by hand after the sheet left the press. A Masorete's note records how scribes counted and spelled. A Latin column stands beside the Greek so a scholar can compare. An editor's apparatus lists the manuscripts that read differently. They share one page, but they do different jobs and carry different weight.

Why it matters

Mistake one layer for another and the argument goes wrong. A Latin column beside the Greek is not a second ancient witness. A marginal reference is not a textual variant. A footnote that says “some manuscripts add” records evidence; it is not a verdict. Telling the layers apart is the first skill in reading any edition.
