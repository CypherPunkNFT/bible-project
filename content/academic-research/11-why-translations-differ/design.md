# Why translations differ: design proposal

The research record: [11-why-translations-differ.json](../11-why-translations-differ.json). There is no page yet, and this file proposes what one could be. Nothing here is built.

## What this page is for

A reader opens two Bibles at the same verse and finds different words. They have been told that modern Bibles "took verses out", or that the King James is "word for word", or that it does not matter which one they read. They leave able to ask of any difference whether it lies in the text being translated or in the translating, to name the texts underneath (the Masoretic Hebrew, the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Septuagint, and the Greek New Testament's printed editions and families), to read the notes in their own Bible, and to choose a translation for reading and for study. The question it carries: is this difference in the text, or in the translating?

This study takes in the Scripture & Theology draft 35, Versions and translation, which will not have its own page. Its reader's guide becomes section 06, in a plainer voice that the page says it is borrowing, and its three English examples (charity, prevent, conversation) move into section 03, now quoted from the Berean.

## The material's own shape

Every difference the record holds goes back to one of two decisions: which wording to translate, where the copies disagree, and how to carry that wording into English. The Berean's own notes already sort themselves this way. A note that names an ancient text (MT, DSS, LXX, BYZ, TR) marks the first decision; a note that begins "Or" or "Literally" marks the second. So the shape is a fork, and the reader's own Bible prints the signpost at each one. The page is built as that fork.

## Directions, and one honest gap

The study standard asks for three or four directions built as mock-ups, then a stop for the owner to choose. That stop is not possible inside a remote job, because the owner cannot answer mid-job, and this job is a draft only. So three directions were weighed in writing, and one is recommended. The choice is the owner's.

1. **One verse, two rulers (recommended).** Each verse is one line of the Berean. Beneath it run two short rulers. The first, *which words*, has named stops for the texts (Masoretic, Dead Sea, Septuagint, other early witnesses; or Textus Receptus, Majority, critical), coloured by the site's fixed colours for the Hebrew, Greek Old Testament, Greek New Testament and early-translation traditions. The second, *how carried*, has three named stops: formal, functional, paraphrase. Each named translation is a small mark on both rulers; choosing one prints its wording beside the Berean's. The pattern shows at once (rule A5 of the study standard, the central comparison seen early): at a text verse the marks spread along the first ruler and sit together on the second; at a rendering verse the reverse. The evidence can bear it, because both rulers are named stops, not continuous scales, so nothing is placed more precisely than a preface or a note allows.
2. **Parallel columns, the way Origen laid out his Hexapla.** Six Bibles side by side down a passage. Familiar and honest, but rejected as the lead: it is the dense comparison block the owner has ruled out, it makes every difference look alike, and it never says why one arose. Its good part survives as section 02's table of three Greek texts, held to nine rows, each tagged with its pattern.
3. **A family tree of Bibles.** King James to Revised to American Standard to World English; Berean to Majority Standard; the printed Greek from Erasmus to Scrivener. It explains why translations agree, which matters (section 06, step 06), but not why a verse differs, and most lines of descent are not stated by the Bibles themselves. It survives only as a small drawing in section 06, with a line drawn only where a preface says so.

Against the site's other studies: a glossed page (30), a wheel (29), a cutaway house (27), rulers and staves (32), coins on a line (34, draft) and an inspection desk (01) are taken. The nearest neighbour is A passage through the manuscripts (02), which compares Greek manuscripts' readings; this page never weighs a reading. It shows what English Bibles do with readings already weighed, and with idioms no manuscript disputes. Strong's numbers (26), How Hebrew works (28) and How New Testament Greek works (31) are linked wherever a difference turns on a form, and nothing of theirs is repeated.

## Drawings

All new, in the Reading & interpreting field's colour, in the site's one hand (thin stroke, round caps, faint wash), labelled "Illustrative forms · not facsimiles".

- **Header:** an open book whose two pages carry the same short line in two scripts, with a fine fork of lines running between them. In a faint circle with a dotted orbit.
- **Section 02 card miniatures:** a bound codex (Masoretic), a rolled scroll (Dead Sea), a codex with Greek letters (Septuagint), three small stacked books (early witnesses); for the New Testament, three printed title blocks for the three editions held.
- **Section 03:** a single bridge drawn three ways, close to the water, high and level, and as a ferry, for the three ways of carrying across.

Motion: the orbit turns slowly, and on the two rulers a faint glint passes along whichever ruler the chosen verse spreads on. All of it stops when the reader asks for reduced motion.

## Behaviour

The view lives in the address: `verse` (the chosen verse), `edition` (the translation shown beside the Berean), `open` (the record sheet). An unknown value falls back to the first verse. The rulers are pressed buttons with visible names, never colour alone. Hebrew and Greek appear only in the table of section 02 and on the record sheets, marked for direction and not for machine translation; the reader never needs to read them.

## What is checked, and what is not

A throwaway script in the job's workspace checked, against the site's own text files, that every verse in the record exists in the Berean with its stated phrase, that all 41 quoted renderings in eleven verse records are word for word as the named translation holds them (Psalm 22 and 40 read at their Greek numbers in Brenton), that each quoted Berean note is exactly the note on that verse, and that every source named in the record is registered. A second script made the counts. A build should repeat both. None of this proves that any reading or rendering is right. Publishers' statements and four encyclopedia pages were read through a tool that reports on a page, and are to be rechecked before a page is built.
