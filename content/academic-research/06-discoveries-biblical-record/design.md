## Chosen idea and alternatives

The question is how an ancient object becomes a historical argument. The most useful contrast is between what physically survives, what is editorially restored, and what is historically inferred. A findspot map would explain provenance but need extra spatial data and could distract from reading. A continuous discovery timeline would clarify modern research history but mix object dates with excavation dates. The chosen approach is an excavation-and-reading-room composition: an illustrated introduction, a concrete campaign case, an object collection and a sequence of distinct reading problems. It gives both objects and methods tangible visual forms without pretending to reproduce unseen details.

The existing campaign workspace remains the signature historical comparison. The new visual collection connects its method to six different discoveries, and the surface-method workspace explains why improved imaging cannot interpret a disputed mark automatically. The page uses the ambition of the reference study and teacher pages inspected during this thread, not their exact layouts or unsupported quantitative charts.

## Chosen organizing idea

**Two places, one siege.** Begin inside one substantial case rather than asking readers to choose from a collection before they know what to look for. Nineveh's commemorative image and Lachish's ground occupy equal, stable positions. Written accounts sit immediately underneath. Changing the question changes the captions and bounded conclusion, never the objects' positions.

This makes a relationship visible that prose alone obscures: evidence useful for Lachish's capture can be irrelevant to the cause of Jerusalem's survival. The pair of royal records is explicitly identified as coming from the same institution. There is no document-count score or claim that more selected records mechanically produce certainty.

## Signature visual specification

The worktable is a single bordered editorial spread, cream on warm paper. Two contexts split the width equally: a sandstone display field at left and an olive earth section at right. Their positions express distinct contexts, **not distance, chronology or degree of certainty**. Underneath, two ordinary text areas identify royal annals and Kings. These are not stylized ancient manuscripts.

The left illustration is an architectural display metaphor: a broad recessed panel, substantial stone surfaces, simple slab divisions and an explicitly modern editorial label. It does not reproduce the palace room or relief scene. The right illustration is a conceptual outside/inside section: an external ramp, wall, mound and internal counter-ramp. Its dimensions, stone pattern, wall height and layer lines are illustrative. It is not a stratigraphic drawing and must never be used to date a particular depicted wall. No soldiers, siege engines, ancient glyphs or unseen object details are invented.

Question controls:

- “Was Lachish captured?” → named depiction / siege and destruction / wider campaign / campaign setting.
- “Why date Level III to 701?” → campaign association / relative sequence / historical anchor / named campaign.
- “Why did Jerusalem survive?” → does not answer / different city / confinement claimed / divine deliverance.

Each contribution remains next to its source link. The final dark-green band contains the dossier's finding and caution in full. No probability, confidence bar, fake count or causal arrow is added. The meaningful action is changing the question, not accumulating evidence tokens.
