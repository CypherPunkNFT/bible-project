## Worked-example evidence

1. **Philippians 4:10–20.** Contrasts in 11–12 govern the discussion of strength in 13. Verse 14 affirms sharing Paul's affliction; 15–18 describes repeated support and the gift through Epaphroditus. Contentment is not interpreted as rejecting material help. No prison location or personal-outcome guarantee is inferred.
2. **Jeremiah 29:1–14.** Verses 1–4 identify captives taken from Jerusalem to Babylon and name the sender and addressees. Verses 5–7 direct life in the city; 8–9 warn about false prophecy; 10 gives the seventy-year setting; 11–14 concern the LORD's purpose, prayer and return. The draft distinguishes later learning from direct reassignment of the promise. No calculation of start/end dates is needed.
3. **Luke 15:1–7.** The narrator reports the complaint in 2, identifies a parable in 3 and records Jesus' explanation in 7. The draft concentrates on loss, finding and rejoicing. It does not identify each of the ninety-nine or explain away the final phrase about repentance. Matthew's related passage is outside this particular exercise, not an omitted proof of identical context.
4. **Romans 12:1–8.** The sequence supports connecting renewal, humility, body membership and gifts. The larger reference of “therefore” is acknowledged, not exhaustively settled. Connecting words invite study; they are not a mechanical rule by which every translation's punctuation yields one indisputable outline.
5. **Matthew 4:1–11; Deuteronomy 8:1–10.** Compare Matthew 4:4 with Deuteronomy 8:3 and its wilderness setting. Matthew 4:6–7 demonstrates competing uses of scriptural wording. The draft does not newly interpret Psalm 91 in full; that would require reading its complete context. Any broader Israel/Jesus typology remains an interpretation requiring demonstrated evidence.
6. **James 1:19–27.** The text joins hearing, speech, wrath, reception, doing, the mirror comparison, continuing, the tongue, care and conduct. Application examples are explicitly proposed actions rather than extra requirements attributed to James. The KJV's “glass” means the reflecting object in the comparison; no separate word-history claim is made.
