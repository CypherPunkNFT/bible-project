# 34 — Reading a passage in context

First content draft ready for owner review · 2026-10-13 · Not final-copy approval.

## Purpose and scope

Develop practical questions for careful reading. The [draft](content.md) distinguishes observation, interpretation and application through six worked examples. It builds on studies 31–33 rather than creating another general introduction to biblical books or literary collections.

## Developed sections

1. Paragraph: Philippians 4:10–20, contentment and shared material help.
2. Speaker and audience: Jeremiah 29:1–14, the letter to captives and the promised return.
3. Genre: Luke 15:1–7, a parable answering a complaint.
4. Argument: Romans 12:1–8, mercies, renewal, sober judgment and one body.
5. Earlier Scripture: Matthew 4:1–11 with Deuteronomy 8:1–10.
6. Application: James 1:19–27, hearing, doing and particular acts of care.

Two subdivisions per section are editorial units, not a layout decision. Six full-verse anchors preserve KJV wording; explanations use ordinary prose.

## Boundaries and questions for review

- This is a set of reading practices, not a claim that a six-step method resolves every interpretive disagreement.
- Original hearers and circumstances remain visible when considering current application.
- Philippians' strength language is read with hunger, plenty and generosity; Jeremiah's promise retains its named recipients and seventy-year setting.
- Do not impose an independent symbolic meaning on every parable detail. Luke 15's complete three-parable sequence offers further study; this worked example covers 1–7 only.
- Romans 12:1 points to a larger argument. The draft identifies the need to read it rather than pretending eight verses exhaust that argument.
- Explicit quotation, shared imagery and editorial cross-reference are different kinds of evidence.
- A proposed personal action is not automatically a new divine command.
- No site design, routes, builds or infrastructure changes.

## Connections and next step

Study 31 concerns whole books, 32 whole letters, and 33 the wider collections. Study 17 treats Jesus' teaching in greater depth; 27 treats Scripture's witness. These examples teach a method without replacing those studies.
