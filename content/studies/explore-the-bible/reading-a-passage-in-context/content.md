# Reading a passage in context

Working content draft · Study 34 · Explore the Bible · Owner review pending

A verse belongs to a passage. Someone is speaking, something has prompted the words, and the sentences around them help us understand what is being said. Reading in context begins by giving those things our attention.

Keep three kinds of statement distinguishable as you study. An observation identifies something in the text: Paul names hunger and plenty. An interpretation explains its meaning: he is speaking of Christ's strength amid differing circumstances. An application considers a response: I can bring present need before Christ while learning contentment. The explanation and response should be accountable to what we have read.

These six examples practise that movement. Read the main passage before the explanation, then return to it. Write down what you can show from the words, what you think they mean and what still needs consideration. Careful reading makes room for questions while seeking a faithful response to what is understood.

## 01 Read the sentences around the verse

**Main reading:** Philippians 4:10–20.

> I can do all things through Christ which strengtheneth me.

Philippians 4:13 · KJV

### What are the “all things” here?

Paul rejoices that the Philippians' care for him has flourished again. He then explains that he has learned contentment: he knows how to be abased and how to abound, how to be full and how to be hungry, how to have plenty and how to suffer need. His statement about Christ strengthening him stands within that explanation.

These are observations available before consulting an account of Paul's circumstances elsewhere. Read verses 11–13 together and name the contrasts. They give substance to the claim about strength. The passage directs attention to dependence on Christ through changing conditions, rather than promising success in every undertaking a reader might select.

The following sentence matters too. Paul says the Philippians have done well to share in his affliction. Contentment does not make their gift unnecessary or their care unimportant. He speaks appreciatively of their repeated help and of the things received through Epaphroditus. Christ's strengthening and the believers' practical generosity belong in the same passage.

### Make the summary fit the paragraph

Try summarizing the passage in one sentence that includes both contentment and the gift. Then check whether your sentence accounts for Paul's lack as well as his abundance, and for his gratitude as well as his strength. A summary that omits half the paragraph may reveal that a familiar verse has drawn your attention away from its setting.

A possible application is to learn dependence on Christ amid need while receiving help gratefully and sharing in another's trouble. That response follows the paragraph's concerns. It does not require claiming that Paul has guaranteed a particular result for your plans.

When the bounds of a passage are unclear, read farther in both directions. Look for a change of subject, an explanation, a contrast or a closing thanksgiving. A verse number helps you find words; their relationships help you understand them.

**Consider:** How do verses 11–12 and 14–18 shape your reading of verse 13?

**Reflect:** Which receives less attention in your own reading: learning contentment or sharing practically in another's need?

## 02 Identify the speaker and the first hearers

**Main reading:** Jeremiah 29:1–14.

> For I know the thoughts that I think toward you, saith the LORD, thoughts of peace, and not of evil, to give you an expected end.

Jeremiah 29:11 · KJV

### The letter names its recipients

The chapter begins by identifying a letter sent by Jeremiah from Jerusalem to people carried captive to Babylon. The address includes elders, priests, prophets and the people. In verse 4 the LORD addresses those carried away from Jerusalem to Babylon. This identifies the “you” before we arrive at the promise in verse 11.

The instructions are particular: build houses, plant gardens, establish households, seek the city's peace and pray for it. The letter also warns against false prophecy. The promise of return is set alongside seventy years at Babylon. Hope does not remove the need to live faithfully through a prolonged period away from home.

Record those details before describing what the promise means for a reader today. The first hearers are a people in a named situation, and the promised return belongs to that situation. Keeping them in view prevents us from turning their promised future into a timetable for every private ambition.

### Learn from the promise without replacing its hearers

The passage gives readers reason to consider the LORD's faithfulness, prayer and a people's life while they wait. Those are subjects grounded in its actual words. Their application should acknowledge the difference between learning from this promise and claiming that its precise circumstances have become our own.

For example, a reader can consider what faithful prayer and concern for neighbours might require during an unwanted season. That is a proposed response informed by the passage. It is not the same as saying that Jeremiah has promised this reader a particular job, house or date of relief.

Name both the connection and the difference. Who receives the original command or promise? What does the passage show about God and his people? How is your proposed response supported? These questions preserve the force of the promise by allowing it to mean something definite.

**Consider:** What does the letter ask its recipients to do before the promised return?

**Reflect:** How might hope and responsible attention to present neighbours belong together in your circumstances?

## 03 Ask what kind of speech you are hearing

**Main reading:** Luke 15:1–7.

> And when he hath found it, he layeth it on his shoulders, rejoicing.

Luke 15:5 · KJV

### Hear the parable within the encounter

Publicans and sinners draw near to hear Jesus. The Pharisees and scribes murmur that he receives sinners and eats with them. Jesus then speaks a parable about a lost sheep, its being sought and found, and the invitation to rejoice. The opening encounter gives us a question with which to hear the story: how does this rejoicing answer the complaint about his welcome?

Follow what happens in the parable. The sheep is lost; the man goes after it; he finds it and rejoices; he calls friends and neighbours to rejoice with him. Jesus then speaks of joy in heaven over one sinner who repents. His own explanation should guide our account of the comparison.

The story's details help us feel the movement from loss to joy. We do not need to assign a separate hidden meaning to every object or action before hearing that movement. If proposing further symbolism, show what supports it rather than presenting an imaginative association as the explanation Jesus gives.

### Let the form guide the question

A parable works through a comparison. Read it as the story Jesus tells within this encounter, not as a command that every hearer must reproduce its details literally. The passage calls attention to finding the lost and rejoicing, with a particular challenge to the complaint that prompted it.

The same habit helps elsewhere. In a narrative, distinguish the narrator's explanation from a character's speech. In a prayer, listen to what is being asked and what is being confessed. In a proverb, attend to its comparison and reasons. Recognizing the form helps you ask what a sentence is doing before deciding what response it requires.

Here an application can ask whether we welcome the repentance that Jesus says brings heavenly joy. That question grows from the stated explanation and the surrounding encounter, rather than from an invented meaning for the shoulders or the journey home.

**Consider:** What connections can you demonstrate between the complaint in verse 2 and the rejoicing in verses 5–7?

**Reflect:** How do you respond when someone you have kept at a distance turns toward God?

## 04 Follow the reasons and the movement

**Main reading:** Romans 12:1–8.

> So we, being many, are one body in Christ, and every one members one of another.

Romans 12:5 · KJV

### Read the appeal and its reasons together

Paul beseeches the brethren by the mercies of God to present their bodies a living sacrifice. He continues with the renewing of the mind, then warns each person against thinking too highly of himself. He explains the many members of one body and their differing offices, and proceeds to gifts and their exercise.

Notice the words that join these statements: therefore, for, as and so. They invite us to follow relationships between sentences rather than collect separate subjects. The chapter's opening “therefore” also sends us back to the preceding argument of Romans. Reading that larger argument is necessary for a fuller account of the mercies to which Paul appeals.

Within verses 1–8 we can already observe that renewed thinking is followed by sober judgment about oneself and attention to others in one body. That is an interpretation supported by the sequence. It keeps renewal connected to the shared life addressed by the following verses.

### Test the outline against the details

Give each movement a brief description: offering oneself to God; renewed and sober thinking; one body with differing members; the exercise of gifts. Then ask how each relates to the next. An outline should help you explain the passage's movement, not merely divide it into convenient lengths.

The examples of giving, ruling and showing mercy bring the instruction into particular actions. Paul's appeal is not exhausted by a private resolve to think differently. The relationships and responsibilities named in the passage help show what the response involves.

When a connection remains uncertain, record that uncertainty. A nearby sentence may illustrate, explain, contrast with or draw a conclusion from the previous one. Read it more than once and test your proposed account against the words. A careful explanation should be open to correction by the passage itself.

**Consider:** How does the image of one body support Paul's warning against thinking too highly of oneself?

**Reflect:** What responsibility toward others becomes visible when you read renewal and gifts together?

## 05 Read an earlier passage when it is quoted

**Main readings:** Matthew 4:1–11; Deuteronomy 8:1–10.

> But he answered and said, It is written, Man shall not live by bread alone, but by every word that proceedeth out of the mouth of God.

Matthew 4:4 · KJV

### Follow the quotation to its setting

Matthew tells of Jesus in the wilderness, hungry after fasting. The tempter tells him to command stones to become bread. Jesus answers with the words about living by what proceeds from God's mouth. Read Deuteronomy 8 alongside that answer: Israel is told to remember the wilderness, hunger and the manna with which the LORD fed them.

Deuteronomy explains the instruction associated with that experience: man does not live by bread only. Its surrounding words concern remembering, humility, obedience and the LORD's provision. The quotation in Matthew therefore brings us to an earlier passage with a setting and purpose of its own.

First establish the connection that the wording supports. Then explain what the earlier passage contributes to your reading of Jesus' answer. A proposed larger comparison between Israel's experience and Jesus' testing should remain an argued interpretation, with its evidence shown, rather than an assumed meaning attached to every detail.

### A quotation must still be understood

Later in the same account the devil also introduces words with “it is written,” using them in a demand that Jesus cast himself down. Jesus answers with the command not to tempt the Lord. The exchange shows why recognizing scriptural wording is not the end of discernment: we must consider how it is being used and what action it is being made to support.

Do the same when following a cross-reference. Read the linked passage, identify the shared words or subject and ask what kind of connection is present. Is the later writer explicitly quoting it, retelling it or using related imagery? Or has a reader suggested a comparison? These connections can be useful without all having the same evidential force.

Here the practical question begins with dependence and obedience in testing. Keep that response anchored in what Jesus says and does, rather than assuming every proposed use of a biblical sentence carries the sentence's authority.

**Consider:** What does Deuteronomy's account of hunger and manna contribute to Matthew 4:4?

**Reflect:** How can you test whether a familiar verse is being used to support something its passage actually teaches?

## 06 Move from hearing to a faithful response

**Main reading:** James 1:19–27.

> But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves.

James 1:22 · KJV

### Observe, explain and respond

James tells the brethren to be swift to hear, slow to speak and slow to wrath. He calls them to receive the word with meekness and to do it. The comparison with a person looking in a glass and then forgetting gives force to the warning against hearing without responding. The passage continues with the tongue, the fatherless and widows in affliction, and being unspotted from the world.

An observation names these instructions and the comparison. An interpretation explains their relationship: receiving the word is to become a continuing, active response rather than a moment of attention followed by forgetting. An application might identify a particular act of listening, restrained speech or care for someone in affliction.

Keep the steps visible. If you propose visiting someone this week, that is your concrete response to the concern James names; the passage has not prescribed that person's name or your chosen hour. This distinction lets application become particular without turning every practical decision into a command attributed to Scripture.

### Return to the passage after choosing a response

Write one action you can take, then check it against the reading. Does it address something James actually says? Does it account for the care and conduct he names, or simply reinforce a project you already preferred? What would continuing in the word look like after the first action?

Some passages require more study before an application is clear. Keep that question open without using uncertainty about one detail to evade what is plain. Discuss your reading with others and listen to reasons that might correct it. The goal is neither a clever interpretation nor an impressive plan, but attentive hearing that bears fruit in conduct.

For your next passage, use the same sequence: read its surroundings, identify the speaker and hearers, notice its form, follow its reasoning, examine its scriptural connections and consider a response. Record observations, explanations and applications separately, then check that they belong together.

**Consider:** Which practical concerns in verses 19–27 give content to being a doer of the word?

**Reflect:** What specific response will you make, and when will you return to the passage to consider whether you have continued in it?
