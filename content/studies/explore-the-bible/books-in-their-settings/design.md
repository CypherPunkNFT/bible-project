## Direction

Teach one habit: read a verse inside the book it belongs to. The study's own picture carries the page. A lamp in the
ancient world lit the next few steps, not the whole road (Psalm 119:105), so the page gives the reader one step at a
time and lets each part light only what it needs. Its two worked examples come at the habit from opposite sides, and
the page draws them that way: Jonah from the whole book inward, Jeremiah 29:11 from one verse outward. One direction
was built, because the text already existed.

## The page

- **Opening.** The standard heading: the study trail (Study / Explore the Bible), the four concepts in gold capitals,
  the title *Reading the Bible* with *well* in gold italic, the subtitle, the lead and Psalm 119:105. Beside it, in a
  faint circle and dotted orbit, an ancient clay oil lamp set low on a path of stepping stones: its light falls on the
  next three stones and the rest of the path is only dotted. The motto under it: "One lamp. One step. / Then the next."
  No person is drawn. A bar of counts, each computed from the Berean and each saying what it means: the 66 books of
  the library; John's 21 chapters (three weeks at a chapter a day); Jonah's 48 verses (one sitting); the two years
  Hananiah promised against the seventy the letter gave; the references on the page.
- **00 A library, not one book.** The two opening paragraphs, then the library as a shelf of 66 spines in the order of
  the Berean, each as tall as its number of chapters and coloured by its part of the Bible. The five books the study
  opens (Psalms, Jonah, Jeremiah, John, Acts) stand out and each links to the part that reads it; the rest are faint.
  The selection note sits under the shelf.
- **Each part takes the colour of the book it opens.** Parts 01 and 02 the Gospels & Acts violet (John, Acts), 03
  the page's own teal, 04 and 05 the Prophets' blue (Jonah, Jeremiah), 06 back to the lamp. The colour key is the shelf.
- **01 Where to start: John.** The text, John 20:30–31 printed in full, the three things to notice, then John as
  three weeks: 21 squares, one per chapter, in three rows of seven, each opening its chapter in the reader's sheet.
  The day's question from the text sits above the squares. Chapter 1 carries "the opening, read on and come back";
  chapter 20 carries "the purpose, 20:30–31".
- **02 A simple daily habit.** Acts 17:11 with its two halves, then the five small steps as five steps across the
  width. The missed-day paragraph is drawn as six example weeks of reading with gaps in them, shown two ways: counted as
  a streak, the count falls to nothing at every gap; counted as coming back, the gaps stay and the line simply
  resumes. The figure is labelled as an example, not a record. The care note follows.
- **03 Using the site's reader.** A sample of the reader drawn from the Berean itself, Jonah 1:15–2:2, with four
  labels floating beside it and joined to it by thin lines: the editors' heading ("Jonah's Prayer", not part of the
  text), the chapter number the scene runs straight across (added in the early 1200s), a verse number, and LORD in
  capitals (God's personal name). The open check in the text, that each feature named exists in the current reader,
  is shown where it stands.
- **04 One whole book: Jonah.** The book's 48 verses on one line in four chapter bands. The line follows the story's
  own direction of travel: level at the call, down to Joppa, down into the lowest part of the ship, into the sea, to
  the roots of the mountains, then up onto dry land and level again for chapters 3 and 4. Marked on it: the same
  command opening both halves (1:2, 3:2), the four things God appointed (fish, vine, worm, east wind), the reason
  Jonah gives only in 4:2 with a dashed arc back to his flight in 1:3, and the open end at 4:11 where the book stops on
  a question. Choosing a verse (pointer or arrow keys) shows it beneath the line. A second, small figure sets Jonah
  4:2 beside Exodus 34:6 and Joel 2:13, with a mark for each of five phrases in each verse, so the reader sees whose
  words Jonah was using. The care note with the crisis lines stands before the paragraphs on Jonah's wish to die.
- **05 One passage: Jeremiah 29:11 in its letter.** The verse alone on a plain card, as it is usually seen. Beside it,
  Jeremiah 29:1–14 set out as the letter it is, with verse 11 in its place, ten verses down. Seven notes in the margin,
  each joined by a line to its verses (who it was sent to, build houses, pray for the city, do not be deceived,
  seventy years, the promise, seek Me); pointing at a note lights its verses. Under the letter, two lines to the same
  scale: "within two years" (Hananiah, 28:3) and "seventy years" (29:10). The three kinds of statement close the part.
  On phones the notes sit above their verses.
- **06 How to keep going.** The six questions as six stepping stones, the hero's path again; the lamp sits on the
  stone chosen and its question is read beneath. Then the three helps.
- **Close.** The way of reading, the close, the invitation with John 20:31, the reader's question and "Read the
  passage again before you answer.", Jonah 4:1–11 printed in full, the lamp again, the sources, what is still to be
  checked before publishing, and a back link to Explore the Bible.
- **Every reference** opens its verses in a sheet from the right, in the Berean, and puts the reference in the
  address; Escape closes it.

## Colour

The page's ground colour is the Poetry teal of Psalm 119, where the lamp comes from; it frames the heading, the shelf
and the close. Each part then takes the colour of the book it reads, so the colour itself repeats the habit: a
passage belongs to its book. The site's gold marks the care notes and the lamp's flame, the one point of light on the
page.

## Doctrine and care

The site's voice follows the Baptist Faith and Message 2000. Scripture is "truth, without any mixture of error"
(article I), and Jonah is read as Jesus spoke of him; the teaching-story reading of some modern scholars is named as
their reconstruction and not argued down. The Jewish reading of Jonah on Yom Kippur and of Jeremiah 29:7 in the
Mishnah is shown in Jewish terms before the Christian reading. Salvation is by personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord
(article IV); no habit of reading is made a condition of God's favour, and a missed day is never drawn as failure.
Where the text marks advice ("this site's advice, drawn from long Christian practice"), the page keeps it marked as
advice. The crisis lines come before Jonah's wish to die, God's answer to Jonah (a question and shade) is shown, and
the numbers still marked "check before publishing" stay marked. No face or figure of God is drawn.
