## Passages this study owns

### Jonah 1–4 (the whole-book example)

**Context.** Jonah son of Amittai is placed by 2 Kings 14:25 in the reign of Jeroboam II of Israel (8th century BC). Nineveh was a major city of Assyria, which conquered the northern kingdom in 722 BC (2 Kings 17). The book names no Assyrian king and gives no date of writing. Structure: two halves opening with the same command (1:1–2; 3:1–2); Jonah's "going down" (1:3, 5; 2:6); four things God "appoints" (1:17; 4:6, 7, 8); the motive revealed only in 4:2, quoting the self-description of Exodus 34:6–7 (also Joel 2:13); the book ends on God's unanswered question (4:10–11).

**Main ways it is read, and who holds them.**
- *Jewish reading:* read as the haftarah at the afternoon (Minchah) service of Yom Kippur; heard as a book of repentance and of God's mercy over all His creatures. Described in Jewish terms in the study before the Christian reading.
- *As history:* the traditional reading of synagogue and church, held by this site in line with article I of the Baptist Faith and Message 2000 and supported by Jesus' reference alongside the queen of the South (Matthew 12:39–42; Luke 11:29–32).
- *As a teaching story or parable about a historical prophet:* held by some modern scholars; labelled in the study as their reconstruction. - *Christian reading:* the "sign of Jonah" as pointing to Jesus' death and resurrection; mercy to the nations (often used in missions teaching). Drawn from where the New Testament draws it.
- *On Jonah's motive:* the study follows the book's own explanation (4:2) rather than fear of danger or a reconstructed nationalism.

**Pastoral care.** Jonah 4:3 and 4:8 contain a wish to die. Crisis lines placed before the discussion; God's response (a question, shade, care) shown; no endorsement of death as relief; feelings named as passing.

### Jeremiah 29:1–14 (the passage example: 29:11 read in its letter)

**Context.** A letter to the exiles of the 597 BC deportation under Jeconiah (2 Kings 24:10–17), carried by Elasah and Gemariah (29:3). Written against Hananiah's two-year promise (Jeremiah 28:3, 11, 15). Instructions to settle (29:4–7), a warning against false prophets (29:8–9), seventy years (29:10; compare Jeremiah 25:11–12), then the promise (29:11–14). The "you" of 29:11 is plural in Hebrew.

**Main ways it is read, and who holds them.**
- *Contextual reading:* a promise to the exiled community of return after seventy years and of renewed seeking of God; applied today through God's character and the call to seek Him and serve one's city. The study's reading.
- *Jewish diaspora reading:* 29:7 as a basis for praying for the welfare of the city and government where Jews live (compare Mishnah, Avot 3:2, Rabbi Hanina).
- *Popular personal-promise reading:* 29:11 taken as a direct individual guarantee of good outcomes. The study names the limits of this without mocking those who hold it, and adds a care note.
- *Christian reading through Christ:* promises as "Yes" in Christ (2 Corinthians 1:20); some also read the exiles' situation as a picture of the church as "exiles" (1 Peter 1:1; 2:11), not developed here.
- *The seventy years:* calculated differently (for example from 605 BC to the return about 538 BC, or from the temple's destruction in 586 BC to its rebuilding about 516 BC). An open historical question; the study does not choose. Later Scripture reads it in Daniel 9:2, 2 Chronicles 36:21 and Ezra 1:1.
