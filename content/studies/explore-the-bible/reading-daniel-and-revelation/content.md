# Reading Daniel and Revelation

**Study 49 · Explore the Bible · First content draft · 2026-11-06**

Two men, more than six centuries apart, each far from home under an empire. Daniel was a young captive when "Nebuchadnezzar king of Babylon came to Jerusalem and besieged it" (Daniel 1:1 · BSB). John was "on the island of Patmos because of the word of God and my testimony about Jesus" (Revelation 1:9 · BSB). Both saw beasts rise out of the sea. Both saw a throne. And at the end of their books they were given opposite orders:

> But you, Daniel, shut up these words and seal the book until the time of the end.

Daniel 12:4 · BSB

> Then he told me, "Do not seal up the words of prophecy in this book, because the time is near."

Revelation 22:10 · BSB

What Daniel was told to close, John was told to leave open. These two books belong together, and they are written in a kind of writing that many readers find strange: visions, beasts, horns, numbers, thrones. This study is about how that kind of writing works, and about the comfort it was written to give.

Two neighbouring studies are not repeated here. Study 30, The hope to come, walks Christ's return, the resurrection, the judgment and the new creation, and lays out the maps Baptists use for the thousand years of Revelation 20. Study 12, Names and titles of Jesus, reads the "One like the Son of Man" of Daniel 7. Carry three questions into every vision you read:

1. **Does the vision explain itself?** Let the angel's interpretation speak before yours.
2. **Where has Scripture used this picture before?** The Old Testament is the dictionary.
3. **What was it for?** Ask what it told its first hearers, then what it asks of you.

## 01 What kind of writing this is

**Main readings:** Daniel 1:1–2; 2:4; 7:1; 12:4; Revelation 1:1–4, 11; Matthew 24:15.

The opening words of the last book of the Bible tell you what it is: "This is the revelation of Jesus Christ" (Revelation 1:1 · BSB).

### What the text shows

Daniel falls into two halves. Chapters 1–6 are stories told about Daniel and his friends at the court of Babylon and then Persia. Chapters 7–12 are visions told by Daniel himself: "I, Daniel" (7:15 · BSB). Even the language changes. The Berean's note at 2:4 says that "the original text from this point of Daniel 2:4 through Daniel 7:28 is in Aramaic"; the rest is Hebrew.

Revelation names itself three ways in its first four verses. It is an unveiling, "which God gave Him to show His servants what must soon come to pass" (1:1 · BSB). It is a prophecy: "Blessed is the one who reads aloud the words of this prophecy" (1:3 · BSB). And it is a letter, opening like Paul's: "John, To the seven churches in the province of Asia: Grace and peace to you" (1:4 · BSB). John is told, "Write on a scroll what you see and send it to the seven churches" (1:11 · BSB), seven real congregations in what is now western Turkey.

### What it means

Readers call this kind of writing *apocalyptic*, from the Greek word in Revelation 1:1 that means an uncovering. Its marks are visions in which heaven is opened, an angel who explains, creatures and numbers that stand for something, and history seen from God's side until His kingdom comes. Because Revelation is also a letter, it was meant to be understood when it was read aloud in church; a blessing rests on those who "hear and obey what is written in it" (1:3 · BSB).

### Where the reading stops

*Apocalyptic* is a reader's label, not a word either book uses for itself, and the two books are not alike in everything. Daniel is set in the late seventh and the sixth centuries before Christ, from the third year of Jehoiakim (1:1) "until the first year of King Cyrus" (1:21 · BSB). Since Porphyry, a pagan critic of the third century after Christ, some have argued that the book was written much later, in the time of the Greek king Antiochus IV in the second century BC, and many modern critical scholars agree. This site reads Daniel as it presents itself, with Jesus, who spoke of "the prophet Daniel" (Matthew 24:15 · BSB), and with the Baptist Faith and Message, which holds Scripture to be "truth, without any mixture of error".

**Consider:** Revelation 1:3 blesses the one who reads aloud and those who hear. What does that tell you about where and how the book was first read?

**Reflect:** Have you avoided these books because they seemed too strange? What would it take to read one chapter aloud this week?

## 02 Pictures that explain themselves

**Main readings:** Daniel 7:15–17; 8:20–21; Revelation 1:12–20; 5:5–8; 7:4–9; 12:9.

Daniel saw four beasts and was frightened, so he did the wise thing: he asked. "I approached one of those who were standing there, and I asked him the true meaning of all this" (Daniel 7:16 · BSB).

### What the text shows

Again and again these books interpret their own pictures:

- "These four great beasts are four kings who will arise from the earth" (Daniel 7:17 · BSB).
- "The two-horned ram that you saw represents the kings of Media and Persia. The shaggy goat represents the king of Greece" (8:20–21 · BSB).
- "The seven stars are the angels of the seven churches, and the seven lampstands are the seven churches" (Revelation 1:20 · BSB).
- The great dragon is "that ancient serpent called the devil and Satan" (12:9 · BSB).
- The golden bowls of incense "are the prayers of the saints" (5:8 · BSB).

Watch, too, for what John hears and then sees. An elder tells him, "Behold, the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David, has triumphed". John turns, and "I saw a Lamb who appeared to have been slain" (5:5–6 · BSB). The Lion is the Lamb: the conqueror won by dying. (Study 12 reads this scene.) In chapter 7 John hears "the number of those who were sealed, 144,000" (7:4 · BSB), then looks and sees "a multitude too large to count" (7:9 · BSB). Some readers take the multitude as the same people seen a second way; others, among them Criswell (part 06), take the 144,000 as drawn from Israel and the multitude as a second vision, of the Gentiles. The text gives the order, heard and then seen; it does not say which.

### What it means

The pictures are true, but they are pictures. The Lamb has "seven horns and seven eyes, which represent the seven Spirits of God" (5:6 · BSB): this is not how Jesus looks, but what He is. B. B. Warfield, the Presbyterian theologian, put his principle this way: "The descriptions are descriptions not of the real occurrences themselves, but of symbols of the real occurrences"; so "we are to avoid pressing details" (*The Millennium and the Apocalypse*, 1904). Readers of the futurist kind, such as the Baptist pastor W. A. Criswell, give more weight to the plain sense of each detail and distrust what Criswell called "spiritualizing". Both would grant that where the book explains a picture, its explanation comes first.

### Where the reading stops

Not every picture is explained. The angel says the four beasts are four kings, but not what the leopard's four wings mean (Daniel 7:6). Once John is told, "Seal up what the seven thunders have said, and do not write it down" (Revelation 10:4 · BSB). Some things are left unsaid on purpose.

**Consider:** In Revelation 5:5–6, what does John hear, and what does he see? What does the second tell you about the first?

**Reflect:** When a passage puzzles you, do you look first for its own explanation, or for someone else's?

## 03 Old pictures in a new vision

**Main readings:** Revelation 1:7, 13–14; 4:6–7; 10:9–10; 11:4; 13:1–2; 15:3; 16:3; Daniel 7:3–9, 13; Ezekiel 1:10; 3:1–3; Zechariah 4:2–3, 14; Exodus 7:20.

Revelation never once says "as it is written". Yet almost every line sounds like one you have read before.

### What the text shows

- **Daniel's beasts, merged.** John sees "a beast with ten horns and seven heads rising out of the sea", "like a leopard, with the feet of a bear and the mouth of a lion" (Revelation 13:1–2 · BSB). Daniel's lion, bear and leopard, and his fourth beast with ten horns, have become one. The Berean's headings pair Revelation 13 with Daniel 7.
- **Daniel's throne-room.** The risen Jesus has hair "white like wool, as white as snow" (Revelation 1:14 · BSB), the Ancient of Days' description in Daniel 7:9, given now to "One like the Son of Man" (1:13 · BSB).
- **Ezekiel's creatures and scroll.** Around the throne the living creatures are "like a lion", "like a calf", with "a face like a man", and "like an eagle in flight" (Revelation 4:7 · BSB), the four faces of Ezekiel 1:10. John eats a scroll, "as sweet as honey in my mouth" (10:10 · BSB), as Ezekiel did (Ezekiel 3:3).
- **Zechariah's lampstand.** The two witnesses "are the two olive trees and the two lampstands" (Revelation 11:4 · BSB), from Zechariah 4.
- **The plagues of Egypt.** Water becomes blood (Exodus 7:20; Revelation 8:8; 16:3), with hail, sores and darkness. The victors sing "the song of God's servant Moses and of the Lamb" (15:3 · BSB).
- **Two prophets in one verse.** "Behold, He is coming with the clouds, and every eye will see Him—even those who pierced Him" (1:7 · BSB) joins Daniel 7:13 with Zechariah 12:10.

### What it means

The Old Testament is the dictionary for Revelation's pictures. A reader who knows Exodus hears the trumpets and bowls as God doing again what He did to Pharaoh: judging a cruel power and bringing His people out. A reader who knows Daniel sees in the beast every empire Daniel saw, rolled into one.

### Where the reading stops

An echo is not a quotation, and readers count the echoes differently. Test each one: is the wording close, and does the older passage fit the new one? If not, do not force it.

**Consider:** Read Daniel 7:3–7, then Revelation 13:1–2. What is the same? What is different, and why might John see them as one?

**Reflect:** How well do you know the Old Testament books Revelation draws on? Which one could you read next?

## 04 Numbers that speak

**Main readings:** Revelation 7:4–9; 11:2–3; 12:6, 14; 13:5, 18; 21:12–17; Daniel 7:25; 9:24–27; 12:7; Mark 13:32; Acts 1:7.

Seven churches, seven seals, seven trumpets, seven bowls. Revelation counts the way a musician counts beats.

### What the text shows

**Seven** runs through the book: the churches (1:11), the seals (5:1), the trumpets (8:6), the bowls (16:1), "seven horns and seven eyes" (5:6 · BSB). **Twelve** marks God's people: 12,000 from each tribe (7:5–8); a city with "twelve gates inscribed with the names of the twelve tribes of Israel" and "twelve foundations bearing the names of the twelve apostles of the Lamb" (21:12, 14 · BSB), measuring "12,000 stadia" with a wall of "144 cubits" (21:16–17 · BSB).

**Three and a half** appears in three forms. Daniel speaks of "a time, and times, and half a time" (Daniel 7:25 · BSB; 12:7). Revelation speaks of "42 months" (11:2 · BSB; 13:5) and "1,260 days" (11:3 · BSB; 12:6), which is 42 months of 30 days. In Revelation 12 the woman is kept safe in the wilderness "for 1,260 days" (12:6 · BSB) and "for a time, and times, and half a time" (12:14 · BSB): one flight, two ways of counting it.

### How readers take the numbers

How literally to count is one of the places readers differ most, and this study does not choose. Take Daniel's "Seventy weeks" (9:24 · BSB), which the Berean's note says may be read "Seventy sevens". John Calvin read them as weeks of years that began with the return under Persia and "did not terminate till the baptism of Christ" (*Commentaries on Daniel*, on 9:24–25), with the Messiah "cut off" (9:26 · BSB) in the last week: the prophecy fulfilled at Christ's first coming. Criswell, preaching in 1967, counted sixty-nine weeks from the Persian king Artaxerxes' decree to the cross, and held that the seventieth week is still to come, and is the time Revelation describes from chapter 4 on.

Then there is the most famous number in the Bible: "Here is a call for wisdom: Let the one who has insight calculate the number of the beast, for it is the number of a man, and that number is 666" (Revelation 13:18 · BSB). The Berean notes that "some manuscripts" read 616. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, knew that variant about AD 180 and held that 666 stood in "all the most approved and ancient copies". He listed three names whose Greek letters add to 666 (Euanthas, Lateinos and Teitan), leaned toward Teitan, and still would not pronounce on it: "It is therefore more certain, and less hazardous, to await the fulfilment of the prophecy, than to be making surmises" (*Against Heresies*, book 5, chapter 30).

### Where the reading stops

No number in these books is given to fix the date of Christ's return. Jesus said, "No one knows about that day or hour, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father" (Mark 13:32 · BSB), and "It is not for you to know times or seasons that the Father has fixed by His own authority" (Acts 1:7 · BSB). A number may be literal or a symbol; it is never a code for a calendar.

**Consider:** Revelation 12:6 and 12:14 describe the same flight. Why might the book give the same span in two forms?

**Reflect:** Have you ever been anxious or excited about a date someone set for the end? What does Acts 1:7 say to that?

## 05 Beasts and thrones

**Main readings:** Daniel 2:31–45; 4:25, 34–35; 7:2–14, 18; Revelation 4:1–11; 5:6–14; 13:1–10; 17:14.

In Daniel 7 the beasts come first, and then the thrones are set. In Revelation the throne comes first, and the beast does not rise from the sea until chapter 13.

### What the text shows

Nebuchadnezzar dreamed of a great statue of gold, silver, bronze and iron, on feet "part iron and part clay" (Daniel 2:33 · BSB). A stone "cut out, but not by human hands" struck it, and "became a great mountain and filled the whole earth" (2:34–35 · BSB): "the God of heaven will set up a kingdom that will never be destroyed" (2:44 · BSB). The proud king himself had to learn "that the Most High rules over the kingdom of mankind and gives it to whom He wishes" (4:25 · BSB).

In Daniel 7 the beasts come up out of a churning sea, until:

> As I continued to watch, thrones were set in place, and the Ancient of Days took His seat.

Daniel 7:9 · BSB

The kingdom is given to One like the Son of Man (7:13–14), and "the saints of the Most High will receive the kingdom and possess it forever" (7:18 · BSB).

Revelation opens heaven before it shows a single beast: "I looked and saw a door standing open in heaven", and "I saw a throne standing in heaven, with someone seated on it" (Revelation 4:1–2 · BSB). In the centre of the throne stands the Lamb (5:6). Only then, nine chapters later, does the beast rise, and even then everything it has is given: the dragon "gave the beast his power and his throne" (13:2 · BSB); it "was given a mouth"; it was given "authority to act for 42 months" (13:5 · BSB); it "was permitted to wage war against the saints" (13:7 · BSB). The world asks, "Who is like the beast, and who can wage war against it?" (13:4 · BSB). Heaven answers: "the Lamb will triumph over them, because He is Lord of lords and King of kings" (17:14 · BSB).

### What it means

On earth, empires look like statues of gold. From heaven they look like beasts: hungry, violent, and on a short leash. The throne is never empty. That is the heart of both books, whatever you decide about the details.

### Which empires?

Daniel names the first: "You are that head of gold" (2:38 · BSB), Babylon. In a separate vision he names Media and Persia, and Greece (8:20–21). Calvin, like Jerome before him, read the four as Babylon, Persia, Greece and Rome, and said the dream was given "not for the king's sake so much as for the consolation and support of the remnant of the faithful" (*Commentaries on Daniel*, on chapter 2). Those who date Daniel to the second century BC end the four with the Greek kingdoms; Porphyry took the fourth to be the successors of Alexander the Great. Either way, the kingdom that outlasts them all is God's.

**Consider:** List what the beast "was given" in Revelation 13:2–7. Who is doing the giving, and what does that tell you?

**Reflect:** What power in your world looks like a statue of gold? How does it look from the open door of Revelation 4?

## 06 Four ways to read Revelation

**Main readings:** Revelation 1:1, 3, 19; 4:1; 13:1–8; 22:6–10.

The book says its events "must soon come to pass" (1:1 · BSB). Soon for whom: for John's churches, for every age, or for the last generation?

### What the text shows

Three verses set the frame. "Soon" (1:1), with the Berean's note "Or suddenly or quickly"; "the time is near" (1:3 · BSB), repeated at the end (22:10). John is told to "write down the things you have seen, and the things that are, and the things that will happen after this" (1:19 · BSB), and then the door opens: "Come up here, and I will show you what must happen after these things" (4:1 · BSB).

### The four readings

Criswell laid out these four in a 1961 sermon, "For the Time Is at Hand", before choosing his own. Many readers combine them.

- **Mostly past.** Most of chapters 4–19 was fulfilled in the first century. Albert Barnes, the Presbyterian commentator, summarises Moses Stuart of Andover this way: chapters 4–11 concern Jerusalem and Judea, chapters 12–19 "persecuting and heathen Rome". Those who hold it within the Baptist Faith and Message still look for Christ's personal return, as Revelation 22:20 promises. Many date the book before AD 70; the main early witness against that is Irenaeus, who says the vision was seen "towards the end of Domitian's reign", in the 90s AD.
- **The church's history.** The visions trace history from the apostles to the end. Many Reformers and older Baptists read this way: the Second London Baptist Confession (1677/1689) names the pope as "that antichrist, that man of sin", reading 2 Thessalonians 2 in this tradition. (The Baptist Faith and Message 2000 names no such figure.) Criswell, citing the scholar Joseph Angus, objected that its interpreters disagree widely over which events the visions mean.
- **Mostly future.** From chapter 4 on, the book describes the last years before Christ returns. This was Criswell's view: he read the letters of chapters 2–3 as the course of the church, and the open door of 4:1 as the turning point. He gave two reasons: the book calls itself prophecy, and what it describes, the dead raised and a new heaven and earth, has not yet happened.
- **The whole age, told again and again.** The visions show, in symbols, the battle between Christ and evil through the whole time between His comings, told several times over. Warfield saw seven parallel sections, each "repeating with progressive clearness, fullness and richness the whole history" of that time, written "not for knowledge's own sake" but "to make known unto action". Criswell described this school as reading the book as an allegory tied to no event, and liked it least; Warfield, by contrast, reads each section as the real history between Christ's comings.

### One picture, four ways

Take the beast from the sea (13:1–8). Read as past, it is the persecuting Roman Empire. Read as church history, it has often been taken as the papacy. Read as future, it is a world ruler still to come. Read as the whole age, it is every power that demands the worship that belongs to God. Notice what all four share: the beast is real, it is opposed to Christ, it is permitted and limited, and it falls.

### Where Baptists stand

The Baptist Faith and Message names none of these ways and no timetable. It affirms that Christ will return "personally and visibly". Study 30 shows how Baptists differ over the thousand years; this study, in the same spirit, shows the four ways and does not choose. What every reader within the confession holds is plain: Jesus is coming, and the new creation of chapters 21–22 is still ahead.

**Consider:** Read Revelation 1:19 and 4:1 together. How does each of the four readings use them?

**Reflect:** Which way of reading were you taught, if any? Can you state another fairly, as its holders would?

## 07 Written to comfort

**Main readings:** Daniel 3:16–18; 6:10; 12:13; Revelation 2:10; 6:9–11; 12:11; 13:10; 19:10; 22:9.

The first readers of these books did not need a chart. They needed courage for the next morning.

### What the text shows

Daniel's friends answered the king before the furnace: "If the God whom we serve exists, then He is able to deliver us… But even if He does not, let it be known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods" (Daniel 3:17–18 · BSB). Daniel heard the law against prayer and "got down on his knees, prayed, and gave thanks to his God, just as he had done before" (6:10 · BSB).

To the church in Smyrna the risen Christ wrote:

> Do not fear what you are about to suffer. Look, the devil is about to throw some of you into prison to test you, and you will suffer tribulation for ten days. Be faithful even unto death, and I will give you the crown of life.

Revelation 2:10 · BSB

Every one of the seven letters holds a promise for the one who overcomes (2:7, 11, 17, 26; 3:5, 12, 21). The martyrs under the altar cry, "How long, O Lord, holy and true" (6:10 · BSB), and are told "to rest a little while longer" (6:11 · BSB). Heaven sings of the faithful: "They have conquered him by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of their testimony" (12:11 · BSB). In the middle of the beasts the book stops to say, "Here is a call for the perseverance and faith of the saints" (13:10 · BSB).

### What it means

The comfort is not that God's people will escape suffering. In these books they are thrown in furnaces and lions' dens, imprisoned and killed. The comfort is that the throne is occupied, the Lamb has already conquered, the beast's time is measured, and the end is sure. Twice John fell down before the angel who showed him these things, and twice he was told, "Worship God!" (19:10; 22:9 · BSB). The visions do not end in fascination with beasts. They end in worship of God.

### Where the reading stops

These books do not promise that faithfulness will be rescued in this life: "even if He does not." Their promise reaches past death. Daniel's last word from the angel was this:

> But as for you, go on your way until the end. You will rest, and will arise to your inheritance at the end of the days.

Daniel 12:13 · BSB

**Consider:** Revelation 13:10 sits in the middle of the beast's chapter. Why put a call to endurance there?

**Reflect:** Where do you need the courage of Daniel 3:18, "even if He does not"?

## Read Daniel and Revelation again

Daniel was told to seal his book; John was told to leave his open, "because the time is near" (Revelation 22:10 · BSB). The visions are no longer shut. Read them the way this study has: let the vision explain itself, look for the older passage behind the picture, and ask what it was for. Hold the four ways of reading with an open hand, and the throne with a closed one. The book that began by blessing whoever reads it aloud ends with a prayer: "Amen. Come, Lord Jesus!" (22:20 · BSB).
