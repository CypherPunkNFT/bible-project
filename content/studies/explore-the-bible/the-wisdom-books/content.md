# The wisdom books

Concepts: Fear of the LORD · Proverbs, not promises · Under the sun · Love as strong as death

Motto: Silver from the mine. / Wisdom from God.

Lead: Four books in the middle of the Old Testament teach a way of living more than they tell a story: Job, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and the Song of Songs. Christians call them the wisdom books. Wisdom here does not mean being clever. It means the skill of living well in God's world, before God. This study walks all four and teaches one habit: before you ask what a line promises, ask who is speaking and what kind of saying it is.

> And He said to man, ‘Behold, the fear of the Lord, that is wisdom, and to turn away from evil is understanding.’

Job 28:28 · BSB

Habit: These four books do not all speak the same way. A reader who treats every line as a promise will get hurt, or hurt someone else. In Job, three friends talk for chapters about why people suffer, and at the end God tells them, "you have not spoken about Me accurately" (Job 42:7). Their words are in the Bible, and the Bible reports them as wrong. In Proverbs, two sayings stand side by side and tell you to do opposite things (Proverbs 26:4–5). Ecclesiastes keeps saying that what it describes happens "under the sun" (Ecclesiastes 1:3). The Song of Songs is a love poem. A report of a debate, a proverb, a searching question and a song each ask to be read in their own way. So ask first: who is speaking here, and what kind of saying is this?

Setting: In the Hebrew Bible these books stand in its third part, the Writings, after the Law and the Prophets. Jewish communities read two of them aloud each year among five short scrolls: the Song of Songs at Passover, the spring festival remembering the rescue from Egypt, and Ecclesiastes at Sukkot, the autumn harvest festival when families live for a week in temporary shelters. Christians have read all four as God's word from the beginning, and the New Testament quotes them.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. The study walks Job 28 and Job 38–42; Proverbs 1–9 and a handful of sayings from chapters 10, 16, 22 and 26; Ecclesiastes 1–3 and 12; and the Song of Songs as a whole. It does not walk the long debate in Job 3–27 and 29–37, most of Proverbs 11–31, or Ecclesiastes 4–11. The Psalms are often counted among the wisdom writings too; they have their own study. Catholic and Orthodox Bibles add two further wisdom books, the Wisdom of Solomon and Sirach, which Baptists, with other Protestants, do not count as Scripture. This study reads Scripture; it is not medical, financial or professional guidance.

## 01 Where can wisdom be found?

**Main readings:** Job 28

Hook: People can sink a shaft into the dark and bring out silver. Wisdom is not down there.

Setting: Job is a man the book calls blameless. In two chapters he loses his herds, his servants, his ten children and his health (Job 1–2). Three friends come to comfort him, and the comfort turns into a long argument about why he is suffering (Job 3–27). Then comes this quiet poem. The Berean prints it inside Job's speech; some readers take it as the book itself pausing for breath. Either way, it asks the question the whole book is asking.

Limit: The poem does not explain Job's suffering. It says where the answer is kept, and who keeps it.

### The miner

The poem opens underground: "Surely there is a mine for silver and a place where gold is refined" (Job 28:1). It admires what human beings can do. "Man puts an end to the darkness; he probes the farthest recesses for ore in deepest darkness" (Job 28:3). He goes where no animal goes: "No bird of prey knows that path; no falcon’s eye has seen it" (Job 28:7). He dams streams and splits mountains to bring what is hidden to light (Job 28:9–11).

Then the turn:

> But where can wisdom be found, and where does understanding dwell?

Job 28:12 · BSB

### Not for sale, not underground

The miner's skill finds silver, but "No man can know its value, nor is it found in the land of the living" (Job 28:13). The sea says it is not there (Job 28:14). "It cannot be bought with gold" (Job 28:15), and the poem piles up gold of Ophir, onyx, sapphire, coral and topaz only to set them all aside (Job 28:16–19). Even Abaddon and Death, names for the place of the dead, have only "heard a rumor about it" (Job 28:22).

### God knows the way

"But God understands its way, and He knows its place" (Job 28:23). The One who weighed the wind and gave the rain its limit looked at wisdom and established it (Job 28:25–27). And then He speaks to human beings, in the verse that opens this study. Wisdom, for us, is not a secret dug up. It is "the fear of the Lord", and turning away from evil (Job 28:28).

[read] The fear of the LORD runs through all of these books, so it needs explaining now. It is not terror that drives a person away from God. It is the awe that draws a person near, takes God seriously and wants what He wants. In the Berean, "LORD" in capitals stands for God's personal name, revealed to Moses; "Lord" in ordinary type, as in Job 28:28, translates a word meaning master. Both name the same God.

[read] The poem gives Job no reason for his pain. What it gives is a direction. Understanding why may stay out of reach; reverence and goodness do not.

**Consider:** What can the miner find, and what can he not? Where does the poem say wisdom's place is known?

**Reflect:** What are you digging for, hoping it will make your life make sense? What would it look like to fear the Lord and turn from evil while the question stays open?

## 02 Out of the whirlwind: God answers Job

**Main readings:** Job 38–42

Hook: Job asked for a hearing for thirty chapters. God comes, and asks the questions.

Setting: The friends have said that suffering must be punishment for hidden sin. Job has insisted he is innocent and demanded that God answer him. A younger man, Elihu, has spoken too (Job 32–37). Now God speaks.

Limit: God never tells Job why he suffered. The happy ending is what God did for Job; it is not a promise that every loss is repaid in this life.

### What Job never hears

Before the debate begins, the reader is let into a council in heaven, where the Accuser asks whether Job serves God only for what he gets (Job 1:6–12; 2:1–6). Job is never told about this, not even in God's speeches. The reader knows why the trial began; Job does not; and God does not tell him.

At first Job meets his losses with worship:

> Naked I came from my mother’s womb, and naked I will return. The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away. Blessed be the name of the LORD.

Job 1:21 · BSB

When his wife tells him to curse God, he answers, "Should we accept from God only good and not adversity?" (Job 2:10). Then, after seven days of silence, "Job opened his mouth and cursed the day of his birth" (Job 3:1): "May the day of my birth perish" (Job 3:3). The man who blessed God's name and the man who cursed his own birthday are the same man, and the book keeps both. Neither cancels the other.

Read more in *Fear, anxiety and despair*.

Who the Accuser is, and what the heavenly council means, is taken up in its own study. Read more in *Angels, demons and spiritual powers*.

### The questions

"Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind" (Job 38:1). His first words are a charge: "Who is this who obscures My counsel by words without knowledge?" (Job 38:2). Then a flood of questions:

> Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? Tell Me, if you have understanding.

Job 38:4 · BSB

God walks Job through creation as if through His own house: the earth's foundations, laid "while the morning stars sang together and all the sons of God shouted for joy" (Job 38:7); the sea, told "You may come this far, but no farther; here your proud waves must stop" (Job 38:11); the dawn, the gates of death, the storehouses of snow (Job 38:12–23). Then the animals, one after another: the lioness, the raven, the mountain goat, the wild donkey that "scorns the tumult of the city", the wild ox, the ostrich, the war horse, the eagle (Job 38:39–39:30).

[read] Look at what these questions notice. God sends rain "on a desert where no man lives" (Job 38:26). He feeds the raven's young "when its young cry out to God" (Job 38:41). He even jokes about the ostrich, who leaves her eggs in the sand: "For God has deprived her of wisdom; He has not endowed her with understanding" (Job 39:17). The world is larger than human needs, and God cares for parts of it no person will ever see. This is a poem, not a science lesson, and its point is that God's care and rule reach far beyond what Job can survey.

### Job's first answer, and the second speech

Job answers briefly: "Behold, I am insignificant. How can I reply to You? I place my hand over my mouth" (Job 40:4).

God speaks again, and names the real problem: "Would you really annul My justice? Would you condemn Me to justify yourself?" (Job 40:8). In defending his innocence, Job had come close to saying God was unjust. God then shows him Behemoth and Leviathan, two vast creatures no hunter can take (Job 40:15–41:34). If no one can stand against Leviathan, "Then who is able to stand against Me? Who has given to Me that I should repay him? Everything under heaven is Mine" (Job 41:10–11). Paul quotes that last question when he praises God's wisdom, which no one can repay or advise (Romans 11:35).

[open] What are Behemoth and Leviathan? Many readers take them as the hippopotamus and the crocodile, described in a poet's terms. Others hear pictures of the great powers of chaos that only God masters, since Leviathan appears elsewhere as a sea monster God defeats (Psalm 74:14; Isaiah 27:1). The confession this site writes under does not speak to it, and this page does not choose. Either way, the point holds: if Job cannot master a creature God made, he is in no place to judge its Maker.

### "Now my eyes have seen You"

Job replies: "I know that You can do all things and that no plan of Yours can be thwarted" (Job 42:2). "Surely I spoke of things I did not understand, things too wonderful for me to know" (Job 42:3). Then the heart of the book:

> My ears had heard of You, but now my eyes have seen You.

Job 42:5 · BSB

"Therefore I retract my words, and I repent in dust and ashes" (Job 42:6). To repent means to turn: to change one's mind and direction before God. Job does not confess the hidden sins his friends accused him of. He takes back his rash words about God's justice.

[care] Translations of Job 42:6 differ, because the Hebrew is hard [check: the main alternative renderings, to name them on the page]. What the verse does not do is agree with the friends that Job's suffering was punishment.

### What God said to the friends

Then God turns to Eliphaz: "My wrath is kindled against you and your two friends. For you have not spoken about Me accurately, as My servant Job has" (Job 42:7). He says it twice (Job 42:8). The friends must bring a sacrifice, and Job is to pray for them.

[read] How can Job be rebuked in chapter 38 and praised in chapter 42? A reading this site holds: Job spoke to God, honestly, even in complaint and even in error, and God corrected him face to face. The friends spoke about God, and turned Him into a formula: suffering means sin. That formula was false, and God said so. This is a reading, not a sentence the text states, but it fits both verses.

### The ending, and what it does not promise

"After Job had prayed for his friends, the LORD restored his prosperity and doubled his former possessions" (Job 42:10). His family come and comfort him "over all the adversity that the LORD had brought upon him" (Job 42:11). The book does not pretend God had nothing to do with it. Job's animals are doubled, yet "he also had seven sons and three daughters" (Job 42:13), the same number as before. His daughters are named, and "their father granted them an inheritance among their brothers" (Job 42:15), which was not the usual custom of the time.

[care] This ending is what God did for Job. It is not a promise that every loss will be repaid in this life, and it does not make the children who died replaceable; that sentence is this site's own word, not the text's. Scripture remembers Job for something else: James points to his endurance and to the Lord's compassion and mercy (James 5:11), and Ezekiel names him with Noah and Daniel as a righteous man (Ezekiel 14:14).

**Consider:** What does God say in His speeches, and what does He never say? Why are the friends rebuked?

**Reflect:** Are you more like Job, bringing your complaint to God's face, or like the friends, explaining someone else's pain from a distance? What would it mean to know God when you still do not know why?

## 03 The fear of the LORD: the awe where wisdom begins

**Main readings:** Proverbs 1–9

Hook: A father talks to his son, and two women call from the street.

Setting: Proverbs is a collection: "the proverbs of Solomon son of David, king of Israel" (Proverbs 1:1), with later collections gathered by Hezekiah's men and the words of Agur and of King Lemuel (Proverbs 25:1; 30:1; 31:1). Chapters 1–9 are not short sayings but longer talks, mostly from a father to a son, that prepare the reader for the sayings to come.

Limit: These chapters promise that wisdom leads to life. They do not promise that a wise person will never suffer, and they do not offer health or wealth as a reward for faith.

### A book with a purpose, and a door

The book says what it is for: "for gaining wisdom and discipline, for comprehending words of insight" (Proverbs 1:2), for the simple, the young and the wise alike (Proverbs 1:4–5). Then it names its foundation:

> The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, but fools despise wisdom and discipline.

Proverbs 1:7 · BSB

Notice where these chapters end: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom, and knowledge of the Holy One is understanding" (Proverbs 9:10). The same line stands at the front door and the back door. Job 28 said the same thing. Everything between is built on it.

### A father and a mother

"Listen, my son, to your father’s instruction, and do not forsake the teaching of your mother" (Proverbs 1:8). Wisdom is first learned at home. The father pleads more than he commands. He tells the son to seek wisdom "like silver" and "like hidden treasure" (Proverbs 2:4), the same picture as Job's miner, but now with a promise: "For the LORD gives wisdom; from His mouth come knowledge and understanding" (Proverbs 2:6). Job 28 says wisdom cannot be dug out; Proverbs 2 says why the search still succeeds. God gives it.

Then come words many readers know by heart:

> Trust in the LORD with all your heart, and lean not on your own understanding; in all your ways acknowledge Him, and He will make your paths straight.

Proverbs 3:5–6 · BSB

"Be not wise in your own eyes; fear the LORD and turn away from evil" (Proverbs 3:7). Even hardship is reframed: "the LORD disciplines the one He loves, as does a father the son in whom he delights" (Proverbs 3:12). Discipline here means training and correction, not cruelty. The letter to the Hebrews quotes these verses to encourage struggling believers (Hebrews 12:5–6). James and Peter both quote another line, "He mocks the mockers, but gives grace to the humble" (Proverbs 3:34; James 4:6; 1 Peter 5:5). Grace means God's kindness given to people who have not earned it.

Then the centre of the father's teaching: "Guard your heart with all diligence, for from it flow springs of life" (Proverbs 4:23). In the Bible the heart is the place where a person thinks, chooses and loves.

### Warnings with real names

The father warns against gangs who promise easy money (Proverbs 1:10–19), against laziness, "Walk in the manner of the ant, O slacker; observe its ways and become wise" (Proverbs 6:6), and against seven things God hates, from "haughty eyes" to "one who stirs up discord among brothers" (Proverbs 6:16–19).

Above all he warns against adultery. Four times he describes the "forbidden woman ... who abandons the partner of her youth and forgets the covenant of her God" (Proverbs 2:16–17). A covenant is a binding promise made before God; here it is marriage. Chapter 7 tells the story from a window, of a young man drifting to her corner at dusk (Proverbs 7:6–23). Because a father is speaking to a son, the warning names a woman; the faithfulness it asks of him is asked of everyone. Against the warning stands a blessing: "May your fountain be blessed, and may you rejoice in the wife of your youth" (Proverbs 5:18).

Read more in *Sexuality, singleness and divorce*.

### Wisdom calls in the street

The other voice in these chapters is Wisdom herself, pictured as a woman. "Wisdom calls out in the street, she lifts her voice in the square" (Proverbs 1:20). She is not hidden away for experts. She calls at the city gates to anyone passing (Proverbs 8:1–5). "To fear the LORD is to hate evil; I hate arrogant pride, evil conduct, and perverse speech" (Proverbs 8:13).

Then she speaks of creation: "The LORD created me as His first course, before His works of old" (Proverbs 8:22). She was there when He marked out the sea's boundary, and "Then I was a skilled craftsman at His side, and His delight day by day, rejoicing always in His presence" (Proverbs 8:30).

[read] Christians have long heard Christ in this poem. Paul names Christ as God's wisdom (1 Corinthians 1:24), and the New Testament speaks of the Son as the One through whom all things were made (John 1:3; Colossians 1:16). But Proverbs 8 does not name Him, and translations differ on the verb in verse 22. In the fourth century Arius used that verse to argue that the Son was a created being; Athanasius answered him in his *Orations against the Arians* [check: the discourse and sections on Proverbs 8:22], and the Nicene Creed confesses the Son "begotten, not made". The confession this site writes under holds that the Son is eternal (article II, God). So this site reads Proverbs 8 first as a poem about God's own wisdom, and hears in it a likeness the New Testament fills out in Christ, without treating each line as a statement about the Son.

Read more in *Christ in the Old Testament*.

### Two banquets

The section ends with two invitations. Wisdom has built her house and set her table: "Come, eat my bread and drink the wine I have mixed. Leave your folly behind, and you will live" (Proverbs 9:5–6). Folly calls out the same first words to the same passers-by, and offers something else: "Stolen water is sweet, and bread eaten in secret is tasty!" (Proverbs 9:17). The narrator adds what Folly does not say: "But they do not know that the dead are there, that her guests are in the depths of Sheol" (Proverbs 9:18). Sheol is the Hebrew name for the grave, the place of the dead.

[care] Some lines here sound like guarantees: wisdom brings "healing to your body" (Proverbs 3:8), and honouring God with your wealth fills your barns (Proverbs 3:9–10). They describe how God's world tends to work, and they are true as that. They are not medical advice, and they are not a deal in which giving buys riches. The confession asks believers to give "cheerfully, regularly, systematically, proportionately, and liberally" (article XIII, stewardship), and it does not promise wealth in return. Read more in *Money, possessions and generosity*.

**Consider:** Which line opens and closes Proverbs 1–9? What does Wisdom offer, and what does Folly hide?

**Reflect:** Whose voice are you most listening to this week? Which table are you being invited to, and what does the invitation leave out?

## 04 Proverbs, not promises

**Main readings:** Proverbs 10; Proverbs 16; Proverbs 22:6; Proverbs 26:4–5

Hook: Two sayings, side by side, tell you to do opposite things. Both are right.

Setting: From chapter 10 the book changes pace: "The proverbs of Solomon" again (Proverbs 10:1), now in short two-line sayings, mostly in no story order. The second line often turns against the first: "A wise son brings joy to his father, but a foolish son grief to his mother" (Proverbs 10:1).

Limit: A proverb tells how life usually goes in God's world. It is not a contract that God must keep in every single case.

### How a proverb works

Many sayings in chapter 10 describe what you could see for yourself. "Idle hands make one poor, but diligent hands bring wealth" (Proverbs 10:4). "When words are many, sin is unavoidable, but he who restrains his lips is wise" (Proverbs 10:19). Some simply report: "The wealth of the rich man is his fortified city, but poverty is the ruin of the poor" (Proverbs 10:15). That line does not approve of what it describes. It tells you how the world feels to each.

One line from this chapter is echoed in the New Testament: "Hatred stirs up dissension, but love covers all transgressions" (Proverbs 10:12; compare 1 Peter 4:8).

### Two sayings that need judgement

> Do not answer a fool according to his folly, or you yourself will be like him. Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he become wise in his own eyes.

Proverbs 26:4–5 · BSB

Put next to each other on purpose, these lines show what a proverb is. Neither is a rule for every conversation. Sometimes answering a foolish argument drags you down to it; sometimes leaving it unanswered lets it stand. Wisdom is knowing which moment you are in.

[source:talmud-shabbat] The rabbis noticed this pair too. The Babylonian Talmud records a discussion of the two verses, resolved by taking them as fitting different kinds of situation [check: Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 30b, and the exact resolution given].

### The LORD and our plans

Chapter 16 gathers sayings about God's rule over human choices. "Commit your works to the LORD and your plans will be achieved" (Proverbs 16:3) stands a few lines from this:

> A man’s heart plans his course, but the LORD determines his steps.

Proverbs 16:9 · BSB

And the chapter ends: "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (Proverbs 16:33). Christians call this providence: God's ongoing care and rule over everything that happens, even what looks like chance. It does not make planning pointless; the plans are still yours. It means they rest in larger hands. The same chapter warns, "There is a way that seems right to a man, but its end is the way of death" (Proverbs 16:25), and "Pride goes before destruction, and a haughty spirit before a fall" (Proverbs 16:18).

### The hard ones

Some proverbs, read as promises, would break people. "The LORD does not let the righteous go hungry" (Proverbs 10:3). "The fear of the LORD prolongs life, but the years of the wicked will be cut short" (Proverbs 10:27). Yet the same Bible gives us Job, and Ecclesiastes says plainly that the righteous sometimes perish while the wicked live long (Ecclesiastes 7:15). The same chapter of Proverbs already knows that "Better a little with righteousness than great gain with injustice" (Proverbs 16:8), a saying that only makes sense if the righteous sometimes have little.

[care] If you are going hungry, or you have buried someone young who loved God, these sayings do not say you, or they, failed. They describe the grain of God's world. Job and Ecclesiastes stand beside Proverbs in Scripture so that no one reads Proverbs alone. Read more in *Grief and dying*.

### A question readers ask: is Proverbs 22:6 a promise?

> Train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not depart from it.

Proverbs 22:6 · BSB

[read] This site reads it as a proverb: a true word about how children are usually shaped, given to encourage parents to teach patiently and early. It is not a guarantee that every child raised in faith will follow Christ, and the confession this site writes under does not treat it as one. Each person must trust Christ for themselves; "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord" (article IV, salvation). The Hebrew of the verse is brief, and readers have understood "the way he should go" in more than one way.

[care] If your grown child has walked away from faith, this verse is not a verdict on your parenting. Keep praying, keep loving, and keep the door open. Read more in *Children and the home*.

**Consider:** Why do Proverbs 26:4 and 26:5 stand side by side? How does Proverbs 16:9 fit with 16:3?

**Reflect:** Is there a proverb you have been holding as a promise God broke? What changes if you read it as a proverb?

## 05 Under the sun: what wisdom cannot fix

**Main readings:** Ecclesiastes 1–3; Ecclesiastes 12

Hook: A king tries everything, and writes down what he finds.

Setting: The speaker calls himself "the Teacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem" (Ecclesiastes 1:1). The Hebrew title, Qoheleth, means one who gathers people to teach them. Jewish and Christian tradition long took him to be Solomon. The book never gives his name, and many scholars think a later writer spoke in Solomon's voice. This page does not choose; the book is God's word on either view.

Limit: The Teacher describes what can be seen and grasped "under the sun". He does not say that God is absent, or that nothing matters.

### Breath

The book opens with its refrain: "Futility of futilities! Everything is futile!" (Ecclesiastes 1:2). The Hebrew word, hevel, means a breath or a vapour: something real, but brief, and impossible to hold. Then the question: "What does a man gain from all his labor, at which he toils under the sun?" (Ecclesiastes 1:3). The sun rises and sets, the wind circles, rivers run to a sea that is never full, and "there is nothing new under the sun" (Ecclesiastes 1:5–9). Even wisdom hurts: "For with much wisdom comes much sorrow, and as knowledge grows, grief increases" (Ecclesiastes 1:18).

### The experiment

In chapter 2 the king tests every road. "I will test you with pleasure; enjoy what is good!" (Ecclesiastes 2:1). He builds houses, plants vineyards, gathers silver, gold, servants and singers (Ecclesiastes 2:4–8). "Yet when I considered all the works that my hands had accomplished ... I found everything to be futile, a pursuit of the wind; there was nothing to be gained under the sun" (Ecclesiastes 2:11).

He grants that "wisdom exceeds folly, just as light exceeds darkness" (Ecclesiastes 2:13). But death comes to both: "Alas, the wise man will die just like the fool!" (Ecclesiastes 2:16). And he admits, "So I hated life, because the work that is done under the sun was grievous to me" (Ecclesiastes 2:17).

[care] The Bible lets a wise man say he hated life, and it does not scold him for saying it. If you feel that way, you are not the first, and the feeling is not the end of the story. Please look again at the help at the top of this page. Jesus' story of the rich man who built bigger barns, then died that night, echoes the Teacher's worry about leaving everything to someone else (Luke 12:16–21; Ecclesiastes 2:18–21).

### Light in the middle

Then, in the middle of the gloom: "Nothing is better for a man than to eat and drink and enjoy his work. I have also seen that this is from the hand of God. For apart from Him, who can eat and who can find enjoyment?" (Ecclesiastes 2:24–25). Ordinary meals and honest work, received as gifts from God, are not futile. The Teacher comes back to this again and again (Ecclesiastes 3:12–13, 22).

### A time for everything

"To everything there is a season, and a time for every purpose under heaven" (Ecclesiastes 3:1). Fourteen pairs follow, from birth and death to war and peace, including "a time to weep and a time to laugh, a time to mourn and a time to dance" (Ecclesiastes 3:4). These are observations, not commands; the Teacher is not telling anyone when to kill or hate. He is saying that human life moves through seasons we did not choose.

> He has made everything beautiful in its time. He has also set eternity in the hearts of men, yet they cannot fathom the work that God has done from beginning to end.

Ecclesiastes 3:11 · BSB

We are made for more than the moment, and we cannot see the whole. "God does it so that they should fear Him" (Ecclesiastes 3:14). The Teacher also sees injustice, "in the place of judgment there is wickedness", and answers it: "God will judge the righteous and the wicked" (Ecclesiastes 3:16–17).

He even asks, "Who knows if the spirit of man rises upward and the spirit of the animal descends into the earth?" (Ecclesiastes 3:21). Under the sun, no one can see the answer. The book itself answers later: "the spirit returns to God who gave it" (Ecclesiastes 12:7). The confession this site writes under holds plainly that the dead will be raised and judged (article X, last things).

### Remember your Creator

Chapter 12 opens: "Remember your Creator in the days of your youth, before the days of adversity come" (Ecclesiastes 12:1). What follows is a long picture, which a long tradition, Jewish and Christian, reads as images of an ageing body: trembling keepers of the house, grinders that cease because they are few, windows that see dimly (Ecclesiastes 12:3). The silver cord snaps and the pitcher shatters at the spring, "before the dust returns to the ground from which it came and the spirit returns to God who gave it" (Ecclesiastes 12:6–7).

Then another voice speaks about the Teacher: "he pondered, searched out, and arranged many proverbs" (Ecclesiastes 12:9), and "The words of the wise are like goads" (Ecclesiastes 12:11), sharp sticks for moving cattle. Then the end:

> When all has been heard, the conclusion of the matter is this: Fear God and keep His commandments, because this is the whole duty of man. For God will bring every deed into judgment, along with every hidden thing, whether good or evil.

Ecclesiastes 12:13–14 · BSB

[open] How should the whole book be read? Some readers take the Teacher's search "under the sun" as a deliberately limited view, which the closing voice in chapter 12 sets in its true light. Others take the Teacher himself as a faithful, realistic voice throughout, whose call to enjoy God's gifts is the book's own teaching. Both read the book as God's word, and both end at fearing God. ## 06 Love as strong as death: the Song of Songs

**Main readings:** Song of Songs 1–8

Hook: A whole book of the Bible is a love song, and the woman sings most of it.

Setting: The book calls itself "Solomon’s Song of Songs" (Song of Songs 1:1), a Hebrew way of saying the finest of songs. Three voices sing: a woman, the man she loves, and a group called the daughters of Jerusalem. God's name does not plainly appear.

Limit: The Song celebrates love; it does not give rules for every relationship. How far it also pictures God and His people is a question faithful Baptists answer in two ways.

### How Jewish readers have heard it

In Jewish tradition the Song is read aloud at Passover. The Targum, an early Aramaic paraphrase, reads it as the love story of God and Israel, from the exodus onward. The Mishnah, the collection of Jewish teaching written down around AD 200, records Rabbi Akiva calling the Song the holiest of all the Writings [check: Mishnah Yadayim 3:5, and the wording of the translation to be quoted, if any].

### What the Song says

The book opens with longing: "Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth! For your love is more delightful than wine" (Song of Songs 1:2). It moves through spring, "For now the winter is past; the rain is over and gone" (Song of Songs 2:11); through nights of searching, "I sought him, but did not find him" (Song of Songs 3:1; 5:6); to a wedding, "the day of his wedding— the day of his heart’s rejoicing" (Song of Songs 3:11); and to a garden kept for one: "My sister, my bride, you are a garden locked up, a spring enclosed, a fountain sealed" (Song of Songs 4:12).

Look for the Song's own refrains. Three times the woman warns her friends: "Do not arouse or awaken love until the time is right" (Song of Songs 2:7; 3:5; 8:4). Love is powerful, and it has its time. Three times she speaks of belonging, and the words shift: "My beloved is mine and I am his" (Song of Songs 2:16); "I belong to my beloved and he belongs to me" (Song of Songs 6:3); "I belong to my beloved, and his desire is for me" (Song of Songs 7:10). Some readers hear in that last line an answer to Genesis 3:16, where the same word for desire marks love's damage after the fall; that is a reading, not something the Song says.

Near the end comes the book's great statement:

> Set me as a seal over your heart, as a seal upon your arm. For love is as strong as death, its jealousy as unrelenting as Sheol. Its sparks are fiery flames, the fiercest blaze of all. Mighty waters cannot quench love; rivers cannot sweep it away. If a man were to give all the wealth of his house for love, his offer would be utterly scorned.

### Two readings, both Baptist

[read] The plain reading takes the Song as what it first appears to be: a poem of love between a man and a woman, moving toward and within marriage, given by God to honour the love of husband and wife, bodies included. It stands beside "rejoice in the wife of your youth" (Proverbs 5:18) and the first marriage in Genesis (Genesis 2:24–25). Among Southern Baptists, Daniel Akin has taught the Song this way [check: Daniel L. Akin, *God on Sex* (2003), title and year].

[read] The Christ-and-church reading hears in the Song, as well or instead, the love between Christ and His people. Christ means the anointed King, the Messiah; the church means all who belong to Him. This reading is old: Origen in the third century and Bernard of Clairvaux in the twelfth wrote on the Song this way. Among Baptists, John Gill expounded the whole book as Christ and His church [check: Gill's *An Exposition of the Book of Solomon's Song*, first edition and year], and Charles Spurgeon preached many sermons from it in the same way [check: the title of the collected volume of Spurgeon's Song of Songs sermons]. Its readers point to the New Testament, which calls marriage a picture of Christ and the church (Ephesians 5:31–32) and speaks of the church as the bride (Revelation 19:7).

## Reading the wisdom books

Way: Here is the habit to carry to the next page of these books. Ask who is speaking: Job, a friend, God, a father, Wisdom, Folly, the Teacher, the bride. Ask what kind of saying it is: a report, a proverb, a searching question, a song. Ask what it does not promise. Then ask where it points, and you will usually find the same place: the fear of the Lord, and turning from evil.

Close: Job learned that wisdom belongs to God, and met God without being told why. Proverbs showed what reverence looks like at home, at work and in the street. Ecclesiastes told the truth about death and limits, and still found gifts from God's hand. The Song sang that love is stronger than death. Jesus once said that someone greater than Solomon was standing in front of the crowd (Matthew 12:42). The wisdom these books seek is finally found in Him.
