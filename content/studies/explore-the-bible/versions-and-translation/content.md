# Versions and translation

Working content draft · Study 35 · Explore the Bible · Owner review pending

When two Bibles use different words, begin by finding out what you are comparing. A translation's wording, a revision of that translation, the books included in an edition and the explanations printed beside the text are related questions, but they are not the same question.

This study uses the King James Version (KJV), American Standard Version (ASV) and World English Bible (WEB) texts held in this library for a small set of comparisons. It does not rank every translation. The aim is to identify an edition, notice differences accurately, consult its explanations and return to reading a complete passage.

Scripture passages below provide material for comparison and reflection. They are not claims that a biblical writer prescribed a modern translation policy. Keep the words of the passage, a translator's explanation and your own conclusion distinguishable.

## 01 What choices are being compared?

**Main reading:** 1 Corinthians 13, comparing 13:4 in KJV, ASV and WEB.

> Charity suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not; charity vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up,

1 Corinthians 13:4 · KJV

### Start with a specific difference

At this verse the KJV uses “Charity,” while the ASV and WEB use “Love.” The WEB also gives “is patient” where the other two say “suffereth long.” These are observable differences in the texts. Write them down before explaining them or deciding what they imply about the translations as a whole.

Read the qualities described in the surrounding sentences. The chapter speaks about conduct and disposition, including kindness, envy and pride. Its meaning cannot be restricted to giving money simply because a reader associates the English word charity mainly with donations. The description around the word helps establish what is being discussed.

A changed word does not by itself show that the subject has changed. Equally, similar wording does not guarantee that readers have understood it. Comparison is useful when it sends us back to the whole sentence and the argument of the passage.

### Read the translators' stated aims

A preface can explain why an edition uses the kind of English it does. The WEB preface describes a revision of the ASV intended to improve readability, using contemporary spoken English. That is the project's account of its work; individual renderings still need examination in context. [WEB preface](https://ebible.org/engwebp/FRT01.htm)

Treat broad descriptions of translation approach as a starting point. A label cannot explain every choice in every verse. Ask what has changed here: vocabulary, sentence structure, punctuation, an explicit explanation or the underlying reading being translated. If you cannot yet tell, record the question instead of assigning a cause from appearance alone.

**Consider:** Which differences in 1 Corinthians 13:4 can you describe directly, before making a judgment about them?

**Reflect:** Does another rendering help you attend more closely to the conduct the chapter describes?

## 02 Familiar English may need careful hearing

**Main reading:** 1 Thessalonians 4:13–18 in KJV and ASV.

> For this we say unto you by the word of the Lord, that we which are alive and remain unto the coming of the Lord shall not prevent them which are asleep.

1 Thessalonians 4:15 · KJV

### Compare the word within its sentence

The ASV renders the relevant phrase “shall in no wise precede them that are fallen asleep.” Read that beside the KJV's “shall not prevent them which are asleep.” If prevent makes you think only of stopping something from happening, the comparison helps expose a possible misunderstanding.

The next verses speak of the dead in Christ rising first and the living being caught up together with them. The discussion concerns those who have died and those who remain, within an appeal that ends by telling the readers to comfort one another. The surrounding passage supports hearing the disputed word in relation to going before, rather than hindering.

This is a reason to examine familiar wording patiently. Being able to recite a verse is not always the same as understanding how every word functions in it. Nor does older wording become useless because one word requires explanation.

### Let clarification return you to the passage

After comparing, read verses 13–18 through again. Notice the grief addressed, the hope expressed and the comfort requested. A vocabulary discovery should help you hear those concerns more accurately; it should not reduce the passage to a lesson about an unusual word.

Keep a short note in your own words: what you first understood, what the comparison clarified and which surrounding sentences support the clarification. If a question requires a dictionary or discussion of the source language, use a source that explains the word in its actual setting. Comparing English versions can reveal a question without by itself proving every detail of the answer.

**Consider:** How do verses 16–18 help you understand the relationship described in verse 15?

**Reflect:** What familiar biblical expression might you need to read more slowly rather than assume you already understand?

## 03 Know which edition is in your hands

**Reading exercise:** Examine an edition's title, preface, revision information and contents; then read Luke 1:1–4.

> That thou mightest know the certainty of those things, wherein thou hast been instructed.

Luke 1:4 · KJV

### A name and a year do not answer every question

Record the full edition name, its language, any revision identification and the source from which you are reading. Distinguish a translation's historical name or initial publication year from the date of a particular printing, revision or digital file. A single year in a catalogue cannot necessarily answer all those questions.

The local catalogue, for example, labels its KJV entry with 1611. That field alone does not establish that the digital text reproduces every spelling, punctuation mark or page feature of a first printing. It identifies catalogue metadata; determining the precise textual edition would require a documented source history.

Luke's opening states the purpose of his own writing to Theophilus. It does not settle the identity or reliability of a later English edition. Read the passage for its stated purpose while allowing bibliographic questions about the edition to be answered by the relevant evidence.

### Check contents as well as wording

Editions within one translation family can differ in the books included and other conventions. The publisher identifies the WEB edition used as the source of this library's `web` text as a 66-book edition and lists related variants. Use the complete designation rather than assuming that every text bearing the family name has identical contents. [WEB edition information](https://ebible.org/engwebp/)

When a book or passage seems absent, first inspect the edition's contents and numbering information. A question about included books differs from a question about how a sentence is translated. Neither should be answered merely by counting search results or comparing the size of two files.

Keep unresolved questions specific: which revision is this, which books does it include, and does the reference system align with the other edition? This produces a more useful inquiry than simply calling one text older or newer.

**Consider:** What does your edition explicitly identify, and what would still need checking?

**Reflect:** How could recording the full edition name make a shared study or quotation clearer?

## 04 Read notes as explanations of the text

**Main reading:** Nehemiah 8:8; consult the preface and note conventions of the edition being compared.

> So they read in the book in the law of God distinctly, and gave the sense, and caused them to understand the reading.

Nehemiah 8:8 · KJV

### Identify what the note is doing

Nehemiah describes reading and helping the people understand. It gives us a reason to value understanding, but it does not make every modern footnote or heading part of the biblical passage. Read editorial material as assistance whose explanation can be examined.

A note may offer another rendering, explain a name, point to a related passage or identify a different textual reading. Those functions should not be confused. An alternative English rendering asks a different question from a note reporting that the text being translated differs among sources.

For a concrete example, the WEB preface explains the abbreviations MT, TR and NU used for different textual sources in its notes. Their meanings come from that edition's explanation; do not guess them from the letters alone. Detailed evaluation of those textual sources belongs to a fuller textual-history study. [WEB note conventions](https://ebible.org/engwebp/FRT01.htm)

### Keep the note attached to its claim

When a difference catches your attention, read the note on both sides of the comparison, where available. Write down what each actually says. A note may identify uncertainty without settling it. Your own conclusion should not sound more definite than the evidence you have examined.

Also check the format you are using. A plain-text excerpt can preserve verse wording while leaving out explanatory material available in the full edition. Absence of a note from an excerpt does not establish that the publishers supplied no explanation. Return to the source edition when the missing information matters.

Headings and cross-references can direct attention, but test the proposed connection by reading the passage itself. A helpful explanation should make the text easier to examine, not take its place.

**Consider:** Can you tell whether a particular note concerns wording, source text, background or a suggested cross-reference?

**Reflect:** What difference would it make to separate an editor's explanation from what the biblical sentence itself says?

## 05 Compare complete thoughts

**Main reading:** Philippians 1:27–30, comparing verse 27 in KJV, ASV and WEB.

> Only let your conversation be as it becometh the gospel of Christ: that whether I come and see you, or else be absent, I may hear of your affairs, that ye stand fast in one spirit, with one mind striving together for the faith of the gospel;

Philippians 1:27 · KJV

### Put the relevant wording beside itself

| Edition | Opening wording in Philippians 1:27 |
| --- | --- |
| KJV | “Only let your conversation be as it becometh the gospel of Christ” |
| ASV | “Only let your manner of life be worthy of the gospel of Christ” |
| WEB | “Only let your way of life be worthy of the Good News of Christ” |

These excerpts identify one comparison; they do not replace the full verse. Read the continuation about standing fast in one spirit and striving together. It helps show why conversation here should not be reduced to speech alone. The appeal concerns a shared manner of life in relation to the gospel.

Notice agreements as well as differences. All three connect the readers' conduct with Christ's gospel and continue with their standing together. A comparison that highlights only changed words can conceal the substantial thought the versions share.

### Ask one question at a time

Begin with a precise question: does this word concern speech only, or conduct more broadly? Use the surrounding sentences and the compared wording to work on that question. Do not leap from one clarified word to a claim that a translation is always superior or always unreliable.

Likewise, agreement among three English versions is evidence to consider, not an independent vote that settles every source-language question. The versions may share earlier work. The WEB's own preface identifies its relationship to the ASV, so those two should not be treated as wholly unrelated witnesses. [WEB preface](https://ebible.org/engwebp/FRT01.htm)

After comparison, read the complete paragraph in one edition again. State what has become clearer and what remains unresolved. The purpose is to understand and receive the passage, not assemble a private sentence by selecting whichever wording is most convenient from each version.

**Consider:** What supports understanding conversation as manner of life in this passage?

**Reflect:** How does the clarified wording sharpen the call to stand and strive together?

## 06 Choose for sustained reading and careful study

**Main reading:** 2 Timothy 3:14–17.

> That the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works.

2 Timothy 3:17 · KJV

### Make the reading purpose clear

For sustained reading, ask whether you can follow a paragraph's reasoning and a book's movement in the edition you use. For a shared study, consider whether everyone can locate the passage and identify the edition. For closer comparison, keep another clearly identified text available and consult explanatory notes when a difference matters.

Try a complete passage rather than choosing entirely from a favourite verse. Read aloud, summarize what you understood and identify words that need explanation. Notice whether notes and headings help you return to the text or distract you from its course. These are practical questions about your use of an edition, not a universal ranking of translations.

The examples in this study were chosen because their local texts could be checked. Inclusion here is not a claim that these are the only suitable editions or that every reader should use the same combination.

### Let comparison serve understanding and response

Second Timothy speaks of continuing in what has been learned, the Scriptures making one wise unto salvation through faith in Christ, and instruction that equips for good works. The passage's purpose gives a fitting question for our study: is the attention we give to wording helping us hear, learn and respond?

Keep your main reading continuous enough to understand whole books. Pause for a comparison when it clarifies a real question, and record the result accurately. If the evidence does not resolve the question, keep it open for further study while continuing with what is clear.

For your next reading, note the full edition name, read a paragraph, compare one important phrase and consult any relevant explanation. Then write a short account of the passage and one response it calls for. Good comparison ends with renewed attention to Scripture itself.

**Consider:** What do you need from an edition for continuous reading, and what additional help do you need for close study?

**Reflect:** How will you keep questions about wording connected to the understanding and conduct the passage seeks?
