# Letters and their message

Working content draft · Study 32 · Explore the Bible · Owner review pending

The New Testament letters speak to churches, fellowlabourers and beloved brethren. They give thanks, announce the gospel, answer questions, correct wrongdoing, ask for help and strengthen those who suffer. Their teaching comes through these acts of communication. A greeting, an argument and a request belong to the same writing.

The 21 letters from Romans to Jude differ in length, form and occasion. Some address a congregation; others name an individual; some speak to readers spread across several places. We learn to hear them by following what is said, to whom, and for what purpose, before gathering their sentences under a subject of our own.

These six studies offer a way into that reading. Philemon supplies a complete short example. Passages from other letters show why the same care matters when the writing is longer, the concern is different or the argument is difficult.

## 01 To whom is the letter spoken?

**Main readings:** Philemon 1–7; 1 Corinthians 1:10–13; Philippians 1:1–11.

> Now I beseech you, brethren, by the name of our Lord Jesus Christ, that ye all speak the same thing, and that there be no divisions among you; but that ye be perfectly joined together in the same mind and in the same judgment.

1 Corinthians 1:10 · KJV

### Read the address, not only the title

Philemon is named in the opening of the letter that bears his name. Apphia, Archippus and the church in his house are also addressed. The appeal that follows concerns Philemon personally, yet the opening places it among other people who share the life of the church. Read both the named person and that wider company into your understanding of the communication.

Philippians addresses all the saints in Christ Jesus at Philippi, with the bishops and deacons. Thanksgiving then remembers their fellowship in the gospel. The letter speaks within a relationship already marked by prayer, affection and shared labour. Its commands do not arrive as an unrelated list of things to improve.

Write down the addressees before summarizing a letter's message. Notice when the writer speaks to everyone and when a named person receives a particular charge. A letter's concern can be shared by the whole congregation while a specific action belongs to one reader.

### Hear a shared responsibility

In 1 Corinthians Paul beseeches the brethren to put away divisions. The following verses name the competing claims being made around Paul, Apollos, Cephas and Christ. His question, whether Christ is divided, meets a problem among people who are being addressed together.

The passage therefore asks more than whether one reader feels peaceful. It concerns what they say about themselves and one another, and how they understand their belonging to Christ. The communal address helps us hear the responsibility accurately. Personal application follows, but it should include our part in the life of others rather than shrinking every appeal to a private feeling.

**Consider:** What does each greeting or address tell you about whose response the writer seeks?

**Reflect:** Which instructions have you tended to hear only as an individual, although they concern a whole congregation?

## 02 Why is this being written?

**Main readings:** 1 Corinthians 1:10–13; 7:1–7; Galatians 1:6–12; Jude 3–4.

> Beloved, when I gave all diligence to write unto you of the common salvation, it was needful for me to write unto you, and exhort you that ye should earnestly contend for the faith which was once delivered unto the saints.

Jude 3 · KJV

### Begin with the reasons the writer gives

Paul says that people of Chloe's house have reported contentions among the Corinthians. Later he introduces matters about which the Corinthians wrote to him. The letter itself therefore names more than one source of concern: news received and questions sent. We can identify those sources without pretending to possess the full report or the Corinthians' original letter.

Jude is unusually direct about his purpose. He speaks of writing concerning the common salvation and of the need to exhort the readers to contend for the faith. The next verse describes people who have crept in, turning God's grace into lasciviousness and denying the Lord. Read the exhortation alongside that stated concern; it is not a general permission to make every disagreement a contest.

An occasion may involve several needs at once. Preserve that breadth when making an introduction. A letter can answer questions, give correction and encourage faithfulness without all its contents being explained by one incident.

### Do not invent the missing side of the conversation

Galatians speaks of people troubling the readers and perverting the gospel of Christ. Paul responds urgently and explains the source of the gospel he preached. Those words give us a real controversy. They do not give us a transcript of everything the troubling teachers said or a complete account of each person's motive.

The same restraint matters in Philemon. Paul writes conditionally about a wrong or debt; he does not narrate a theft. When reconstructing an occasion, distinguish the writer's statement from what you think may have happened. A plausible story should remain a proposal until there is evidence for it. Often the appeal can be understood more clearly by staying close to the words that are present.

**Consider:** Which reasons for writing are explicit in these passages? Where would a fuller account require an inference?

**Reflect:** How might your response change if you first understood the need an instruction addresses?

## 03 Follow an appeal from beginning to end

**Main reading:** Philemon 1–25.

> If thou count me therefore a partner, receive him as myself.

Philemon 17 · KJV

### Thanksgiving prepares the request

Paul gives thanks for Philemon's love and faith, and for the refreshing of the saints through him. He then makes an appeal for Onesimus. The movement is important: the love already acknowledged becomes relevant to the welcome now being requested. The opening is part of the appeal's meaning.

Paul says he could enjoin what is fitting, but rather beseeches for love's sake. He calls Onesimus his son, begotten in his bonds, and asks that he be received. He explains why he did not act without Philemon's mind: the benefit should be willing, not of necessity. Read those statements together to hear both the seriousness of the request and the willingness Paul seeks.

An outline of this letter can remain simple: greeting, thanksgiving, appeal for Onesimus, partnership and repayment, then lodging and greetings. What matters is seeing how one movement prepares the next. Dividing the text should help us follow the request rather than scatter it into independent sayings.

### Partnership becomes a particular action

The appeal reaches the words, receive him as myself. Paul offers to put a wrong or debt on his own account and writes that he will repay. He asks for joy and refreshing in the Lord, expresses confidence in Philemon's obedience, and requests a lodging in hope of coming through their prayers.

This is not merely a statement that kindness is good. It places a person's reception, a possible debt and the writer's own relationship with Philemon within the appeal. The wording about a beloved brother deepens what welcome means. The letter does not record Philemon's eventual action, and we should leave that outcome unstated.

Reading the whole communication also guards against reducing it to a slogan about authority or obedience. Paul presents love, brotherhood, partnership, willingness and practical responsibility together. A sound summary must make room for all of them.

**Consider:** How do the thanksgiving and the offer to repay strengthen the request in verse 17?

**Reflect:** What would it mean for an appeal to reconciliation to include your own practical responsibility?

## 04 Compare shared words within their arguments

**Main readings:** Romans 3:21–31; 6:1–4; Galatians 5:1–6; James 2:14–26.

> For in Jesus Christ neither circumcision availeth any thing, nor uncircumcision; but faith which worketh by love.

Galatians 5:6 · KJV

### Ask what each passage is answering

Romans speaks of righteousness, redemption in Christ, faith and the exclusion of boasting. James asks what profit there is when someone says he has faith but has no works. His immediate example is a brother or sister lacking clothing and daily food, sent away with words but without what the body needs. These are concrete arguments, not interchangeable entries under a single word.

Before comparing their conclusions, describe the question each passage addresses. In Romans, read the claims about grace and faith alongside the question about Jews and Gentiles. In James, follow the movement from a claim to faith through the neglected neighbour to Abraham and Rahab. Do not omit the sentences that make a difficult comparison more demanding.

This first reading does not settle every question Christians have debated about justification. It gives those questions a responsible beginning: whole arguments, accurately represented, rather than isolated phrases made to answer one another without their context.

### Let each letter keep its full witness

Paul's rejection of continuing in sin in Romans 6 belongs beside his teaching on grace. Galatians speaks of faith working by love within its warning about circumcision and justification by the law. James insists that faith without works is dead. Keeping those statements visible prevents a summary of one writer from erasing something that writer or another passage actually says.

A shared theme becomes a reason to read more carefully. Write a short account of each passage first, then compare them. Name both agreements and remaining questions. Where an explanation goes beyond the passage into a theological synthesis, identify it as an interpretation and show how it accounts for the words under discussion.

**Consider:** What does each passage say about the response to God, and what mistaken response does it challenge?

**Reflect:** Does your preferred summary leave room for every part of the readings, including the call to care for a neighbour?

## 05 Greetings, prayers and closing words belong

**Main readings:** Philemon 1–25; 1 John 1:1–4; Hebrews 13:20–25; Colossians 4:7–18.

> And these things write we unto you, that your joy may be full.

1 John 1:4 · KJV

### Do not force every letter into one pattern

Philemon has a greeting, thanksgiving, central appeal and closing greetings. First John begins with what has been heard, seen and handled concerning the Word of life, then speaks of declaration, fellowship and joy. Hebrews closes with blessing, exhortation, news and greetings, but its opening is the declaration that God has spoken by his Son.

These differences deserve to remain visible. A familiar letter pattern can help us notice a greeting or request, but it should not make us invent missing parts. Read the opening the work actually has. Ask what its first words establish and what kind of attention they invite.

First John's stated purpose joins writing to fellowship and joy. In Philemon, prayer and thanksgiving establish the relationship within which Paul makes his request. Such passages are part of the teaching's substance, not a threshold to hurry across before reaching the useful verses.

### Read the people and prayers at the end

Colossians names Tychicus and Onesimus as bearers of news. It remembers Epaphras labouring in prayer for the readers, sends greetings, gives an instruction to Archippus and ends with a request to remember Paul's bonds. The conclusion places the letter among people who travel, pray, serve and sustain relationships.

Hebrews closes with a prayer that the God of peace equip the readers to do his will, followed by the request to bear with the word of exhortation. Its final words hold divine work and human response together. Finishing a letter means hearing these prayers and requests, not stopping when a doctrinal passage ends.

**Consider:** What does each opening or ending contribute that a list of the letter's main subjects would miss?

**Reflect:** Whose prayers, messages and practical service help sustain the life of your own community?

## 06 Read, share and receive with care

**Main readings:** Colossians 4:7–18; 1 Thessalonians 5:25–28; 2 Peter 3:15–18.

> And when this epistle is read among you, cause that it be read also in the church of the Laodiceans; and that ye likewise read the epistle from Laodicea.

Colossians 4:16 · KJV

### Hear a letter among the brethren

Colossians explicitly instructs its readers to share the letter with the church of the Laodiceans and to read the epistle from Laodicea. First Thessalonians charges that its letter be read to all the holy brethren. These passages give direct evidence of letters intended to be heard beyond a single private reader.

The wording does not identify the epistle from Laodicea for us, and an introduction should not silently choose an identification. Nor do these two instructions by themselves narrate the complete history of how all 21 letters were collected. They show something more immediate: churches being charged to hear and share written instruction.

Try reading a short letter aloud from beginning to end. Listen for changes from thanksgiving to appeal, from warning to encouragement, and from the writer's circumstances to the readers' responsibilities. Then return to particular passages with the whole communication still in mind.

### Difficulty calls for patient reading

Second Peter acknowledges that some things in Paul's letters are hard to understand and warns about their being wrested, as the other Scriptures are. The closing appeal is to beware and to grow in grace and in the knowledge of Jesus Christ. Difficulty is named without becoming a reason to abandon the words or handle them carelessly.

Keep a record of what is clear, what requires more reading and what depends on an interpretation you have not yet tested. Use the whole letter, related passages and carefully identified explanations to continue. An honest question can remain open while the instruction already understood receives a faithful response.

Choose one letter and write a brief account after finishing it: who is addressed, what occasion is stated, how the writing moves, and what response it seeks. Check the account against its opening and closing. A useful summary should help you return to the letter with greater attention.

**Consider:** What do these passages establish about reading, sharing and interpreting letters? What later historical questions do they leave open?

**Reflect:** Which letter will you read in full, and what will you do with its clearest appeal while continuing to study what is difficult?
