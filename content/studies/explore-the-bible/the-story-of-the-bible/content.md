# The story of the Bible: from the garden to the city

Concepts: Garden · Promise · People · King · Exile · Christ · Nations · City

Motto: A garden lost. A city given. / One promise, kept.

Lead: The Bible is sixty-six books, written by many people over many centuries, and it tells one story. It begins with God and two people in a garden. It ends with God and a great crowd from every nation in a city. This study walks that story in eight movements and teaches one habit: before you read any verse, ask where you are in the story.

> I will bless those who bless you and curse those who curse you; and all the families of the earth will be blessed through you.

Genesis 12:3 · BSB

Frame: Most people meet the Bible one verse at a time: on a card, at a funeral, in a sermon, on a wall. That is not wrong, but it is like walking into a film halfway through and judging it by one scene. A promise in Genesis means more when you know how it was kept. A command at Mount Sinai sounds different once you know whom God had just rescued. A cry from the exile sounds different once you know there was a way home. So this study gives you three questions to ask of any passage. Where am I in the story? What has God promised by this point? What is still waiting to happen? It also gives you one thread to follow all the way through: God's purpose for the nations, meaning all the peoples of the world, not one people only. Watch for it in every movement. It is there from the first page to the last.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. Eight movements is one way to divide the story, chosen because each is a place where Scripture itself turns. Others divide it differently. A common four-part summary is creation, fall, redemption (God buying back and setting free) and new creation. Craig Bartholomew and Michael Goheen, in *The Drama of Scripture* [check: edition and year], tell it in six acts. All of these are maps of the same country. This study is the shelf's map, so it walks no passage at length. Each movement reads a few verses and then points you to the study that reads that part of the story closely. It gives no dates for the earliest movements, because careful readers date Abraham, the exodus and the conquest differently. The Psalms, the wisdom books and most of the prophets sit inside these movements and have studies of their own.

## 01 Creation and the fall: the garden lost

**Main readings:** Genesis 1–3 in outline; Genesis 3:15

Hook: The story's first page is good. Its third page is broken.

Line: God makes a good world and people to share it with Him. They turn away, and the first promise of rescue comes in the same breath as the judgement.

The Bible opens with God speaking a world into being and calling it good. It does not open with a battle between gods or a world that just happened. The people He makes are made "in His image" (Genesis 1:26–27). That means they are made to know Him, to reflect His character, and to look after His world on His behalf. The confession this site writes under, the Baptist Faith and Message 2000, says the same in its article III (man): human beings are specially made by God, and every person of every race "possesses full dignity and is worthy of respect and Christian love".

Genesis 2 puts the first people in a garden, Eden, with work to do and God near. Then chapter 3 tells of the fall. "The fall" is the church's short name for what happens there. The man and the woman listen to the serpent instead of God, and they eat what God told them not to eat. Sin enters the story. "Sin" means turning from God and His ways, in thought, word or act. Shame, blame, pain and death follow, and the chapter ends with the two of them sent out of the garden, its way back guarded (Genesis 3:23–24).

But before they leave, God speaks to the serpent:

> And I will put enmity between you and the woman, and between your seed and her seed. He will crush your head, and you will strike his heel.

Genesis 3:15 · BSB

"Enmity" means lasting hostility. "Seed" means offspring, children and descendants. The verse promises a long war between the serpent's line and the woman's line, and it ends with a crushed head, which is a fatal wound, against a struck heel, which is not.

[other] Jewish readers have often read the verse of the lasting struggle between humanity and the serpent, and so between people and the pull toward evil. Some ancient Aramaic paraphrases read in synagogues, the Targums, connect it with the days of the Messiah, God's promised anointed king [check: which Targums, likely Pseudo-Jonathan and Neofiti, and their wording].

[read] Christians since the early church have heard in it the first hint of the good news. Irenaeus of Lyon, in the second century, read the woman's offspring as Christ (*Against Heresies*, [check: book and chapter, likely 5.21.1]). That is why the verse is sometimes called the "first gospel" ("gospel" means good news). Be careful with what the verse itself says. It names no person, and the New Testament never quotes it word for word, though Paul echoes its picture when he tells a church that God will soon crush Satan under their feet (Romans 16:20). Read in the light of the whole story, Christians take it as a promise, and the rest of the Bible as the account of how it was kept.

Then the story widens. Genesis 4–11 tells of a murder, a flood, and a tower at Babel built to make a name for its builders. It ends with the human family scattered into nations, each with its own language and land (Genesis 10–11). Here our thread begins. The nations are now on the page, and they are far from God.

[open] How long the days of creation were, and how old the earth is, are questions on which Baptists differ. The confession affirms God as Creator and man as specially created, and it does not fix a timeline. This study does not choose.

## 02 The promise to Abraham: one family for every family

**Main readings:** Genesis 12:1–3; Genesis 12–50 in outline

Hook: The nations are scattered. God calls one old man without a child.

Line: God's answer to a scattered world is a promise to one family, made for the sake of every family on earth.

Right after Babel, God speaks to Abram (later renamed Abraham). In the Berean, "the LORD" in small capitals stands for God's personal name, the name He gives Himself.

> Then the LORD said to Abram, “Leave your country, your kindred, and your father’s household, and go to the land I will show you. I will make you into a great nation, and I will bless you; I will make your name great, so that you will be a blessing. I will bless those who bless you and curse those who curse you; and all the families of the earth will be blessed through you.

Genesis 12:1–3 · BSB

Count what God promises: a land, a great nation, a great name, and blessing. To "bless" is to give life and good, and to be glad over someone. The builders at Babel tried to make a name for themselves; here God gives the name. And look at the last line. The promise is not only for Abram. "All the families of the earth" are in view. One family is chosen so that every family can be reached.

God then binds Himself to this promise in a covenant (Genesis 15; 17). A "covenant" is a solemn, binding promise that sets up a relationship, with God as the one who makes it and keeps it. The rest of Genesis follows the promise through a family that keeps nearly losing it: Isaac, born when it seemed impossible; Jacob, a schemer whom God renames Israel; his twelve sons, whose families become the twelve tribes of Israel; and Joseph, sold into Egypt by his brothers and raised up to save them. Genesis ends with the family in Egypt and Joseph in a coffin (Genesis 50:26), still waiting for the land.

[read] The New Testament reads this promise as the start of the good news itself. Paul says Scripture announced the gospel to Abraham in advance, when it said all nations would be blessed through him (Galatians 3:8). The promise in Genesis 12 does not say how the blessing will reach the nations. That is what the rest of the story tells.

## 03 The exodus and Sinai: rescued, then given the law

**Main readings:** Exodus 19:4–6; Exodus and Numbers in outline

Hook: God does not say "obey Me and I will save you". He saves them first.

Line: God frees Abraham's descendants from slavery, brings them to Himself at a mountain, and gives them a calling toward the whole earth.

Abraham's family grows into a people in Egypt, and Egypt makes them slaves. God hears their cry and sends Moses. The book of Exodus (the word means "going out") tells of the plagues, the night of the Passover, and the crossing of the sea.

[other] Jewish families still retell this story every spring at Passover (Pesach), at a meal called the seder, with unleavened bread, bitter herbs and questions asked by the children. The Mishnah, an early collection of Jewish teaching, says that in every generation a person should see himself as if he himself had come out of Egypt (Pesachim 10:5) [check: wording in a standard translation]. Seven weeks later comes Shavuot, the Feast of Weeks, which Jewish tradition connects with God giving the Torah at Sinai. "Torah" is the Hebrew word for God's instruction or law, and the name Jews give to the first five books of the Bible.

[read] Christians read the Passover lamb as pointing ahead to Christ. That reading belongs to the study that owns Exodus 12, and it is done there.

Three months out of Egypt, Israel camps at Mount Sinai, and God gives Moses this message:

> ‘You have seen for yourselves what I did to Egypt, and how I carried you on eagles’ wings and brought you to Myself. Now if you will indeed obey My voice and keep My covenant, you will be My treasured possession out of all the nations—for the whole earth is Mine. And unto Me you shall be a kingdom of priests and a holy nation.’ These are the words that you are to speak to the Israelites.

Exodus 19:4–6 · BSB

Notice the order. Rescue comes first ("I carried you"), and only then the call to obey. The law given at Sinai, beginning with the Ten Commandments, is a way of life for people already set free. It is not a ladder for slaves to climb out of Egypt.

Notice the thread too. "The whole earth is Mine." A priest is someone who stands between God and people, bringing people near to God. Israel is to be a whole "kingdom of priests", a nation set apart ("holy") among the nations, showing the world what God is like. Then God gives instructions for a tent, the tabernacle, so that He can live among His people (Exodus 25:8). The God of the garden is near again, though now behind curtains and sacrifices.

The book of Numbers tells what came next, and it is a hard chapter of the story. At the edge of the land, the people believe the frightened report of their spies and refuse to go in (Numbers 13–14). A whole generation wanders and dies in the wilderness for forty years. Even so, God keeps His word. A foreign prophet, Balaam, hired to curse Israel, can only bless them (Numbers 22–24), and the book ends with their children counted and ready. Deuteronomy follows with Moses' last sermons to that new generation on the plains of Moab.

## 04 The land and the kingdom: a king from David's line

**Main readings:** Joshua 21:43–45; Judges 21:25; 2 Samuel 7:12–16

Hook: God gives His people a home. Then they ask for a king.

Line: Israel enters the land, falls apart without a king, and receives a promise that David's royal line will last forever.

Under Joshua, Moses' successor, Israel crosses the Jordan and enters the land promised to Abraham. The book of Joshua sums up God's side of it plainly:

> Not one of all the LORD’s good promises to the house of Israel had failed; everything was fulfilled.

Joshua 21:45 · BSB

[read] The same book admits that much land was still to be taken (Joshua 13:1), and Judges 1 lists towns Israel did not drive out. Read together, these say that God kept faith, and that Israel did not finish what He gave them to do. The gap between the two is the story of the next book.

Judges tells a cycle that keeps getting worse: Israel turns to other gods, falls under enemies, cries out, and God raises a rescuer, a "judge". Then the cycle begins again. The book's last line sounds like a verdict:

> In those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes.

Judges 21:25 · BSB

Set in those same days is the short book of Ruth, about a widow from Moab, a foreign nation, who chooses Israel's God and becomes the great-grandmother of King David (Ruth 4:17). The nations thread runs right through the darkest times.

Israel asks for a king. The first, Saul, fails. The second, David, a shepherd, is called a man after God's own heart, though he also falls badly. David wants to build God a house, a temple. God answers that He will build David a house instead, meaning a family line of kings. In this chapter the word "house" means both.

> And when your days are fulfilled and you rest with your fathers, I will raise up your descendant after you, who will come from your own body, and I will establish his kingdom. He will build a house for My Name, and I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever. I will be his Father, and he will be My son. When he does wrong, I will discipline him with the rod of men and with the blows of the sons of men. But My loving devotion will never be removed from him as I removed it from Saul, whom I moved out of your way. Your house and kingdom will endure forever before Me, and your throne will be established forever.

2 Samuel 7:12–16 · BSB

"Kingdom" means a king's rule and the people under it. The first answer to this promise is Solomon, David's son, who builds the temple. But verse 14 expects sons who do wrong, and Solomon and those after him do. So the promise of a throne "forever" leaves a question hanging over every king that follows: which son of David will finally keep it?

[other] Jewish prayer still holds on to this hope. The daily prayer called the Amidah asks God to make the offshoot of David flourish [check: wording of the blessing in a standard prayer book].

[read] The New Testament answers that the son is Jesus, called "the son of David" in its first verse (Matthew 1:1). The study of Christ in the Old Testament reads 2 Samuel 7 for the king; Covenants and promises reads it as a covenant.

## 05 Exile and return: carried away and brought home

**Main readings:** Jeremiah 31:31–34; Isaiah 49:6; 2 Chronicles 36:22–23; Nehemiah 8:8

Hook: The temple burns. And in the ashes, God promises something new.

Line: The kingdom splits and falls, the people are taken into exile, and the prophets promise a new covenant, a return, and a light for the nations.

After Solomon the kingdom splits in two: Israel in the north, Judah in the south (1 Kings 12). God sends prophets, messengers who speak His word, to warn both. Kings come and go, most of them unfaithful. The northern kingdom falls to Assyria in the 700s BC. In 587 or 586 BC Babylon destroys Jerusalem and its temple and carries much of Judah away (scholars differ by a year). This is the exile: God's people taken out of the land, as the first couple were taken out of the garden. Lamentations and Psalm 137 give voice to that grief.

In that dark time, God speaks through Jeremiah:

> Behold, the days are coming, declares the LORD, when I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel and with the house of Judah. It will not be like the covenant I made with their fathers when I took them by the hand to lead them out of the land of Egypt— a covenant they broke, though I was a husband to them,” declares the LORD. “But this is the covenant I will make with the house of Israel after those days, declares the LORD. I will put My law in their minds and inscribe it on their hearts. And I will be their God, and they will be My people. No longer will each man teach his neighbor or his brother, saying, ‘Know the LORD,’ because they will all know Me, from the least of them to the greatest, declares the LORD. For I will forgive their iniquities and will remember their sins no more.

Jeremiah 31:31–34 · BSB

The problem was never that God's law was bad. The people broke it. So the new covenant promises a change inside: the law written "on their hearts", every one of them knowing God, and sins ("iniquities") forgiven and remembered no more. On the night before He died, Jesus spoke of "the new covenant" in His blood (Luke 22:20), and the letter to the Hebrews quotes this passage at length (Hebrews 8:8–12).

Isaiah, too, looks past the return to something wider. God speaks to His Servant:

> He says: “It is not enough for You to be My Servant, to raise up the tribes of Jacob, and to restore the protected ones of Israel. I will also make You a light for the nations, to bring My salvation to the ends of the earth.”

Isaiah 49:6 · BSB

[other] A few verses earlier the Servant is addressed as "Israel" (Isaiah 49:3), and Jewish readers take the Servant as the people of Israel, or a faithful part of it, called to be a light to the nations.

[read] The New Testament hears Jesus in it. Old Simeon, holding the baby Jesus, calls Him a light for revelation to the Gentiles (Luke 2:32), "Gentiles" meaning the non-Jewish nations; and Paul and Barnabas quote this verse to explain why they took the message to the nations (Acts 13:47). Either way, look at the thread: restoring Israel is "not enough". Salvation is meant for "the ends of the earth". Remember that phrase. It comes back.

The return came in the 530s BC, when Persia had conquered Babylon. The books of Chronicles retell the whole story from Adam to this moment, and close with it:

> In the first year of Cyrus king of Persia, to fulfill the word of the LORD spoken through Jeremiah, the LORD stirred the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia to send a proclamation throughout his kingdom and to put it in writing as follows: “This is what Cyrus king of Persia says: ‘The LORD, the God of heaven, who has given me all the kingdoms of the earth, has appointed me to build a house for Him at Jerusalem in Judah. Whoever among you belongs to His people, may the LORD his God be with him, and may he go up.’”

2 Chronicles 36:22–23 · BSB

In the Jewish order of the Hebrew Bible, Chronicles is the last book. The whole collection ends on those words: "may he go up". It is an open door, not a full stop. In Christian Bibles the same proclamation opens the book of Ezra (Ezra 1:1–3), which tells of the temple rebuilt. Nehemiah tells of the city walls rebuilt, and of a day when the people gathered to hear God's law:

> So they read from the Book of the Law of God, explaining it and giving insight, so that the people could understand what was being read.

Nehemiah 8:8 · BSB

Yet the return is not the end. The people are home, but under foreign rulers, with no son of David on the throne. The prophets after the exile, Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi, still look forward. Then the Old Testament falls silent for about four centuries.

## 06 Christ: when the time had fully come

**Main readings:** Galatians 4:4; Mark 1:14–15; John 1:14

Hook: Every thread in the story has been left open. Now they are pulled together.

Line: God sends His Son, the promised son of Abraham and son of David, who announces God's kingdom, dies and rises again.

Paul sums up the turn of the whole story in one sentence:

> But when the time had fully come, God sent His Son, born of a woman, born under the law,

Galatians 4:4 · BSB

"Born of a woman", like the woman's offspring in Genesis 3. "Born under the law", a Jewish man inside the covenant made at Sinai. Matthew opens his Gospel by calling Jesus "the son of David, the son of Abraham" (Matthew 1:1). The promises are not dropped. They are kept in a person.

Mark gives Jesus' first message:

> After the arrest of John, Jesus went into Galilee and proclaimed the gospel of God. “The time is fulfilled,” He said, “and the kingdom of God is near. Repent and believe in the gospel!”

Mark 1:14–15 · BSB

"The kingdom of God" is God's rule, the reign the story has been waiting for since Judges ended with "no king in Israel". To "repent" means to turn around: to change your mind about sin and God, and to turn your life back toward Him. "Believe in the gospel" means to trust this good news.

John tells it with the language of the tabernacle:

> The Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us. We have seen His glory, the glory of the one and only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth.

John 1:14 · BSB

"The Word" is John's name for the Son, who was with God and was God before He became a man (John 1:1). The Greek word behind "made His dwelling" is related to the word for a tent, and many readers hear in it the tent where God lived among Israel. "Grace" is God's kindness given to people who have not earned it. God, who walked in the garden and lived in the tabernacle, now lives among people as one of them.

Jesus heals, teaches, forgives and calls followers. Then He is crucified under the Roman governor Pontius Pilate, as the Apostles' Creed puts it. The New Testament says He laid down His life of His own accord (John 10:18), and that He died for the sins of the whole world, whose guilt falls on every one of us, not on one people. On the third day He rose from the dead. This is the centre of the whole story: the serpent's head crushed, the new covenant made in His blood, the Son of David raised to a throne that will last forever.

## 07 The church sent to the nations: to the ends of the earth

**Main readings:** Acts 1:8; Acts in outline

Hook: They ask Him when. He tells them where.

Line: The risen Christ sends His followers, with the Holy Spirit, to take the good news from Jerusalem to every nation.

Before He returns to the Father, the risen Jesus gives His followers a task:

> But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit comes upon you, and you will be My witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth.

Acts 1:8 · BSB

"The Holy Spirit" is God Himself, present and at work in His people. A "witness" tells what they have seen. And there is that phrase again: "the ends of the earth", from Isaiah 49:6. The light for the nations is now to be carried by His people.

The book of Acts follows that line outward, almost like a map. The Spirit comes at Pentecost, a Jewish harvest feast, on Jews gathered in Jerusalem from many lands (Acts 2). The message reaches Samaria (Acts 8), then a Roman officer named Cornelius and his household, the first Gentiles to believe in Acts (Acts 10). Paul carries it across the Roman world, and the book ends with him in Rome, the empire's capital, still preaching. The church (not a building but the people who belong to Christ, gathered in local congregations) is the family of Abraham growing to include every family of the earth. The letters of the New Testament were written to these young churches.

[read] The confession this site writes under says, in article XI (evangelism and missions), that every believer and every church is to "endeavor to make disciples of all nations". The story has not finished this movement. Every reader of this page lives inside it.

[open] Has the church taken Israel's place in the story, or does God still have purposes for the Jewish people as a nation? Peter gives the church names first given to Israel at Sinai (1 Peter 2:9), and Paul speaks of a future for Israel (Romans 11). Baptists answer this in more than one way. Some read the covenants as the Second London Baptist Confession does (chapter 7); some hold a dispensational reading, which keeps Israel and the church more distinct; and some hold what is called progressive covenantalism, set out by Peter Gentry and Stephen Wellum in *Kingdom through Covenant*. The confession does not settle it, and this study does not choose. Covenants and promises shows the views side by side.

## 08 The new creation: the city that comes down

**Main readings:** Revelation 21:1–5

Hook: The story does not go back to the garden. It goes on to a city.

Line: God makes all things new, and lives with His people forever, with nothing left to spoil it.

The last pages of the Bible answer its first ones:

> Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth, for the first heaven and earth had passed away, and the sea was no more. I saw the holy city, the new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband. And I heard a loud voice from the throne saying: “Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man, and He will dwell with them. They will be His people, and God Himself will be with them as their God. ‘He will wipe away every tear from their eyes,’ and there will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain, for the former things have passed away.” And the One seated on the throne said, “Behold, I make all things new.” Then He said, “Write this down, for these words are faithful and true.”

Revelation 21:1–5 · BSB

Listen for the echoes. "They will be His people, and God Himself will be with them as their God" is the covenant promise from Sinai to Jeremiah, now kept in full. "The dwelling place of God is with man" is the garden, the tabernacle and John 1:14 together, with no curtain left. Death, which entered in Genesis 3, is gone. A few verses on, the nations walk by the city's light (Revelation 21:24), and the leaves of the tree of life are "for the healing of the nations" (Revelation 22:2). The tree from the garden is back, and the curse is no more (Revelation 22:3). The thread of the nations ends here, in a city with gates that are never shut. And it is a city, not a garden: God does not simply undo the story. He brings it to a fuller end than its beginning.

[read] Many readers take "the sea was no more" as a picture, since the sea often stands in Scripture for chaos and danger. That is a reading of the image, not a statement of the text.

[read] The confession says plainly, in article X (last things), that Christ will return personally and visibly, the dead will be raised, and Christ will judge all people. Those who belong to Him will live forever with the Lord, and the unrighteous will face everlasting punishment in hell. This same chapter of Revelation says so too (Revelation 21:8). The story has a judgement in it, and it also has a door still open.

[open] The order of events at the end, and what the thousand years of Revelation 20 mean, are questions on which Baptists differ. The confession affirms Christ's return and judgement, not a timetable, and this study does not choose.

[care] If you are grieving, "He will wipe away every tear" is a promise for the end, not a demand that your tears stop now. Jesus Himself wept at a friend's grave (John 11:35). Grief and dying walks with people who are mourning.

## Finding your place in the story

Way: You have walked eight movements: creation and fall, the promise to Abraham, the exodus and Sinai, the land and the kingdom, exile and return, Christ, the church sent to the nations, and the new creation. Here is the habit to carry to the next passage you open. Ask where you are in the story. Ask what God has promised by that point. Ask what is still waiting. And watch for the nations, because from Babel to the city they are never out of God's sight. A verse read this way is not a lonely saying on a card. It is a scene in a story God is telling, and keeping.

Close: Nehemiah's readers explained God's law "so that the people could understand what was being read" (Nehemiah 8:8). That is what this map is for. It does not replace reading. It tells you where you are standing when you read.

Invite: The story has a place in it for you. It is not earned by being good enough, or by belonging to the right family or nation. God offers it freely to everyone who turns from their sin and trusts Jesus Christ, because, as the confession says, "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord". If you want to know what that means and how to respond, begin with The good news.
