# Genesis 1–11: the questions readers bring

**Study 50 · Explore the Bible · First content draft · 2026-11-06**

Most people open the first pages of the Bible with a question already in hand. How long were the days? Where did Cain find a wife? Did Methuselah really live 969 years? Who were the Nephilim? These are fair questions, and the text can bear them. Genesis tells the story of the whole world in eleven chapters, and then, in one verse, narrows to one man:

> Then the LORD said to Abram, "Leave your country, your kindred, and your father's household, and go to the land I will show you."

Genesis 12:1 · BSB

This study takes eight of the questions readers bring to those eleven chapters, one at a time. It does not retell the story: the Topics card *In the beginning* walks its people and families. It does not repeat Study 29, *Humanity, sin and renewal*, which teaches what the image of God and the fall mean. On each question it reads what the text says, shows the views that Bible-believing Christians hold and names who holds them, and says where the text stops. Where the Baptist Faith and Message 2000 decides, this study says so: it confesses God as "the Creator", and that "Man is the special creation of God". Where it does not decide, neither does this study.

Carry three questions into every part:

1. **What does the text say, and what does it leave unsaid?** Read the verse before the view.
2. **Where does the rest of Scripture take it up?** Later Scripture is the first commentary.
3. **Which part is the text, and which part is a reading of it?** A reading may be good and still be a reading.

And notice first what every view in this study holds in common: "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1 · BSB). One God who made everything, a world He called good, one human family, sin that is real, judgment that is real, and grace that does not let go.

## 01 How long were the days?

**Main readings:** Genesis 1:1–2:4; Exodus 20:11; Psalm 90:4; 2 Peter 3:8; Hebrews 11:3.

The sun and moon are not made until the fourth day. Yet already, at the end of the first, "there was evening, and there was morning—the first day" (Genesis 1:5 · BSB).

### What the text shows

Six days close with the same refrain; the seventh has none (2:1–3). The Berean notes that "the first day" is "Literally day one". The lights that "mark the seasons and days and years" come on day four (1:14). The days fall into two sets of three that answer each other: light on day one and the lights on day four; the sky and the waters on day two and birds and sea creatures on day five; dry land and plants on day three and land animals and man on day six. The next verse uses "day" for the whole work: "in the day that the LORD God made them" (2:4 · BSB). And Moses builds Israel's week on the pattern:

> For in six days the LORD made the heavens and the earth and the sea and all that is in them, but on the seventh day He rested.

Exodus 20:11 · BSB

### The views Christians hold

- **Six ordinary days and a young earth.** The days are days as Exodus 20:11 counts them, and the family lists of Genesis 5 and 11 place creation thousands of years ago, not millions. Held by Answers in Genesis and the Institute for Creation Research, and set out for a generation by John C. Whitcomb and Henry M. Morris in *The Genesis Flood* (1961). Among Southern Baptists, Albert Mohler, president of the Southern Baptist Theological Seminary, holds it.
- **Long ages (the day-age view).** "Day" can mean a long period, as in 2:4; the order is real, the length is open. Readers of this kind also cite Psalm 90:4, "a thousand years are but a day that passes" (BSB). Held by Hugh Ross and Reasons to Believe (*A Matter of Days*, 2004).
- **A gap before the days.** A long age passed between 1:1 and 1:2. Spread by the notes of the *Scofield Reference Bible* (1909); fewer hold it today.
- **A framework.** The two sets of three are an arrangement, not a timetable; the week is the frame in which God's making of everything is shown. Meredith G. Kline, a Reformed scholar, argued it ("Because It Had Not Rained", *Westminster Theological Journal*, 1958).
- **God's workweek.** Real days of God's work, told on the pattern of a human week without fixing their length. C. John Collins, *Genesis 1–4* (2006).
- **A temple made ready.** Genesis 1 tells how God ordered the world for its purposes and took up His rest in it, as a temple is dedicated over seven days. John H. Walton, *The Lost World of Genesis One* (2009).

The question is old. Augustine, early in the fifth century, wrote of the first days: "What kind of days these were it is extremely difficult, or perhaps impossible for us to conceive" (*City of God* XI.6), and in the same work held that "not 6000 years have yet passed" (XII.10). John Calvin, against those who said the world was made in a moment, concluded "that God himself took the space of six days" (*Commentaries on Genesis*, on 1:5). Baptists today are found on more than one side: two professors at Southeastern Baptist Theological Seminary, Kenneth Keathley and Mark Rooker, wrote *40 Questions About Creation and Evolution* (2014) from opposite sides of the earth's age.

### Where the reading stops

Young-earth readers answer that Psalm 90 and 2 Peter 3:8 speak of how God sees time, not of how long creation took. The text says God made everything, in order, by His word, and that "it was very good" (1:31 · BSB). It does not state the age of the earth, and Scripture nowhere adds up the years from creation to Abraham. The Baptist Faith and Message confesses God as Creator and does not fix a timeline. This study does not choose. What no view may do is treat Genesis 1 as less than true: "By faith we understand that the universe was formed at God's command" (Hebrews 11:3 · BSB).

**Consider:** Exodus 20:11 grounds a week of rest in the days of creation. Whatever their length, what does the pattern ask of Israel?

**Reflect:** Is your interest in Genesis 1 mostly about *when*? What does the chapter want you to know about *Who*?

## 02 Were Adam and Eve real people?

**Main readings:** Genesis 2:7; 5:1–5; 1 Chronicles 1:1; Luke 3:38; Matthew 19:4–6; Acts 17:26; Romans 5:12–19; Jude 14.

Luke traces Jesus' family back, name by name, until the list runs out: "the son of Seth, the son of Adam, the son of God" (Luke 3:38 · BSB).

### What the text shows

In Hebrew *adam* is both a word and a name; at Genesis 5:2 the Berean notes that "man" is "Hebrew Adam". Yet Genesis gives this Adam a son, an age and a death: "So Adam lived a total of 930 years, and then he died" (5:5 · BSB). Chronicles opens Israel's whole history with three names: "Adam, Seth, Enosh" (1 Chronicles 1:1 · BSB). Jesus answers a question about marriage from "the beginning" (Matthew 19:4 · BSB). Paul tells Athens, "From one man He made every nation of men" (Acts 17:26 · BSB), and sets Adam on a timeline beside Moses: "death reigned from Adam until Moses" (Romans 5:14 · BSB). Jude calls Enoch "the seventh from Adam" (Jude 14 · BSB).

Study 29 reads what Romans 5 teaches about sin and grace. The point here is narrower. The New Testament counts generations from Adam, places him in history before Moses, and builds an argument on "one man" whose one act reached everyone, as Christ's one obedience does (Romans 5:12, 19).

### The views Christians hold

- **The first pair, specially made, the ancestors of all.** The church's reading through most of its history, and the confession's: "Man is the special creation of God, made in His own image", innocent at first, who by his free choice "sinned against God and brought sin into the human race". Young-earth writers hold this, and so do many who hold an old earth.
- **Real people in a longer history.** Adam and Eve were real people and real ancestors, and the earth is old: C. John Collins, *Did Adam and Eve Really Exist?* (2011). Some go further. S. Joshua Swamidass proposes that God specially made Adam and Eve while others lived outside the garden, and that in time every person came to descend from the pair (*The Genealogical Adam and Eve*, 2019). William Lane Craig places them far earlier than most readers have, as the ancestors of all humanity (*In Quest of the Historical Adam*, 2021).
- **Adam as a figure only.** Some scholars read Adam as a picture of Israel or of every person, not a man who lived (Peter Enns, *The Evolution of Adam*, 2012). This view is shown so that you can recognise it. It is not the site's, because the confession, and the New Testament's argument from one man, treat Adam as a man who lived.

Four of these positions answer one another in *Four Views on the Historical Adam* (2013), edited by Matthew Barrett and Ardel Caneday.

### Where the reading stops

The text does not say how many years before Christ Adam lived, and it tells how God formed man "from the dust of the ground" (Genesis 2:7 · BSB) in that one line and no more. The site holds what the confession holds: Adam was a real man, specially created, whose sin brought sin into the human race. How he fits a long history is a question on which people who hold that much still differ, and this study does not choose among them.

**Consider:** Luke ends his list with "the son of God" (3:38). Why carry the line all the way back, past Abraham and David, to Adam?

**Reflect:** Paul says every nation was made "from one man" (Acts 17:26). Who is it hardest for you to see as family?

## 03 Where did Cain's wife come from?

**Main readings:** Genesis 3:20; 4:1–2, 14–17, 25–26; 5:3–4; Leviticus 18:9; Genesis 20:12.

Cain is afraid that "whoever finds me will kill me" (Genesis 4:14 · BSB). Who was out there to find him?

### What the text shows

Genesis names three sons of Adam and Eve: Cain, Abel and Seth. It never says they were the only children. Chapter 5 adds what chapter 4 leaves out: "Adam lived 800 years and had other sons and daughters" (5:4 · BSB). Eve is named "the mother of all the living" (3:20 · BSB). Cain's wife appears without introduction, "And Cain had relations with his wife", and in the same verse he "built a city" (4:17 · BSB), which supposes more than a handful of people. The chapter does not keep time by births: Seth is born after Abel's death (4:25), when Adam is 130 (5:3), long enough for children and grandchildren in plenty.

### The views Christians hold

- **A sister or a niece.** The oldest and most common answer. Augustine wrote that in the first generation "brothers and sisters were united in marriage", because "there were no human beings but the brothers and sisters born of those two first parents", and that once people were many, such marriage became wrong (*City of God* XV.16). The law later forbids it: "You must not have sexual relations with your sister" (Leviticus 18:9 · BSB). Before that law, Abraham married his half-sister: "she really is my sister, the daughter of my father" (Genesis 20:12 · BSB). This is the answer of young-earth writers and of most who hold that Adam and Eve are the ancestors of all.
- **Someone from outside Eden's family.** Some who hold the genealogical view in part 02 suggest that other people were alive and that Cain married among them; they read "the mother of all the living" and Paul's "one man" as an ancestry that in time reaches everyone. An older form, that there were whole peoples before Adam, was argued by Isaac La Peyrère in *Prae-Adamitae* (1655) and rejected by the churches of his day.

### Where the reading stops

Genesis does not name Cain's wife or say whose daughter she was. The chapter was not written to answer this question. It was written to show what became of a man who killed his brother, and how a line of grace went on through Seth: "At that time men began to call upon the name of the LORD" (4:26 · BSB).

**Consider:** Why might Genesis 4 leave Cain's wife unnamed, while it names his son and his city?

**Reflect:** The question about Cain's wife can crowd out the question God asked Cain: "Where is your brother Abel?" (4:9 · BSB). Whose good are you being asked about?

## 04 Did they really live so long?

**Main readings:** Genesis 5:1–32; 6:3; 11:10–32; 25:7; 47:9; Psalm 90:10–12; Matthew 1:8; 1 Chronicles 3:11–12; Luke 3:35–36.

The oldest man in the Bible gets one short verse: "So Methuselah lived a total of 969 years, and then he died" (Genesis 5:27 · BSB).

### What the text shows

Genesis 5 has its own refrain. Each man lives, becomes a father, has "other sons and daughters", and "then he died". It breaks once, for Enoch: "Enoch walked with God, and then he was no more, because God had taken him away" (5:24 · BSB). After the flood the ages fall: Shem 600 (11:10–11), Terah 205 (11:32), Abraham 175 (25:7), Moses 120 (Deuteronomy 34:7). Jacob tells Pharaoh that his years "have not matched the years of the travels of my fathers" (Genesis 47:9 · BSB). By the psalm of Moses, "The length of our days is seventy years— or eighty if we are strong" (Psalm 90:10 · BSB). Just before the flood God says of man, "his days shall be 120 years" (Genesis 6:3 · BSB); readers take it two ways. Some early Christian writers, Lactantius among them, read it as a limit on the length of a life; Calvin answered that it means "a time of repentance to be granted to the whole world" before the flood (*Commentaries on Genesis*, on 6:3).

### The views Christians hold

- **Real years, as written.** Augustine would not hear of reading the numbers as short years, as though "one of our years may be supposed to be equal to ten of theirs" (*City of God* XV.12), and argued that the years then were as long as ours (XV.14). Young-earth writers hold this, and so do many who hold an old earth.
- **Real people, numbers with a purpose.** Some evangelical scholars read the ages as real people's lives told in numbers that also carry honour and pattern, and set them beside Babylonian lists such as the Sumerian King List, in which kings before a flood reign for tens of thousands of years. Beside those lists Genesis is restrained, and it ends every life but one in death.

A second question rides on the first: can the lists be added up to date the creation? James Ussher did so and reached 4004 BC (*Annales Veteris Testamenti*, 1650). William Henry Green of Princeton answered that biblical family lists can skip generations ("Primeval Chronology", *Bibliotheca Sacra*, 1890). Scripture shows such a skip: Matthew writes "Joram the father of Uzziah" (Matthew 1:8 · BSB), where Chronicles lists Ahaziah, Joash and Amaziah between them (1 Chronicles 3:11–12). Luke's line has "Cainan" between Shelah and Arphaxad (Luke 3:36 · BSB); the Berean's note at Genesis 11:13 explains that the Hebrew lacks him and the Greek Old Testament includes him, and adds 100 years to six of the ages in that list. Young-earth writers reply that Genesis 5 and 11 give each father's age when the next name was born, which fixes the span whatever the relationship.

### Where the reading stops

The text gives the numbers and does not explain them, or say why lives grew shorter. What it plainly says is the refrain: "and then he died". The death God warned of reigns over the long lives as over the short ones, all but Enoch's.

**Consider:** In a chapter of "and then he died", what does Enoch's verse make you notice?

**Reflect:** Psalm 90 turns the shortness of life into a prayer: "So teach us to number our days, that we may present a heart of wisdom" (90:12 · BSB). What would numbering yours change this week?

## 05 Who were the sons of God and the Nephilim?

**Main readings:** Genesis 6:1–8; Job 1:6; 38:7; Numbers 13:32–33; 1 Peter 3:19–20; 2 Peter 2:4–5; Jude 6–7; Matthew 22:30.

Four verses, among the strangest in the Bible, stand just before the flood.

> the sons of God saw that the daughters of men were beautiful, and they took as wives whomever they chose.

Genesis 6:2 · BSB

### What the text shows

"The Nephilim were on the earth in those days—and afterward as well—when the sons of God had relations with the daughters of men" (6:4 · BSB). Elsewhere in the Old Testament "sons of God" names heavenly beings, who "came to present themselves before the LORD" (Job 1:6 · BSB) and "shouted for joy" at creation (Job 38:7 · BSB). The Nephilim appear once more, in the spies' report: "We even saw the Nephilim there—the descendants of Anak that come from the Nephilim!" (Numbers 13:33 · BSB), part of what the text calls "a bad report" (13:32 · BSB). The passage leads straight into God's grief, because "every inclination of the thoughts of his heart was altogether evil all the time" (Genesis 6:5 · BSB).

### The views Christians hold

- **Angels.** Fallen angels took human wives. The oldest reading on record. It is told at length in the ancient book called 1 Enoch, which the Berean's own note at 2 Peter 2:4 cites, and Justin Martyr, writing about AD 150, said that the angels "transgressed this appointment, and were captivated by love of women" (*Second Apology* 5). Michael S. Heiser argues it today (*The Unseen Realm*, 2015). Its readers point to Job's use of the phrase, and to Peter and Jude, who speak of angels who sinned and "abandoned their proper dwelling" (Jude 6 · BSB) beside the flood (2 Peter 2:4–5) and beside Sodom's pursuit of "strange flesh" (Jude 7 · BSB).
- **The line of Seth.** The sons of Seth, who called on the LORD (4:26), married into the line of Cain and were drawn away. Augustine held that "the sons of God, who were according to the flesh the sons of Seth" fell into this when "they forsook righteousness" (*City of God* XV.23), and John Calvin followed him. Its readers point to Jesus' words that in the resurrection people "will be like the angels in heaven" (Matthew 22:30 · BSB), who do not marry, and to the two family lines of Genesis 4 and 5 just before. Angel-view readers answer that Jesus speaks of angels "in heaven", not of those who left their place.
- **Kings and great men.** Rulers who claimed divine rank took wives as they pleased: "whomever they chose" (6:2). An ancient Jewish Aramaic translation, which Calvin calls "the Chaldean paraphrase", took the phrase as the sons of nobles (Calvin rejected it), and Meredith G. Kline argued a form of this view ("Divine Kingship and Genesis 6:1–4", *Westminster Theological Journal*, 1962). Its readers point to the "mighty men of old" (6:4) and to an earth "full of violence" (6:11 · BSB).

### Where the reading stops

The text does not say who the sons of God were. It does not quite say that the Nephilim were their children: they were on the earth "when" those unions happened, and readers take that word both ways. Nor does it say what became of them; the meaning of the word *Nephilim* itself is uncertain. Study 53, *Angels, demons and spiritual powers*, reads what Scripture says of angels; this study does not choose among the three views. What Genesis does make plain is where the passage turns: "Noah, however, found favor in the eyes of the LORD" (6:8 · BSB).

**Consider:** Genesis 6:1–4 is followed at once by 6:5–8. Whatever the sons of God were, what does the passage show about how far things had gone?

**Reflect:** The strangest passage in these chapters ends in favour shown to one man. Where have you seen grace come in where you least expected it?

## 06 How big was the flood, and could the ark hold them?

**Main readings:** Genesis 6:13–22; 7:1–24; 8:4–14; 9:11; 41:57; Matthew 24:37–39; Hebrews 11:7; 1 Peter 3:20; 2 Peter 3:5–7.

"The waters rose and covered the mountaintops to a depth of fifteen cubits" (Genesis 7:20 · BSB).

### What the text shows

The ark is "300 cubits long, 50 cubits wide, and 30 cubits high" (6:15 · BSB); the Berean's note gives "approximately 450 feet long, 75 feet wide, and 45 feet high", with "lower, middle, and upper decks" (6:16 · BSB). Noah is to take "Two of every kind of bird and animal and crawling creature" (6:20 · BSB), and "seven pairs of every kind of clean animal" (7:2 · BSB). The words for the flood's reach are total: "all the high mountains under all the heavens were covered" (7:19 · BSB), and "every living thing on the face of the earth was destroyed" (7:23 · BSB). By the narrator's own dates it lasts more than a year, from "the seventeenth day of the second month" of Noah's six hundredth year (7:11 · BSB) until "the twenty-seventh day of the second month" of the next, when "the earth was fully dry" (8:14 · BSB). Jesus, Peter and Hebrews all speak of it as something that happened: "As it was in the days of Noah" (Matthew 24:37 · BSB).

### The views Christians hold

- **A flood over the whole globe.** "All the high mountains under all the heavens" means what it says, and God's promise, "Never again will all life be cut off by the waters of a flood" (9:11 · BSB), would make little sense of a local flood, since local floods still come. Held by Whitcomb and Morris in *The Genesis Flood* and by Answers in Genesis, which built a full-size ark at Williamstown, Kentucky (opened 2016). On the ark's room they answer that Noah took pairs of each *kind* (6:20), not of every variety.
- **A flood over the whole human world.** The waters covered the region where mankind lived and destroyed everyone outside the ark, but not the whole planet. "All the earth" can mean the world of the speaker: in Joseph's famine "every nation came to Joseph in Egypt to buy grain, because the famine was severe over all the earth" (41:57 · BSB). Held by Bernard Ramm (*The Christian View of Science and Scripture*, 1954) and by Hugh Ross. They read the promise of 9:11 as a promise that no flood will again cut off all life.

Both views hold that the flood happened, that it was God's judgment on an earth "full of violence" (6:11 · BSB), and that in the ark "a few people, only eight souls, were saved through water" (1 Peter 3:20 · BSB).

### Where the reading stops

The text gives the ark's size, the calendar and the reach of the waters in the narrator's own words. It does not say how the animals were fed, and it puts the flood on no modern map; the ark rests "on the mountains of Ararat" (8:4 · BSB), a range, not a named peak. The New Testament uses the flood as a warning. Jesus: people were "eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage, up to the day Noah entered the ark" (Matthew 24:38 · BSB). Peter: "the world of that time perished in the flood", and "the present heavens and earth are reserved for fire" (2 Peter 3:6–7 · BSB).

**Consider:** Hebrews says Noah built the ark "when warned about things not yet seen" (11:7 · BSB). What did building it ask of him, year after year?

**Reflect:** In Jesus' words, eating, drinking and marrying are not the sin; the people "were oblivious" (Matthew 24:39 · BSB). What are you most likely to be oblivious to?

## 07 Why was Canaan cursed?

**Main readings:** Genesis 9:6, 18–27; 10:6, 15–19; 15:16; Leviticus 18:24–25; Joshua 9:23; Ezekiel 18:20.

Ham sees; Shem and Japheth cover. Then Noah wakes and speaks a curse, not on Ham, but on Ham's son.

### What the text shows

"And Ham, the father of Canaan, saw his father's nakedness and told his two brothers outside" (9:22 · BSB). His brothers walk in backward with a garment, "Their faces were turned away" (9:23 · BSB). Then:

> he said, "Cursed be Canaan! A servant of servants shall he be to his brothers."

Genesis 9:25 · BSB

The narrator prepares for this twice, "And Ham was the father of Canaan" (9:18 · BSB), and the curse names Canaan three times (9:25–27). Genesis 10 then lists Canaan's sons, "Sidon his firstborn, and of the Hittites", the Jebusites, the Amorites and the rest, and draws his borders "from Sidon toward Gerar as far as Gaza" (10:15–19 · BSB): the peoples of the land Israel would one day enter. Abram is told that "the iniquity of the Amorites is not yet complete" (15:16 · BSB), and the law says of those nations that "the land will vomit out its inhabitants" (Leviticus 18:25 · BSB).

### The readings Christians give

- **Why Canaan, not Ham?** Some read Noah's words as a prophecy about peoples, looking ahead to the Canaanites' own sins and their later subjection; the Gibeonites, a Canaanite people, became "woodcutters and water carriers" (Joshua 9:23 · BSB). Some read it as Ham, who shamed his father, being answered through his own son. Others note that the text says Noah learned "what his youngest son had done to him" (9:24 · BSB) and ask whether Canaan himself had a part. The text does not settle which.
- **What did Ham do?** The text says he "saw" and "told". Most readers take that as the wrong: he exposed his father's shame where his brothers covered it. Others hear more in the phrase, because in the law's list of forbidden unions (Leviticus 18) uncovering nakedness is a Hebrew way of naming a sexual sin. The brothers' answer, covering without looking, fits the plain reading exactly.

### A reading this study refuses

For centuries some Christians claimed that this passage condemned Africans to slavery, calling it "the curse of Ham", and it was used to defend slavery in America (Stephen R. Haynes, *Noah's Curse: The Biblical Justification of American Slavery*, 2002). The text itself refutes it. The curse falls on Canaan, not on Ham. Canaan's descendants are the peoples of Canaan (10:15–19), not of Africa. The passage says nothing about skin. And guilt does not pass by birth: "A son will not bear the iniquity of his father" (Ezekiel 18:20 · BSB). The Southern Baptist Convention was formed in 1845 in a division over slavery; in 1995 it adopted a resolution on racial reconciliation repenting of that past. Its confession says that every person of every race "possesses full dignity and is worthy of respect and Christian love". Earlier in the same chapter, God said why: "for in His own image God has made mankind" (Genesis 9:6 · BSB).

**Consider:** Shem and Japheth "covered their father's nakedness" without looking (9:23 · BSB). What does their act show about honour that does not depend on the one honoured deserving it?

**Reflect:** Has a reading of Scripture you inherited ever served your own side? How would you test it against the text?

## 08 The table of nations and the tower of Babel

**Main readings:** Genesis 10:1, 5, 20, 25, 31–32; 11:1–9; 12:1–3; Acts 2:5–11; 17:26; Revelation 7:9.

Genesis 10 spreads the nations across the map "according to their languages" (10:5 · BSB). Genesis 11 begins, "Now the whole world had one language" (11:1 · BSB). Which came first?

### What the text shows

Genesis 10 is a family tree of peoples, closing, "From these the nations of the earth spread out after the flood" (10:32 · BSB). It already speaks of languages, three times (10:5, 20, 31). Then chapter 11 tells of people who settle "in the land of Shinar", which the Berean notes is "Babylonia" (11:2), and say:

> "Come," they said, "let us build for ourselves a city with a tower that reaches to the heavens, that we may make a name for ourselves and not be scattered over the face of all the earth."

Genesis 11:4 · BSB

The tower that "reaches to the heavens" is so small that "the LORD came down to see" it (11:5 · BSB). What they feared is what happens: "the LORD scattered them from there over the face of all the earth" (11:8 · BSB). The Berean notes that Babel can be read "Babylon" and that the name "sounds like the Hebrew for confused" (11:9).

### The readings Christians give

- **The order.** Most readers take chapter 10 as the wide view, running ahead to the nations, and chapter 11 as the close view, stepping back to tell how the scattering began; Genesis does the same when chapter 2 returns to the making of man in chapter 1. The note on Peleg, "in his days the earth was divided" (10:25 · BSB), is read by some as a pointer to Babel and by others as a division of land or peoples.
- **How far the languages reach.** The traditional reading takes Babel as the beginning of the world's languages. Other readers take "the whole world" (11:1) as the world of the story, the peoples gathered in Shinar, and the event as the start of the scattering rather than the source of every tongue.
- **The tower.** The baked brick and tar of 11:3 are the building materials of Mesopotamia, and many readers connect the tower with the stepped temple towers of Babylonia. That is background brought to the text, not something the text says.

### Where Scripture takes it

The very next chapter answers the builders. They wanted to "make a name for ourselves" (11:4); God tells Abram, "I will make your name great", so that "all the families of the earth will be blessed through you" (12:2–3 · BSB). Paul preaches that God "determined their appointed times and the boundaries of their lands" (Acts 17:26 · BSB). At Pentecost each listener hears the apostles "in his own native language" (Acts 2:8 · BSB). Many readers see Babel answered there, though Acts does not name Babel, so that link is a reading. At the end John sees "a multitude too large to count, from every nation and tribe and people and tongue" (Revelation 7:9 · BSB). The languages are not undone. They are gathered.

**Consider:** In Genesis 11 people try to make a name and stay together; in Genesis 12 God gives a name and sends a man out. What does that contrast teach about where a lasting name comes from?

**Reflect:** Where are you building something to make a name for yourself?

## Read Genesis 1–11 again

You came to these chapters with questions, and they are worth asking. On some of them the text speaks plainly: God made the heavens and the earth; Adam was a real man; the flood was a real judgment; every nation comes from one man. On others it is silent, and Christians who hold the whole Bible as true read it differently. Ask the three questions again on each: what the text says and leaves unsaid, where the rest of Scripture takes it up, and which part is a reading. Hold the readings with an open hand, and what the text plainly says with a closed one.

Then notice the question these chapters answer that you may not have brought. After the murder, the flood and the tower, will God give up on the world He made? Genesis 11 ends with the nations scattered. Genesis 12 begins with God calling one man, so that "all the families of the earth will be blessed through you" (12:3 · BSB). Paul calls that promise the gospel told beforehand: "The Scripture foresaw that God would justify the Gentiles by faith, and foretold the gospel to Abraham" (Galatians 3:8 · BSB).

**Your question:** Of the questions you brought to Genesis 1–11, has any one of them become a reason to keep God at a distance? What would change if you heard these chapters as His answer to whether He will give up on you?
