# Passage notes — Women of the Bible

Study 46 · People of Scripture · 2026-11-05 · First content draft. Written as step 9 of the
[next-20 sequence](../../../../../Pages/Topics/NEXT_20_DRAFTS.md).

**Title and scope.** Set by the step's own brief on 2026-11-05 after the
[redundancy review](../../../../../Pages/Topics/REDUNDANCY_REVIEW.md), which replaces the sequence table's row. No study
about women exists on the site; the [People page](../../../../../Pages/Study/people-and-genealogies/README.md) (/study/people, the built page, not the cut draft 36) lists people and
the [Topics card on women](../../../../../Pages/Topics/people-of-the-bible/women-of-the-bible/README.md)
(/topics/c/people-women, 122 topics) gathers passages, but neither reads the stories. This study reads sixteen, named by
the brief: Sarah, Hagar, Miriam, Rahab, Deborah, Ruth, Hannah, Abigail, Huldah, Esther, Mary the mother of Jesus, Mary
Magdalene, Martha and Mary of Bethany, Lydia, Priscilla and Phoebe. The brief adds Rahab, Huldah, and Martha and Mary
to the table's list. **Draft 36 "People and genealogies" has been cut** from the plan, so this study does not build on
it or link to it.

**Doctrine set by the brief.** The site's own voice keeps within the Baptist Faith and Message 2000 (the Southern
Baptist Convention's statement of faith): men and women equal in worth as bearers of God's image (Article III), the
office of pastor limited to men as qualified by Scripture (Article VI), and the husband's servant leadership in the home
(Article XVIII). The women's faith, courage and service are shown as the text gives them, including Deborah's judging,
Huldah's prophesying and Phoebe's and Priscilla's service, without using them to argue for women pastors. Where the
line is debated, the site's view is stated plainly and other views are labelled as other traditions'. One question on
which Baptists themselves differ, whether women may serve as deacons, is shown with both views and not decided
(rule 2 of the brief: "Where Baptists differ among themselves, show the views and do not choose").

**Scripture source.** Every Bible quotation in [content.md](content.md) is from the Berean Standard Bible, copied from
the site's own text in [data/text/bsb/](../../../../data/text/bsb/) and checked by script: 93 quoted pieces on lines that
carry a "· BSB" reference (block quotations and inline quotations) were matched word for word, quotation marks and
punctuation ignored, against the verses cited on the same line. The 18 the script did not match are all expected: the
Baptist Faith and Message phrases, the quotations from Criswell, Henry and Calvin, two sermon titles, the Berean's own
footnote "Or their wives", and three short echoes whose verse sits elsewhere, checked by hand: "the prophetess" (Exodus
15:20), "God my Savior" (Luke 1:47), "sinful woman" (Luke 7:37). Echoes on lines without a reference were checked by hand
too: "harsh and evil" (1 Samuel 25:3), "from another place" (Esther 4:14), "tender" (2 Kings 22:19), "Martha served"
(John 12:2), "the husband of but one wife" (1 Timothy 3:2), "Or deaconess" (the Berean's note on Romans 16:1). The check
proves the words are the Berean's; it does not prove each reference is the best verse or that the reading is right. The
check script is kept outside the repository, in this job's own folder.

**The way of reading taught.** Three questions for every life: what does the text say she did, and what does it leave
unsaid; what is God doing; how does later Scripture remember her? They come from the material itself. Several of these
women are remembered by later Scripture (Sarah in Hebrews 11:11 and 1 Peter 3:6; Rahab in Hebrews 11:31, James 2:25 and
Matthew 1:5; Miriam in Micah 6:4; Ruth in Matthew 1:5; Hannah's song echoed in Luke 1:46–55; Mary of Bethany in Mark
14:9), which meets the study standard's rule that later Scripture's memory of a subject is shown. The second question
keeps the stories from becoming stories of heroines only; Hannah's and Mary's songs are about God.

**Shape.** The women are read in the Bible's own order, two or three to a part where the text sets them near each other
(one household; one generation; one office of prophet; the same Gospel scenes; the same mission in Acts), each with a hook,
a main reading, explanation with its limits ("Where the passage stops"), a Consider and a Reflect question. One question
section answers whether these women show that women may be pastors, then the close.

## Context, readings and limits

**Opening (Mark 14; John 12).** Mark places the anointing at the home of Simon the Leper, the woman unnamed, the perfume
poured on Jesus' head (Mark 14:3); John places a dinner in Bethany six days before the Passover, with Martha serving and
Mary anointing His feet (John 12:1–3). Most readers take them as one event told from two angles; some see two anointings.
The draft says "most readers take it to be the same one" and makes nothing hang on it. Luke 7:36–50 is a different
anointing, by "a sinful woman" in a Pharisee's house, and is kept apart (part 07).

**01 Sarah and Hagar.** Sarah's plan (Genesis 16:2) and the contempt and harshness that followed (16:4–6) are told plainly,
not excused. "Then a slave woman did something rare in Scripture: she gave the LORD a name" is worded with care: Hagar's
"You are the God who sees me" (16:13) is often called the only time in Scripture a person gives God a name; the draft says
"rare" rather than claim a count it has not made. 1 Peter 3:6 is quoted only for what it says of Sarah; its teaching on
wives belongs to Study 43. Galatians 4:21–31, Paul's use of the two women, is named and set aside.

**02 Miriam and Rahab.** Exodus 2:4 does not name the sister; Numbers 26:59 says Moses had one sister, Miriam, so the draft
says "very likely". Miriam "sang back" (Exodus 15:21) to the song Moses and Israel sang (15:1); the hook says she led the
women, not that she wrote Israel's first song. Numbers 12 is summarised (rebuke, skin disease, Moses' prayer), not quoted
at length. Rahab's lie (Joshua 2:4–5) is named and not excused; Hebrews 11:31 and James 2:25 praise her faith and her
welcome, not the lie. The draft does not enter the long ethical debate over the lie.

**03 Deborah and Huldah.** The most sensitive part. What the text says is stated without shrinking it: Deborah "was
judging Israel" and Israel came to her "for judgment" (Judges 4:4–5); she gave God's command to Barak (4:6); she went with
him (4:9); her song credits God (5:1–31). Huldah, a prophetess, was consulted by the high priest and the king's officials
(2 Kings 22:14), and gave God's word to the king. What the text does not say is also stated: neither was a priest, and
neither was an elder or pastor of a church. The draft does not say God raised Deborah "because men failed" in its own
voice; it gives that as one reading with a named holder (Criswell) and the narrower reading beside it, and says the text
does not give God's reason. Matthew Henry, on Judges 4, held that Deborah judged "not as a princess, by an civil authority
conferred upon her, but as a prophetess, and as God's mouth to them" (sic, "an civil" in the source); read and not used in
the reader's text, as it would add a third reading the part does not need.

**04 Ruth, Hannah and Abigail.** Ruth 1:16 is the anchor; the redemption law behind Ruth 3–4 is not explained (the
[farming year study](../../../../../Pages/Study/the-farming-year/README.md) covers gleaning; the kinsman-redeemer belongs
to a study of Ruth or of the law). Hannah's song (1 Samuel 2:1–10) is echoed in Mary's (Luke 1:46–55); the draft says
"echoes" without listing the parallels. **Abigail and Article XVIII**: Abigail acted "But she did not tell her husband
Nabal" (1 Samuel 25:19), and David blesses her and the LORD who sent her (25:32–33). The draft says what the text says,
then limits it: a household facing slaughter because of a man Scripture calls "harsh and evil" is not the ordinary case
the confession's "servant leadership" describes. That limit is marked as this site's reading.

**05 Esther.** The book does not name God; the draft says so and points to what the narrative shows (a sleepless king,
6:1; Haman hanged on his own gallows, 7:10). Mordecai's "from another place" (4:14) is read as confidence in deliverance
without a promise of Esther's success. Whether "another place" hints at God is debated; the draft does not press it.
Esther's marriage to a pagan king and the violence of chapter 9 are not taken up (see open questions).

**06 Mary the mother of Jesus.** The Gospels' scenes are read in order. The Chalcedonian Definition (451) is quoted from
the site's own creeds page: "of Mary the Virgin, the God-bearer". Its point is Christ's single person. Catholic and
Orthodox teaching on Mary is named in general terms only (her sinlessness; prayers asking her intercession) and labelled
as theirs; no Catholic or Orthodox document was read for this draft, so none is quoted and no dogma is named by date
(see open questions). Baptist position stated from Luke 1:47 and 1 Timothy 2:5.

**07 Mary Magdalene, Martha and Mary.** Luke 8:2–3 is the only Gospel passage that tells her past ("seven demons"). The
prostitute legend is denied only as far as the text goes: "Nothing in the Gospels says Mary Magdalene had been a
prostitute", and the idea came "much later, by joining her to the unnamed 'sinful woman' of Luke 7:37". The usual
attribution of that joining to a sermon of Gregory the Great (591) was **not checked** in any source and is not in the
reader's text. Mark 16:9 ("He appeared first to Mary Magdalene") stands in the longer ending of Mark, which many
manuscripts lack; the draft uses John 20 instead, so nothing rests on it. "To sit at a teacher's feet was the place of a
disciple" is a reading (compare Acts 22:3, where Paul was "educated at the feet of Gamaliel", not quoted in the reader's text); it is widely held and
stated briefly. Henry's "apostle to the apostles" is glossed so a reader does not take it as a claim she was one of the
Twelve.

**08 Lydia, Priscilla and Phoebe.** Acts 16:14 is read for who acts ("The Lord opened her heart"). "Her home was where the
believers of Philippi gathered" rests on Acts 16:40. Priscilla's name before Aquila's: Acts 18:18, 18:26 and Romans 16:3
in the Berean (Prisca in Romans and 1 Corinthians); Acts 18:2 and 1 Corinthians 16:19 put Aquila first. The draft says
"often comes first" and draws nothing from it; John Piper (1984, read, not quoted) called the order "a slender thread" for
arguing that she held a teaching office. Phoebe: the Berean's "servant" with its note "Or deaconess"; Calvin's comment
that Paul "gave this Epistle to be brought" by her is the ground for "took her to be the one who carried this letter".

**Question section.** The site's view, its grounds (1 Timothy 2:12–13; 3:2) and the honouring of women's prophesying and
service (Acts 2:17; 1 Corinthians 11:5, not quoted). Study 28 (The church and its calling) already answers who may be a
pastor and how 1 Corinthians 14:34 fits 1 Corinthians 11:5; this study states the line and links there rather than repeat
the argument. The other traditions' view is stated in general terms (see open questions).

## Doctrine

- **Article III (man):** made in God's image, every person of "full dignity". The ground for "equal in worth" in the
  opening and the close.
- **Article VI (the church):** "the office of pastor is limited to men as qualified by Scripture", and "both men and
  women are gifted for service in the church". Stated in the site's voice in the question section; part 03 says Deborah
  and Huldah were prophets, not pastors; part 08 says Priscilla taught with her husband in their home and Phoebe served.
  Nowhere in the site's voice is a woman presented as a pastor.
- **Article VI on deacons:** the confession names deacons as an office and does not say whether women may hold it.
  Baptists differ; both views shown, not decided.
- **Article XVIII (the family):** quoted in the question section ("In the home") and used for the limit on Abigail.
- **Mary, and Catholic and Orthodox teaching about her:** the confession has no article on Mary; the Baptist position comes
  from Scripture (Luke 1:47; 1 Timothy 2:5) and the confession's teaching that salvation is through Christ alone
  (Article IV).
- **The convention today:** the doctrinal foundation records that in June 2026 the "Truth and Unity" amendment (on the
  office or function of pastor) won a first two-thirds vote and needs a second in 2027. Not mentioned in the reader's
  text; nothing in the draft depends on it, and the draft's line is the confession's own.

## Open editorial questions

1. **Length.** About 5,050 words raw (headings, references and block quotations included), well above the 2,000–2,600
   aim. The brief names sixteen women, and each needs her story, its limits and two questions; at the aim's length each
   would get about 150 words. Recommendation for the design pass: give each woman her own card (sixteen cards in eight
   pairs or trios), so the page stays compact while the text stays whole. First cuts if words must go: the "How
   Christians have honoured her" section of part 06; the Esther "Where the passage stops" section; the Criswell line on
   Deborah's leadership.
2. **Other traditions in their own words.** The view that women may be pastors is stated fairly but in general terms,
   with no holder quoted. Before publishing, read and quote one statement in its holders' words (the Assemblies of God
   position paper, a Christians for Biblical Equality statement, or the Mickelsens' 1984 article), or confirm the owner
   is content with the view stated without a named holder.
3. **A named holder for the narrower reading of Barak.** The view that Judges 4:9 is only a rebuke of Barak's fear is
   given without a named source. Henry (on Judges 4) reads it as a hint to Barak not to insist, which is close; quote him
   or another commentator, or drop the second reading.
4. **Catholic and Orthodox teaching on Mary.** Named in general terms. If the owner wants dogmas named (the Immaculate
   Conception, defined in 1854; the Assumption, 1950), read the documents or the Catechism of the Catholic Church first.
5. **Women deacons.** Both Baptist views are shown with named holders. A Southern Baptist voice for the second view
   (Piper's church is in Converge, not the SBC) would balance it; none was searched for in the time given.
6. **Esther's harder questions.** Her marriage to a pagan king and the fighting in Esther 9 are not discussed. A reader
   may ask; a short note could be added.
7. **Women who are not here.** Eve, Rebekah, Rachel, Leah, Tamar, Bathsheba, Jael, the widow of Zarephath, Elizabeth,
   Anna, the Samaritan woman and Dorcas are among those not read. The opening says this is a selection; the design pass
   may want a line naming where to find the rest (the Topics card).
8. **Mary Magdalene and the prostitute legend.** The draft denies it only from the text. If the page names where the
   idea came from (commonly said to be Gregory the Great, 591), that source must be read first.
