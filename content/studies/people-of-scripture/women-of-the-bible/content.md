# Women of the Bible

**Study 46 · People of Scripture · First content draft · 2026-11-05**

A woman broke a jar of perfume over Jesus at a dinner in Bethany. Some at the table called it waste. Jesus said, "truly I tell you, wherever the gospel is preached in all the world, what she has done will also be told in memory of her" (Mark 14:9 · BSB). Mark does not give her name. John tells of an anointing at a dinner in Bethany, and most readers take it to be the same one: "Then Mary took about a pint of expensive perfume, made of pure nard, and she anointed Jesus' feet" (John 12:3 · BSB). Jesus Himself wanted a woman's act of devotion remembered.

This study reads sixteen women's stories from the text: Sarah, Hagar, Miriam, Rahab, Deborah, Ruth, Hannah, Abigail, Huldah, Esther, Mary the mother of Jesus, Mary Magdalene, Martha and her sister Mary, Lydia, Priscilla and Phoebe. It is a selection, not a census. The [Topics card on women of the Bible](/topics/c/people-women) lists 122 topics and the [People page](/study/people) lists everyone; this study reads the stories.

The site writes in agreement with the Baptist Faith and Message 2000, the statement of faith of the Southern Baptist Convention. Two of its lines matter here. Men and women are equal in worth, because "God created man in His own image" (Article III), and "male and female He created them" (Genesis 1:27 · BSB). And "While both men and women are gifted for service in the church, the office of pastor is limited to men as qualified by Scripture" (Article VI). This study shows what these women did as the text gives it. Whether their stories mean women may be pastors is taken up near the end, with the site's view stated plainly and other traditions' views labelled as theirs.

Carry three questions through every story:

1. **What does the text say she did, and what does it leave unsaid?** Read her story before reading anything into it.
2. **What is God doing?** These are not only stories of great women. They are stories of a great God, as Hannah's song says.
3. **How does later Scripture remember her?** Hebrews, Micah, Matthew and James all look back on women in this study; the way Scripture remembers someone is its own reading of her life.

## 01 Sarah and Hagar: the God who keeps His word, and the God who sees

**Main readings:** Genesis 16:1–13; 18:9–15; 21:1–21; Hebrews 11:11; 1 Peter 3:6.

Two women in one household, one barren and one pregnant, and neither of them treated well by the other.

### Sarah: laughter, then a son

God had promised Abraham a son. Years passed. Sarah, impatient, gave her servant Hagar to Abraham (Genesis 16:2), and when Hagar conceived, the house filled with contempt and harshness. Later, at ninety, Sarah overheard the promise again and "laughed to herself" (Genesis 18:12 · BSB). The LORD answered:

> Is anything too difficult for the LORD? At the appointed time I will return to you—in about a year—and Sarah will have a son.

Genesis 18:14 · BSB

"Now the LORD attended to Sarah as He had said, and the LORD did for Sarah what He had promised" (Genesis 21:1 · BSB). The text does not tidy up her doubt. Yet Hebrews remembers her this way: "By faith Sarah, even though she was barren and beyond the proper age, was enabled to conceive a child, because she considered Him faithful who had promised" (Hebrews 11:11 · BSB). Peter remembers her too, as a wife who "obeyed Abraham" and as a mother to women who "refuse to give way to fear" (1 Peter 3:6 · BSB).

### Hagar: the woman who named the LORD

Hagar was an Egyptian servant with no say in what was done to her. She ran into the desert, and "the angel of the LORD found Hagar by a spring of water" (Genesis 16:7 · BSB). He called her by name, told her to return, and promised her a son, "for the LORD has heard your cry of affliction" (Genesis 16:11 · BSB). Then a slave woman did something rare in Scripture: she gave the LORD a name.

> So Hagar gave this name to the LORD who had spoken to her: "You are the God who sees me," for she said, "Here I have seen the One who sees me!"

Genesis 16:13 · BSB

Years later, sent away with her boy and out of water, she heard again, "Do not be afraid, for God has heard the voice of the boy" (Genesis 21:17 · BSB), and "God opened her eyes, and she saw a well of water" (Genesis 21:19 · BSB).

### Where the passage stops

The promise ran through Sarah's son Isaac, not Hagar's son Ishmael (Genesis 21:12). The story does not pretend the household was just; it shows God keeping His covenant through a flawed family, and seeing the woman everyone else used. (Paul's use of the two women in Galatians 4:21–31 makes a different point and is not read here.)

**Consider:** Sarah laughed and Hagar ran. What did God do for each?

**Reflect:** Is there a place in your life where you feel unseen? What would it mean to pray Hagar's words there?

## 02 Miriam and Rahab: a song by the sea, a cord in the window

**Main readings:** Exodus 2:4–8; 15:20–21; Numbers 12:1–15; Micah 6:4; Joshua 2:1–21; 6:25; Hebrews 11:31; James 2:25; Matthew 1:5.

One woman led Israel's women in praise by the sea. The other was a Canaanite who hid Israel's spies.

### Miriam: a prophetess, and a warning

Miriam was very likely the sister who watched the baby Moses in the reeds (Exodus 2:4; the text there does not name her). After the sea closed over Pharaoh's army:

> Then Miriam the prophetess, Aaron's sister, took a tambourine in her hand, and all the women followed her with tambourines and dancing. And Miriam sang back to them: "Sing to the LORD, for He is highly exalted; the horse and rider He has thrown into the sea."

Exodus 15:20–21 · BSB

Scripture calls her "the prophetess" and remembers her among Israel's leaders: "I sent Moses before you, as well as Aaron and Miriam" (Micah 6:4 · BSB). It also remembers her failure. She and Aaron "criticized Moses" and said, "Does the LORD speak only through Moses?" (Numbers 12:1–2 · BSB). The LORD rebuked them, Miriam was struck with skin disease, and Moses prayed for her healing. Scripture keeps both: her song and her sin.

### Rahab: faith from outside

Joshua, Hebrews and James all call Rahab of Jericho "the prostitute". Scripture does not hide it, and it does not leave her there. She hid two Israelite spies and told them why:

> When we heard this, our hearts melted and everyone's courage failed because of you, for the LORD your God is God in the heavens above and on the earth below.

Joshua 2:11 · BSB

She tied a scarlet cord in her window, and when the city fell, "Joshua spared Rahab the prostitute, with her father's household" (Joshua 6:25 · BSB). Later Scripture remembers her three times. Hebrews names her faith: "By faith the prostitute Rahab, because she welcomed the spies in peace, did not perish" (Hebrews 11:31 · BSB). James names her actions (James 2:25). And Matthew writes her into the family line of Jesus: "Salmon was the father of Boaz by Rahab" (Matthew 1:5 · BSB).

### Where the passage stops

Rahab lied to the king's men to protect the spies (Joshua 2:4–5). Hebrews and James praise her faith and her welcome of the spies; neither praises the lie, and the story is not a lesson in when lying is allowed.

**Consider:** Miriam was an insider who fell; Rahab was an outsider who was brought in. What does each story say about God's grace?

**Reflect:** Do you think your past keeps you out of God's family? Read Matthew 1:5 again.

## 03 Deborah and Huldah: when God's word came through a woman

**Main readings:** Judges 4:1–24; 5:1–31; 2 Kings 22:8–20.

Israel had no king, and life in its villages had stopped. A woman sat under a palm tree, and Israel came to her.

### Deborah: prophetess and judge

> Now Deborah, a prophetess, the wife of Lappidoth, was judging Israel at that time. And she would sit under the Palm of Deborah between Ramah and Bethel in the hill country of Ephraim, where the Israelites would go up to her for judgment.

Judges 4:4–5 · BSB

The judges of this book were the leaders God raised up between Joshua and the kings: some rescued Israel in battle, some settled its disputes. Deborah settled disputes and called the battle, though the text never puts a sword in her hand. She delivered God's command to Barak: "Go and march to Mount Tabor" (Judges 4:6 · BSB). He answered, "If you will go with me, I will go; but if you will not go with me, I will not go" (Judges 4:8 · BSB). She went, and told him, "the road you are taking will bring you no honor, because the LORD will be selling Sisera into the hand of a woman" (Judges 4:9 · BSB). That woman turned out to be Jael, not Deborah (Judges 4:21; 5:24). Deborah's own song gives the credit to God and calls herself "a mother in Israel" (Judges 5:7 · BSB). W. A. Criswell, for many years pastor of First Baptist Church in Dallas, called her "an unusual leader, with great faith and tremendous heart and spirit" (sermon "By Faith Deborah", 1960).

### Huldah: the word of God for a king

Centuries later, a lost book of the law was found in the temple. King Josiah heard it read, tore his clothes, and sent his officials to ask the LORD. They went to "Huldah the prophetess" (2 Kings 22:14 · BSB). She answered with God's word in two halves: judgment was coming on Jerusalem, "according to all the words of the book that the king of Judah has read" (2 Kings 22:16 · BSB), but the king would be spared seeing it, "because your heart was tender and you humbled yourself before the LORD" (2 Kings 22:19 · BSB). The king's reform followed. Matthew Henry, the English Presbyterian commentator of the early 1700s, noted that "her being a wife was no prejudice at all to her being a prophetess" (on 2 Kings 22:14). (Study 38, Prophets in their times, reads her answer among the prophets.)

### What the text gives them, and what it does not

The text gives Deborah and Huldah real callings from God. Both are called prophetesses: people through whom God spoke His word. The high priest went to Huldah with the king's officials. Israel went up to Deborah for judgment. The site honours that as the text does, without shrinking it.

The text does not make either of them a priest; in Israel that office belonged to the men of Aaron's family. And neither was a pastor of a church, the office the New Testament later describes for local congregations (1 Timothy 3:1–7; Titus 1:5–9). How their callings bear on that office is the question taken up near the end.

[open] Some readers take Barak's hesitation, and Deborah's "no honor" for him, as a sign that Israel's men had failed to lead and God raised up a woman in their place. Criswell preached it that way: "If a man cannot be found, if a man fails, then seek out a woman; a consecrated, godly woman that can pray and that knows the Lord" (1960). Others read it more narrowly, as a rebuke of one man's fear. The text says Barak would lose the honour; it does not say why God chose Deborah.

**Consider:** What did Deborah and Huldah each say that began with God's word rather than their own?

**Reflect:** Josiah's heart was "tender" when he heard God's word. How does your heart respond when Scripture rebukes you?

## 04 Ruth, Hannah and Abigail: faith in the dark days

**Main readings:** Ruth 1:1–22; 2:1–12; 3:11; 4:13–17; 1 Samuel 1:1–2:10; 25:2–42.

Three women, in the days of the judges and of Saul, who trusted God when the men around them were failing or gone.

### Ruth: "your God will be my God"

Ruth was a widow from Moab, a people outside Israel. Her mother-in-law Naomi, widowed too, told her to go home. Ruth refused:

> For wherever you go, I will go, and wherever you live, I will live; your people will be my people, and your God will be my God.

Ruth 1:16 · BSB

She gleaned barley in a stranger's field to feed them both. Boaz, the owner, blessed her as one who had come to the God of Israel, "under whose wings you have taken refuge" (Ruth 2:12 · BSB), and later told her, "all my fellow townspeople know that you are a woman of noble character" (Ruth 3:11 · BSB). The book ends with a child and a family line: "they named him Obed. He became the father of Jesse, the father of David" (Ruth 4:17 · BSB). Matthew names her in Jesus' line too (Matthew 1:5).

### Hannah: a prayer poured out

Hannah was barren, and her rival mocked her for years. At the house of God at Shiloh, "In her bitter distress, Hannah prayed to the LORD and wept with many tears" (1 Samuel 1:10 · BSB). The priest Eli thought she was drunk. She said, "I have poured out my soul before the LORD" (1 Samuel 1:15 · BSB). God gave her a son, Samuel, and she gave him back to serve the LORD, as she had vowed. Then she sang:

> He raises the poor from the dust and lifts the needy from the ash heap. He seats them among princes and bestows on them a throne of honor.

1 Samuel 2:8 · BSB

Her song is about God, not about herself. Mary's song in Luke 1 echoes it.

### Abigail: discernment that stopped a massacre

Abigail was "an intelligent and beautiful woman, but her husband, a Calebite, was harsh and evil in his dealings" (1 Samuel 25:3 · BSB). Nabal insulted David, and David set out with four hundred men to kill every male of Nabal's house. Abigail went to meet him with food and words that reminded David of God's promise to him. David answered:

> Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, who sent you to meet me this day! Blessed is your discernment, and blessed are you, because today you kept me from bloodshed and from avenging myself by my own hand.

1 Samuel 25:32–33 · BSB

### Where the passage stops

Abigail acted without telling her husband (1 Samuel 25:19), and the text praises her for it. The danger was a massacre of her household, brought on by a man Scripture calls "harsh and evil". It is a story about saving lives and keeping David from sin, not a pattern for how a wife and a good husband settle ordinary disagreements; the Baptist Faith and Message asks a wife to submit to her husband's "servant leadership" (Article XVIII), which is not what Nabal gave (this site's reading). Study 43, on marriage, sexuality and singleness, reads marriage itself.

**Consider:** Ruth, Hannah and Abigail each say something about the LORD. Find it in each story. What did each believe about Him?

**Reflect:** Hannah poured out her soul; Ruth committed herself; Abigail spoke up wisely. Which do you most need to do this week?

## 05 Esther: for such a time as this

**Main readings:** Esther 2:5–17; 4:1–17; 5:1–8; 7:1–6; 9:20–32.

The book of Esther never names God. Yet nothing in it happens by accident.

A Jewish orphan in Persia was made queen, keeping her people hidden on her cousin Mordecai's advice. Then a royal decree was signed to destroy every Jew in the empire. To go to the king unsummoned was to risk death. Mordecai sent her word:

> For if you remain silent at this time, relief and deliverance for the Jews will arise from another place, but you and your father's house will perish. And who knows if perhaps you have come to the kingdom for such a time as this?

Esther 4:14 · BSB

She asked her people to fast for three days, and said, "After that, I will go to the king, even though it is against the law. And if I perish, I perish!" (Esther 4:16 · BSB). She went, was received, and exposed the plot at a banquet. The Jews were saved, and the feast of Purim was set so that "the memory of them" would not fade (Esther 9:28 · BSB); Jewish families keep it still.

### Where the passage stops

Mordecai does not promise Esther success. He says deliverance will come "from another place" if she stays silent: God's purpose does not depend on her, yet she is called to act within it. The book does not say God sent her dreams or spoke to her. It shows His hand in timing, a sleepless king and a gallows turned around (Esther 6:1; 7:10), and leaves the reader to see it.

**Consider:** Where in Esther do you see God at work, though He is never named?

**Reflect:** Where might God have placed you "for such a time as this"? What would it cost you to speak?

## 06 Mary, the mother of Jesus: the Lord's servant

**Main readings:** Luke 1:26–56; 2:19; John 2:1–11; 19:25–27; Acts 1:14.

An angel greeted a young woman in Nazareth, and she asked how. Then she said yes.

Gabriel told Mary she would bear the Son of God. Her answer is the whole of her faith in a sentence:

> "I am the Lord's servant," Mary answered. "May it happen to me according to your word."

Luke 1:38 · BSB

Her song that follows, often called the Magnificat from its first word in Latin, echoes Hannah's (part 04): "My soul magnifies the Lord, and my spirit rejoices in God my Savior!" (Luke 1:46–47 · BSB). She foresaw, "From now on all generations will call me blessed" (Luke 1:48 · BSB).

The Gospels show her rarely after that, and each time well. She "treasured up all these things and pondered them in her heart" (Luke 2:19 · BSB). At Cana she told the servants, "Do whatever He tells you" (John 2:5 · BSB). She stood "Near the cross of Jesus" (John 19:25 · BSB), where He gave her into the care of the disciple He loved. Her last appearance is among the praying believers before Pentecost, "along with the women and Mary the mother of Jesus, and with His brothers" (Acts 1:14 · BSB).

### How Christians have honoured her

The Council of Chalcedon in 451, whose statement about Christ Baptists share in substance, called her "Mary the Virgin, the God-bearer". A council was a gathering of church leaders called to settle a disputed teaching. The title was a claim about her Son: the child she bore was God the Son made man, one person. Baptists gladly call her blessed, as she said all generations would: "From now on all generations will call me blessed" (Luke 1:48 · BSB).

The Roman Catholic and Orthodox churches go further than Baptists. They do not go further in the same way, and their teachings should not be run together.

- **The Roman Catholic Church** teaches the Immaculate Conception as a dogma, a teaching every Catholic is bound to believe. The name is often mistaken for the virgin birth of Jesus, but it is about Mary's own conception in her mother's womb. Pope Pius IX defined it in 1854, in the document *Ineffabilis Deus*. It says that Mary, "in the first instance of her conception, by a singular grace and privilege granted by Almighty God, in view of the merits of Jesus Christ, the Savior of the human race, was preserved free from all stain of original sin". Original sin is the sin and its guilt that pass from Adam to every human being. This is the Catholic Church's teaching, in its own words.
- **The Orthodox churches** do not accept that dogma. They honour Mary as the Theotokos, the Greek for "God-bearer", and call her the Panagia, "the all-holy". Their liturgy, the set form of their worship, praises her as "more honourable than the cherubim" [check: the wording of this hymn in a standard English translation of the Liturgy of St John Chrysostom]. Orthodox teachers commonly explain their rejection of the Immaculate Conception by a different account of what every person inherits from Adam. They hold that Mary was made holy by God's grace and by her own free response to it, and was not preserved from original sin at her conception [check: a named Orthodox source that states this, quoted in its own words]. So "all-holy" is the Orthodox way of honouring her. It is not the Catholic dogma under another name.

Both churches also ask Mary to pray for them. Those are their teachings. This site holds that Mary was a believer who needed a Saviour, as she herself sang: "my spirit rejoices in God my Savior!" (Luke 1:47 · BSB). It also holds that "there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5 · BSB). A mediator is one who stands between two parties to bring them together.

## 07 Mary Magdalene, Martha and Mary: women who followed Jesus

**Main readings:** Luke 8:1–3; 10:38–42; John 11:25–27; 12:1–8; 19:25; 20:1–18.

The men had fled. The women stood at the cross, and came first to the tomb.

### Women on the road with Him

Luke tells us who travelled with Jesus: the Twelve, "as well as some women who had been healed of evil spirits and infirmities: Mary called Magdalene, from whom seven demons had gone out", with others, and "These women were ministering to them out of their own means" (Luke 8:2–3 · BSB). They gave money and care to Jesus' work. Nothing in the Gospels says Mary Magdalene had been a prostitute. That idea came much later, by joining her to the unnamed "sinful woman" of Luke 7:37, and the text does not support it.

### Martha and Mary of Bethany

When Jesus came to their house, Mary "sat at the Lord's feet listening to His message" while Martha "was distracted by all the preparations" (Luke 10:39–40 · BSB). Jesus said, "Mary has chosen the good portion, and it will not be taken away from her" (Luke 10:42 · BSB). To sit at a teacher's feet was the place of a disciple, and Jesus defended her place there.

Martha is not only the busy sister. When her brother Lazarus died, she went out to meet Jesus, and He spoke to her and she answered with one of the great confessions of the Gospels:

> Jesus said to her, "I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in Me will live, even though he dies. And everyone who lives and believes in Me will never die. Do you believe this?" "Yes, Lord," she answered, "I believe that You are the Christ, the Son of God, who was to come into the world."

John 11:25–27 · BSB

"The Christ" is the Greek form of "Messiah", the King God had promised to send. Read more in *The signs and works of Jesus*.

At a dinner in Bethany later, "Martha served", and Mary anointed Jesus' feet (John 12:2–3 · BSB): the act this study began with.

### The first witness of the risen Lord

"Early on the first day of the week, while it was still dark, Mary Magdalene went to the tomb" (John 20:1 · BSB). She thought the body had been taken, and wept. Then:

> Jesus said to her, "Mary." She turned and said to Him in Hebrew, "Rabboni!" (which means "Teacher").

John 20:16 · BSB

He sent her: "go and tell My brothers" (John 20:17 · BSB). "Mary Magdalene went and announced to the disciples, 'I have seen the Lord!'" (John 20:18 · BSB). Luke adds that when the women told the apostles, "they did not believe the women" (Luke 24:11 · BSB). The risen Lord showed Himself first to a woman. Matthew Henry wrote that "she becomes an apostle to the apostles" (on John 20:17), meaning one sent with a message to them, not one of the Twelve. Criswell marvelled at the same thing: "Mary of Magdala, to whom the Lord first appeared, raised from the dead – to a woman, to a woman" (sermon "Woman's Work in the Church", 1984).

**Consider:** What did each of these women do with Jesus: give, listen, confess, anoint, tell?

**Reflect:** Mary knew Jesus when He said her name. When did you last hear His word as spoken to you?

## 08 Lydia, Priscilla and Phoebe: women in the first churches

**Main readings:** Acts 16:11–15, 40; 18:1–3, 18–28; Romans 16:1–7; 1 Corinthians 16:19.

The first convert Luke records in Europe was a businesswoman at a riverside place of prayer.

### Lydia: an open heart and an open house

> Among those listening was a woman named Lydia, a dealer in purple cloth from the city of Thyatira, who was a worshiper of God. The Lord opened her heart to respond to Paul's message.

Acts 16:14 · BSB

Note who opened her heart: the Lord. She and her household were baptized, and she pressed Paul and his companions to stay with her. When Paul and Silas came out of prison, "they went to Lydia's house to see the brothers and encourage them" (Acts 16:40 · BSB). Her home was where the believers of Philippi gathered.

### Priscilla: a teacher at home, a risk-taker for Paul

Priscilla and her husband Aquila were tentmakers who had been forced out of Rome; Paul lived and worked with them in Corinth (Acts 18:2–3). In Ephesus they heard Apollos, a gifted preacher who knew only part of the story:

> When Priscilla and Aquila heard him, they took him in and explained to him the way of God more accurately.

Acts 18:26 · BSB

Paul called them "my fellow workers in Christ Jesus, who have risked their lives for me" (Romans 16:3–4 · BSB), and a church met in their house (1 Corinthians 16:19). Her name often comes first. Matthew Henry, on Acts 18:26, calls this kind of private instruction "a piece of very good service, both to them and to the church".

### Phoebe: a servant of the church

> I commend to you our sister Phoebe, a servant of the church in Cenchrea. Welcome her in the Lord in a manner worthy of the saints, and assist her with anything she may need from you. For she has been a great help to many people, including me.

Romans 16:1–2 · BSB

John Calvin took her to be the one who carried this letter to Rome, and said she "performed a most honorable and a most holy function in the Church" (on Romans 16:1). The Greek word for "servant" here is the word from which "deacon" comes, and the Berean's own note gives the other rendering: "Or deaconess".

### Junia: in Christ before Paul

A few lines further down the same chapter, Paul greets another woman. She has one verse, not a story, so she is not counted among the sixteen; but the verse is worth reading slowly.

> Greet Andronicus and Junia, my fellow countrymen and fellow prisoners. They are outstanding among the apostles, and they were in Christ before I was.

Romans 16:7 · BSB

What the text says: Andronicus and Junia were Jews, as Paul was. They had been prisoners for the gospel, as he had; the verse does not say whether in the same prison. And they believed in Christ before Paul did, which puts their faith in the first years of the church. Many readers take them to be husband and wife, as Priscilla and Aquila were, but the verse does not say how they were related.

Readers bring two questions to the verse.

**Is Junia a woman?** The Berean prints the woman's name Junia, and most scholars today read it that way. Some later Bibles printed a man's name, Junias. John Chrysostom, a Greek preacher of the late 300s, took her as a woman and marvelled that she was counted worthy to be called an apostle [check: the wording and date of Chrysostom's Homily 31 on Romans in a standard English translation, before quoting it].

**What does "outstanding among the apostles" mean?** "Apostle" means "one who is sent". In its narrow sense the word names the Twelve and Paul, sent by the risen Christ Himself. The New Testament also uses it more widely: Luke calls Barnabas an apostle beside Paul (Acts 14:14), and Paul uses the same Greek word for messengers sent by the churches (2 Corinthians 8:23). Readers take the phrase in two ways:

- **Well known to the apostles.** The apostles held them in high regard; the phrase does not count them among the apostles. Some English versions translate it this way; the English Standard Version has "well known to the apostles".
- **Outstanding as apostles.** They were themselves apostles in the wider sense: missionaries sent out to carry the gospel, as the Berean's wording suggests.

### Where the passage stops

Neither reading makes Junia one of the Twelve, and the verse says nothing about her teaching or leading a congregation. With the Baptist Faith and Message, this site holds that "the office of pastor is limited to men as qualified by Scripture" (Article VI) [check: whether article VI now reads "pastor" or "pastor/elder/overseer", against bfm.sbc.net]. A woman whom Paul honours as a believer older in the faith than he was, who went to prison for Christ, stands well within that line, whichever reading of the phrase is right. Churches that ordain women as pastors often point to this verse as a woman holding an apostle's authority. That is their reading, not this site's.

### Where Baptists differ: may women serve as deacons?

The Baptist Faith and Message names two offices, "pastors and deacons" (Article VI). It limits the first to men and says nothing more about the second. A deacon serves the church in its practical care, and is not one of its pastors. Paul's list for deacons includes a verse the Berean renders "the women must be dignified" (1 Timothy 3:11 · BSB), with the note "Or their wives". Baptists read these verses in two ways:

- **Deacons are men.** Criswell, in 1984: "Because of the biblical interdiction against the ordination of a woman as a pastor or as a deacon, I am going to create in the church a pastor's council that will include women along with dedicated men."
- **Women may be deacons, though not elders.** John Piper, then pastor of Bethlehem Baptist Church in Minneapolis, said of Phoebe, "She may well have been an official deacon in that church," and, "There is no reason why (as I read the qualifications in 1 Timothy 3) women cannot be deacons." He kept the eldership for men: "The elders are men and are charged with the governance and teaching of the church" (sermon on Romans 16, 2006).

Both hold that the office of pastor is for men. This study does not choose between them.

**Consider:** Lydia gave her home, Priscilla her teaching and her safety, Phoebe her help, Junia her freedom in prison. What did each give, and to whom?

**Reflect:** Which of these women's gifts do you see in someone in your church? Have you thanked them, as Paul did?

Readers bring this question to Deborah, Huldah, Priscilla, Phoebe and Junia. Here is the site's answer, and where others differ.

**This site's view.** With the Baptist Faith and Message, this site holds that "the office of pastor is limited to men as qualified by Scripture" (Article VI). The grounds are in Paul's letters: "I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man" (1 Timothy 2:12 · BSB), a rule he grounds in creation, "For Adam was formed first, and then Eve" (1 Timothy 2:13 · BSB); and an overseer is to be "the husband of but one wife" (1 Timothy 3:2 · BSB). The same New Testament honours women who prophesied, as Joel foretold, "Your sons and daughters will prophesy" (Acts 2:17 · BSB), and women who taught, gave, hosted and served. These stories do not overturn that rule; they show the many ways women served within it. Deborah and Huldah were prophets under the old covenant, not elders of a church. Priscilla taught with her husband in their home. Phoebe served. Junia was honoured for her faith and her suffering. John Calvin, who was not a Baptist, answered the appeal to Deborah: "Extraordinary acts done by God do not overturn the ordinary rules of government, by which he intended that we should be bound" (on 1 Timothy 2:12).

**Praying, prophesying and silence.** In one letter Paul writes two things readers find hard to fit together. In chapter 11 he assumes that women pray and prophesy aloud in the gathering, and he tells them how:

> But I want you to understand that the head of every man is Christ, and the head of the woman is man, and the head of Christ is God. Every man who prays or prophesies with his head covered dishonors his head. And every woman who prays or prophesies with her head uncovered dishonors her head, for it is just as if her head were shaved.

1 Corinthians 11:3–5 · BSB

Three chapters later he writes:

> Women are to be silent in the churches. They are not permitted to speak, but must be in submission, as the law says. If they wish to inquire about something, they are to ask their own husbands at home; for it is dishonorable for a woman to speak in the church.

1 Corinthians 14:34–35 · BSB

Careful readers fit the two in more than one way:

- **Silent in the weighing of prophecy.** Some take 14:34–35 with the verses just before it, where prophets speak in turn and the others weigh carefully what is said (1 Corinthians 14:29). Women may pray and prophesy, as chapter 11 assumes, but are not to take part in the public judging of prophecy, which carries the authority to teach and correct. The question about asking husbands at home (14:35) suggests questioning in the meeting, not every word.
- **The strict reading.** Albert Barnes, a Presbyterian commentator, takes 14:34 as it stands: "This rule is positive: explicit and universal". He answers chapter 11 by saying that the women who prophesied at Corinth were inspired, so "their example is no warrant now" for public teaching without inspiration (on 1 Corinthians 14:34 and 11:5) [check: both quotations against the text of Barnes' Notes].

This page, like *The church and its calling*, does not settle how exactly the two fit. Both readings keep chapter 11 and chapter 14 as Scripture without setting either aside, and both keep the office of pastor for men. Read more in *The church and its calling*.

**Other traditions' view.** Many Protestant churches, among them most Methodist churches, many Pentecostal and Anglican churches, and some churches that call themselves Baptist, ordain women as pastors. They point to these same women, and to "neither Jew nor Greek, slave nor free, male nor female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28 · BSB), and read 1 Timothy 2:12 as a rule for one church's situation in Ephesus rather than for every church. That is their reading, not this site's.

**In the home.** The same confession says husband and wife are "of equal worth before God, since both are created in God's image". It asks the husband to love his wife "as Christ loved the church", and the wife to submit "graciously to the servant leadership of her husband" (Article XVIII). Peter calls husband and wife "fellow heirs of the gracious gift of life" (1 Peter 3:7 · BSB). Equal worth and different callings are held together in the same texts.

## What these women show

Read together, these sixteen stories say something about God before they say anything about women. He kept His word to a laughing Sarah, saw a runaway slave, brought a Canaanite and a Moabite into the family line of His Son, spoke through Deborah and Huldah, heard Hannah, used Abigail's wisdom and Esther's courage, chose Mary to bear His Son and Mary Magdalene to announce His rising, and opened Lydia's heart. Equal in worth as bearers of His image, these women served Him in faith, often at great cost. The three questions work on every life you read in Scripture: what the text says she did, what God was doing, and how later Scripture remembers her.
