# The apostles and their witness

Working content draft · Study 40 · Owner review pending

Jesus called the Twelve to be with him and sent them to preach. The Gospels also show their questions, ambition and need of correction. Acts follows their witness to the risen Jesus, while the letters address the faith and life of the communities that received their preaching. Their importance rests in the one to whom they bear witness.

Following an apostle through Scripture means listening to each account in its setting. A Gospel scene, a speech in Acts and a personal recollection in a letter may illuminate the same life, but each has its own purpose. Nor does Scripture give every apostle an equally detailed biography. These readings follow selected people and encounters without filling the quieter lives with imagined journeys or speeches.

## 01 Calling to be with him and to preach

**Read:** Mark 3:13–19; Luke 6:12–16; Acts 1:15–26; 1 Corinthians 15:8–11.

> And he ordained twelve, that they should be with him, and that he might send them forth to preach,

Mark 3:14 · KJV

### Jesus chooses the Twelve

Mark places being with Jesus beside being sent by him. The twelve are called into his company and given work to do. The following verse speaks of authority over sicknesses and devils; the list then names individuals, including Judas Iscariot, whom Mark identifies as the betrayer. Being named among the Twelve does not make the account a series of unbroken successes.

Luke describes Jesus spending the night in prayer before choosing twelve from his disciples and naming them apostles. That wording distinguishes the disciples from this particular group within them. It is useful to retain the distinction when reading later scenes: a reference to disciples does not automatically mean only the Twelve, and a reference to the Twelve identifies a particular company.

The names deserve attention without invented portraits. Mark identifies brothers and gives certain additional names, but he does not supply a personality study for every man. Where an apostle appears principally in a list, the list still tells us something definite: he belongs among those Jesus called. Brevity is not a judgment that his service mattered less.

### Matthias and Paul enter the account differently

In Acts 1, Peter describes the witness needed to take Judas's place. The proposed man must have accompanied them during Jesus' ministry, from John's baptism to the ascension, and become a witness with them of his resurrection. After prayer and the giving of lots, Matthias is numbered with the eleven apostles. These are the stated conditions for that appointment in this scene.

Paul speaks of himself differently in 1 Corinthians 15. He places Christ's appearance to him after the appearances he has already listed, remembers that he persecuted the church, and attributes his subsequent labour to God's grace. His account must be heard on its own terms. Acts 1 is not a reason to erase Paul's testimony or quietly substitute him for Matthias.

**Consider:** What does each passage actually say about the person's calling, and who is telling it?

**Reflect:** How does being with Jesus give substance to the work of being sent?

## 02 Learning the way of service

**Read:** Mark 10:35–45; Luke 22:24–34; John 21:15–19.

> And whosoever of you will be the chiefest, shall be servant of all.

Mark 10:44 · KJV

### James and John ask for places of honour

In Mark 10, James and John ask to sit at Jesus' right and left in his glory. Jesus answers by speaking of the cup he drinks and the baptism with which he is baptized. Their request for places of honour meets a teaching about sharing his suffering. When the other ten become displeased, Jesus gathers them and addresses the whole company.

His instruction concerns their relationships with one another. The rule exercised by the great among the Gentiles is not to determine greatness among them. The one who would be chief is to serve all. Jesus then gives the reason in his own coming to serve and to give his life a ransom for many. Service here receives its meaning from him; it is more than a technique for gaining influence.

The account does not invite us to stand above two unusually ambitious disciples. The displeasure of the ten brings everyone into the conversation. Readers can ask how they seek honour, whose needs they overlook, and whether their understanding of leadership agrees with Jesus' words.

### Peter needs prayer and correction

Luke 22 places another dispute about greatness beside Jesus' words about service. Jesus then addresses Simon, speaks of Satan's demand, and tells him that he has prayed for him. Peter declares himself ready for prison and death; Jesus foretells his denial. Confidence in his own readiness does not mean Peter understands the trial before him.

The charge to strengthen his brethren accompanies Jesus' prayer. In John 21, the risen Jesus asks Peter whether he loves him and charges him to feed his lambs and sheep. These passages give service a personal seriousness: the one who cares for others is himself dependent on Jesus. They should not be flattened into either a flawless portrait or a story in which failure has the final word.

**Consider:** What do the disciples ask or claim, and how does Jesus redirect them?

**Reflect:** Where does the desire to be important need to give way to the responsibility to serve?

## 03 Witness to the risen Jesus

**Read:** Acts 4:5–20; 1 Corinthians 15:1–19.

> For we can not but speak the things which we have seen and heard.

Acts 4:20 · KJV

### Peter and John answer the council

The council asks by what power or name the deed has been done. Peter's answer points to Jesus Christ of Nazareth, whom his hearers crucified and whom God raised from the dead. The healed man stands there with them. Peter connects the deed to Jesus and speaks of salvation in his name; he does not make the apostles themselves the source of the man's restoration.

The rulers recognize Peter and John's boldness and that they have been with Jesus. When ordered not to speak or teach in Jesus' name, the two appeal to what is right in God's sight and to what they have seen and heard. Their refusal belongs to this specific conflict over their witness to Jesus. It does not make every personal conviction equivalent to apostolic testimony.

Notice the content as well as the courage. An account reduced to brave men confronting powerful men loses the central claim: God raised Jesus from the dead, and salvation is in him. Their speech is intelligible because it names the person and event for which they are answering.

### Paul recalls the gospel he delivered

In 1 Corinthians 15, Paul reminds the Corinthians of the gospel he preached and they received. He speaks of Christ's death for sins, burial and resurrection according to the Scriptures, followed by appearances to named people and groups. He includes himself while remembering his persecution of the church and the grace that changed his work.

This recollection serves the argument that follows. Some at Corinth say there is no resurrection of the dead. Paul asks what that would mean for the proclamation that Christ has risen, for faith, for sins and for those who have died in Christ. The list of witnesses is part of his instruction to a church, not an isolated list of notable names.

His conclusion in verse 11 joins his preaching to theirs: whether he or they preached, this is what the Corinthians believed. Read the witnesses in relation to that shared proclamation. The differences in their lives do not displace the risen Christ at its centre.

**Consider:** What is being witnessed to, and what question or opposition gives the testimony its immediate setting?

**Reflect:** When speaking about faith, how clearly do you identify what is being said about Jesus?

## 04 Journeys that gather and strengthen disciples

**Read:** Acts 13:1–4; 14:8–28.

> And when they were come, and had gathered the church together, they rehearsed all that God had done with them, and how he had opened the door of faith unto the Gentiles.

Acts 14:27 · KJV

### Sent from a praying church

Acts 13 begins with named prophets and teachers in the church at Antioch. While they minister to the Lord and fast, the Holy Ghost calls for Barnabas and Saul to be separated for his work. Prayer, fasting and the laying on of hands accompany their departure. Acts describes them as sent forth by the Holy Ghost.

Their journey is therefore more than an individual's decision to travel. The sending belongs within the life of a church, and the account directs attention to God's work. That perspective continues when they return and gather the church to report what God has done with them. Departure and return belong together.

Acts 14 calls both Barnabas and Paul apostles. The word is being used here beyond the original Twelve. Keeping that wording prevents a reader from forcing every occurrence into a list that is too narrow for the passage. It also keeps Barnabas visible in a narrative often remembered only as Paul's journey.

### Read the encounters between the places

At Lystra, the healing of a man who could not walk leads the crowd to call Barnabas and Paul gods. They tear their clothes and insist that they are men, calling their hearers to turn to the living God. The crowd's enthusiasm is not treated as success when it misunderstands the message and directs worship toward the messengers.

Opposition follows, and Paul is stoned and dragged out of the city. After going to Derbe, he and Barnabas return through places where they have preached. They strengthen disciples, exhort them to continue in faith, appoint elders, pray with fasting and commend them to the Lord. The work includes caring for the communities that remain after the visitors leave.

A place name alone cannot carry all this. For each stop, ask whom they met, what they said, how people responded and what followed. Travel, proclamation, suffering and the strengthening of disciples belong in the same account. A route can help locate the events; it cannot replace them.

**Consider:** What changes when you read the return visits alongside the first encounters?

**Reflect:** How might care for those already reached receive the same attention as going somewhere new?

## 05 Letters that continue the care

**Read:** 1 Thessalonians 1:1; 2:1–13; 5:12–28; 1 Peter 1:1–2.

> So being affectionately desirous of you, we were willing to have imparted unto you, not the gospel of God only, but also our own souls, because ye were dear unto us.

1 Thessalonians 2:8 · KJV

### Hear the address and the remembered visit

First Thessalonians opens with Paul, Silvanus and Timotheus addressing the church of the Thessalonians. Attend to those named senders before treating every plural statement as though it concerns Paul alone. Chapter 2 recalls suffering before their arrival, boldness in speaking the gospel, their conduct among the believers and the affection with which they served.

The recollection includes what they refused: deceit, flattering words, covetousness and seeking glory from people. It also describes gentleness, labour and personal exhortation. The gospel they spoke and the manner in which they lived are placed together. Their readers are repeatedly called upon as people who know and remember what happened.

This is a relationship being continued through a letter. The opening of 1 Peter likewise names a sender and addresses people in particular regions. Before extracting a line of instruction, ask who is being addressed and what the letter seeks for them. Its address is part of its meaning, not something to skip on the way to a familiar verse.

### Read instruction as care for a community

The closing instructions of 1 Thessalonians move through the community's life: esteem those who labour, live at peace, warn the unruly, comfort the fainthearted, support the weak and be patient toward all. Different needs receive different responses. Patient care is not reduced to saying the same thing to everyone.

Prayer, thanksgiving, discernment and the refusal to repay evil also belong together here. The letter concludes with prayer for the believers, confidence in the faithfulness of God, a request for their prayers, and a charge that the letter be read to all the brethren. Its words are intended to reach the community.

Reading a letter in this way gives apostolic witness a continuing dimension. It proclaims Christ and also teaches people how to live together before him. Names, memories, correction, encouragement and prayer should remain connected rather than becoming unrelated quotations.

**Consider:** How does the remembered conduct in chapter 2 help you hear the instructions in chapter 5?

**Reflect:** Which person needs warning, comfort or support, and how can patience shape your response?

## 06 What the accounts tell and what they leave unsaid

**Read:** John 21:18–25; Acts 12:1–5; 28:30–31.

> Then went this saying abroad among the brethren, that that disciple should not die: yet Jesus said not unto him, He shall not die; but, If I will that he tarry till I come, what is that to thee?

John 21:23 · KJV

### John corrects a saying that went abroad

After Jesus speaks to Peter about his death and calls him to follow, Peter asks about the disciple whom Jesus loved. Jesus answers with a conditional statement and returns Peter to his own following. The account then distinguishes that answer from the saying that spread among the brethren: Jesus had not said that the other disciple would not die.

The correction gives a concrete lesson in reading. A statement can travel beyond its wording and become a claim the speaker did not make. Here the account supplies both the mistaken saying and the words that correct it. Repeating a familiar story is not a substitute for returning to the passage.

John also explains that Jesus' words to Peter signify the death by which he would glorify God. That explanation should be retained, while recognizing that the chapter does not narrate Peter's execution. It gives a saying about his future, not a full account of its fulfilment.

### Let a life remain only partly told

Acts explicitly reports that Herod killed James the brother of John with the sword. The end of Acts, by contrast, leaves Paul receiving visitors and preaching and teaching during two years in his hired house. It does not finish by narrating his death. Different endings give different kinds of information; they should not be expanded into matching biographies.

A later account of an apostle's journeys or death needs its own named source and assessment. It cannot be presented as though it were another verse in Acts. Nor does an absence from the biblical narrative prove that a proposed event never happened. The honest statement may be that this account does not tell us.

Choose one apostle and follow a small group of passages. Write down the calling, words, actions and relationships they actually give. Keep an explanation drawn from comparing passages distinguishable from a direct statement, and leave an unanswered question open. The aim is to hear their witness faithfully, including where the account directs attention away from curiosity about the messenger and back to following Jesus.

**Consider:** Where does a passage state an event, interpret a saying, or leave the next part of a life untold?

**Reflect:** What familiar claim about an apostle would benefit from being checked against the words of its source?
