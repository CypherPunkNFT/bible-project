# Rulers and their choices

Working content draft · Study 39 · People of Scripture · Owner review pending

Scripture tells of rulers through more than their titles, territory or years on a throne. It records requests, counsel, worship, judgments, wrongdoing and responses to the word of the LORD. A ruler's choices affect other people, and the accounts often explain those effects as well as describing what happened.

A timeline helps locate a reign. A short verdict can point toward the account's judgment. Neither replaces reading the decisions, the people affected and the qualifications the writer preserves. A promising beginning does not tell us everything about a reign, and a later act of humbling does not make earlier harm disappear.

These six readings use selected kings of Israel and Judah. They are worked examples, not a complete survey of every ruler in Scripture. Read the narrator's evaluation, a ruler's claim and a prophet's speech as distinct voices, then consider how they belong together in the account.

## 01 Accession: what responsibility has been received?

**Main readings:** 1 Kings 3:3–15; 11:1–13.

> Give therefore thy servant an understanding heart to judge thy people, that I may discern between good and bad: for who is able to judge this thy so great a people?

1 Kings 3:9 · KJV

### Solomon asks for understanding to judge

Solomon speaks of being made king in place of David his father. He describes himself as a little child who does not know how to go out or come in, and he places himself among the people God has chosen. His request concerns the responsibility of judging that people and discerning between good and bad.

The account says this request pleased the Lord. God's answer contrasts what Solomon asked with long life, riches and the life of his enemies. Read the request and answer together: wisdom here concerns a responsibility toward others, not merely the possession of remarkable knowledge.

An accession study should therefore ask more than who inherited the throne. What duty is acknowledged? What does the ruler seek? What instruction or condition accompanies the authority received? This scene gives a particular answer without supplying a general promise that every ruler who asks for wisdom will receive Solomon's gifts.

### Read the beginning without making it the whole reign

The chapter already includes a qualification beside its statement that Solomon loved the LORD: his sacrifice and incense in high places. God's answer also speaks of walking in his ways and keeping his commandments. These words should remain in view alongside the gift of understanding.

Later, 1 Kings 11 says Solomon's heart turned after other gods and that he did evil in the sight of the LORD. The earlier request must not be used to conceal that evaluation. Equally, the later wrongdoing does not make the account of the earlier request disappear.

Follow the reign through its changes. Record what the text commends, what it qualifies and what it condemns, with references for each. A life can contain a wise request and later disobedience; reading both keeps a summary accountable to the narrative.

**Consider:** What does Solomon's request reveal about the responsibility he has received, and what conditions remain in God's answer?

**Reflect:** When you receive responsibility, do you ask mainly for advantage or for understanding of those affected by your decisions?

## 02 Decisions: whose counsel is heard?

**Main reading:** 1 Kings 12:1–16.

> And the king answered the people roughly, and forsook the old men’s counsel that they gave him;

1 Kings 12:13 · KJV

### The people's request comes before the advisers' answers

At Shechem the people ask Rehoboam to lighten the grievous service and heavy yoke associated with his father. He tells them to return after three days and seeks advice. The older men counsel service, a favourable answer and good words. The men who grew up with him counsel an assertion of greater severity.

Read both responses in relation to what the people have asked. The issue is not simply whether the advisers are old or young. Their advice differs in its treatment of the people and in the kind of relationship it seeks between king and subjects.

The text says Rehoboam forsakes the older men's counsel and answers roughly. His reply repeats the threat to increase the burden. The narrative gives us the request, the available advice and the chosen answer, allowing the decision to be examined rather than merely labelled unwise.

### Keep the narrator's explanation beside the choice

Verse 15 also connects the turn of events with the LORD's word through Ahijah to Jeroboam. This explanation belongs to the account alongside the king's refusal to listen. Do not omit it in order to turn the scene into a self-contained lesson about management, or use it to erase the harsh answer the account has just described.

The people respond by rejecting their share in David's house and departing to their tents. The consequences reach beyond Rehoboam's conversation with his advisers. Authority exercised in speech affects the people who must live under it.

When studying a decision, record the request, the advice, the chosen action, the response and the narrator's explanation. This preserves more than a general maxim about listening. It helps show precisely what was heard, what was refused and what followed.

**Consider:** How does each group of advisers propose that Rehoboam relate to the people?

**Reflect:** What makes it difficult to receive counsel that asks you to serve rather than assert your position?

## 03 Worship: what does the ruler ask the people to follow?

**Main reading:** 1 Kings 12:26–33.

> And Jeroboam said in his heart, Now shall the kingdom return to the house of David:

1 Kings 12:26 · KJV

### The account states Jeroboam's concern

Jeroboam considers what may happen if the people go to Jerusalem to sacrifice. He fears that their hearts will return to Rehoboam and that they will kill him. This motive is supplied by the narrative itself; it is not a psychological reconstruction we need to invent.

He then makes two calves of gold, sets them in Bethel and Dan, and addresses the people with language about being brought out of Egypt. The passage continues with a house of high places, priests who are not sons of Levi, and a feast. Read these acts as connected decisions rather than separate items in a catalogue of buildings.

The ruler's concern for his own position has consequences for the people's worship. The account makes that connection available for examination through what Jeroboam says in his heart and what he subsequently establishes.

### Hear the evaluation, not only the justification

Jeroboam's words to the people present the journey to Jerusalem as too much for them. The narrator says that the thing became a sin and describes the month as devised from Jeroboam's own heart. His stated justification and the account's evaluation are not the same voice.

An accurate summary should include both. Reporting that a ruler offered convenience or security does not establish that the account approves what he did. Ask how the writer names the action and what relationship it has to the LORD's worship and instruction.

This passage is about Jeroboam's particular actions and their effect on Israel. Applications about influence or self-interest should grow from that evidence without turning every unfamiliar practice into an exact equivalent. The first task is to understand the choices the text actually describes.

**Consider:** How does the passage connect Jeroboam's fear with the worship he establishes?

**Reflect:** Where might protecting your position distort what you encourage others to do?

## 04 Justice: whose loss is hidden by royal gain?

**Main reading:** 1 Kings 21:1–29.

> And Naboth said to Ahab, The LORD forbid it me, that I should give the inheritance of my fathers unto thee.

1 Kings 21:3 · KJV

### Follow the vineyard through the whole account

Ahab wants Naboth's vineyard because it lies near his house. He offers another vineyard or payment. Naboth refuses in relation to the inheritance of his fathers. Ahab returns displeased, and Jezebel arranges letters in his name and under his seal directing the city's elders and nobles to act against Naboth.

The narrative describes false witnesses, an accusation, Naboth's death and the message that Ahab can take possession. These are not incidental details on the way to a ruler acquiring land. They show how authority, public proceedings and the cooperation of others are used against a named person.

Keep the agents distinct. Jezebel writes the instructions; the elders and nobles carry them out; witnesses make the accusation; Ahab goes to possess the vineyard. A careful reading neither assigns every action to one person nor uses the involvement of several people to make responsibility vanish.

### The prophetic word addresses possession and guilt

The LORD sends Elijah to meet Ahab at the vineyard with the question about killing and taking possession. The account's judgment follows Ahab into the place of his gain. Naboth's death is not left as an unfortunate event unrelated to the king's benefit.

Read through the end of the chapter as well. It strongly condemns Ahab's wrongdoing, then reports his humbling himself and the LORD's word about delaying judgment on his house. That response belongs to the account without cancelling the wrong done to Naboth.

When studying royal achievement, ask whose circumstances the narrative also records. A building, territory or possession may look different when the account names the people harmed in acquiring it. The biblical judgment cannot be replaced by a tally of what the ruler gained.

**Consider:** How does the passage distribute actions and responsibility among the people involved?

**Reflect:** What could help you notice another person's loss when you stand to benefit from a decision?

## 05 Prophetic encounters: does the ruler receive correction?

**Main reading:** 2 Samuel 12:1–14.

> And David said unto Nathan, I have sinned against the LORD. And Nathan said unto David, The LORD also hath put away thy sin; thou shalt not die.

2 Samuel 12:13 · KJV

### Nathan's account turns judgment toward David

Nathan tells David about a rich man taking a poor man's cherished lamb rather than using his own abundance. David responds with anger and a demand for judgment. Nathan then identifies David as the man and speaks the LORD's word concerning the gifts he received and the wrongdoing he committed.

The rebuke names Uriah's death and the taking of his wife. It describes the wrongdoing as despising the commandment of the LORD. The encounter is therefore more than a ruler being embarrassed by a clever story. The king who readily judges another is confronted with his own conduct.

Read the accusation with the reminder of what David received. The account sets royal privilege beside accountability. A person's honoured position within the wider narrative does not make the present rebuke less serious.

### Confession and consequences both remain

David confesses that he has sinned against the LORD, and Nathan announces that his sin has been put away and that he will not die. The surrounding speech also announces consequences. Keep those words together rather than making forgiveness mean the passage has said nothing further about the damage or what follows.

The reading should retain the people harmed, the sin named and the response given. A summary that speaks only of David's restoration can neglect the severity of the rebuke; a summary that omits his confession and Nathan's reply also fails to represent the account.

When a ruler encounters a prophet, trace the exchange. What is exposed? How does the ruler answer? What does the passage say about mercy, resistance or judgment? The answer should come from the recorded encounter rather than from a fixed reputation assigned to the ruler beforehand.

**Consider:** How does Nathan's rebuke connect the gifts David received with the wrongs he committed?

**Reflect:** What is the difference between recognizing wrongdoing in a story about another person and receiving correction about yourself?

## 06 The account's verdict: preserve what each writer tells

**Main readings:** 2 Kings 21:1–16; 2 Chronicles 33:1–17.

> And prayed unto him: and he was intreated of him, and heard his supplication, and brought him again to Jerusalem into his kingdom. Then Manasseh knew that the LORD he was God.

2 Chronicles 33:13 · KJV

### Read Kings and Chronicles in their own sequence

Both passages describe Manasseh's evil, his establishment of idolatrous worship and the harm of his influence on the people. Kings also speaks of the innocent blood he shed and the announced judgment on Jerusalem and Judah. Those statements give specific content to its evaluation of the reign.

Chronicles recounts his refusal to listen, his capture and affliction, his humbling himself and prayer, and his return. It then reports the removal of strange gods and altars, the repair of the LORD's altar and his command that Judah serve the LORD. The passage also retains a qualification about the people's continued sacrifice in high places.

Read each sequence before combining them. The shared account of wrongdoing and the additional movement in Chronicles should both remain visible. An explanation should not make Kings say that it narrates the return, or use the return in Chronicles to erase the evil both books describe.

### Make a judgment record rather than a score

For a reign study, keep the verdict's wording beside its reference and the actions it concerns. Note qualifications, changes of conduct and differences between accounts. A short label may help you find the reign, but it cannot carry every part of the narrative's evaluation.

The same restraint applies to chronology. A stated age, reign length or relationship to another king's year differs from a proposed date in a modern chronology. Record which kind of evidence you are using instead of giving every date the same authority.

Choose one ruler and assemble a modest record: how the reign begins, a decision, worship, treatment of others, a prophetic encounter if recorded, and the account's evaluation. Keep each passage's voice identifiable. The purpose is to understand power and responsibility as Scripture presents them, including the people affected and the limits of what the accounts tell us.

**Consider:** What does each account of Manasseh emphasize, and what would a single verdict label leave out?

**Reflect:** How can attention to both wrongdoing and subsequent response improve the way you read a complicated life?
