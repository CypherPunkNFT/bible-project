# Rulers and their choices

Concepts: Who says so · Who paid · What came after
Motto: A crown. A scroll. / A line let down.
Lead: The writer of Kings gives nearly every king of Israel and Judah a line of verdict, and very often that line names someone earlier as the measure. A verdict is short. The choices behind it are not, and other people paid for them. So this study teaches three questions for any reign you read: who says so, who paid for it, and what came after?

> When he is seated on his royal throne, he must write for himself a copy of this instruction on a scroll in the presence of the Levitical priests. It is to remain with him, and he is to read from it all the days of his life, so that he may learn to fear the LORD his God by carefully observing all the words of this instruction and these statutes. Then his heart will not be exalted above his countrymen, and he will not turn aside from the commandment, to the right or to the left, in order that he and his sons may reign many years over his kingdom in Israel.

Deuteronomy 17:18–20 · BSB

Selection: This page draws all {{rulers}} rulers the books of Kings tell of in the two kingdoms after they parted, with the reign the text states and the verdict it gives. It reads six reigns closely, follows six kings through Chronicles as well, and sets three royal letters side by side. These are worked examples. David and Solomon reigned before the kingdoms parted, so neither is on a beam: David appears as the peg Judah's verdicts look back to, and Solomon has a reading of his own. Tibni, who contended with Omri in 1 Kings 16:21–22, is not drawn, because no reign is stated for him. Saul, the judges, governors and the rulers of other nations belong to the site's wider collection of rulers, apart from Xerxes, who is here for his signet ring. No present-day leader is scored on this page, and no year on it comes from a modern chronology.

MeasureLead: Who is this king being measured against? The writer often says. Each string hangs from its kingdom's beam, as long as the reign the text states. Each shape is the writer's verdict. A thread above the beam runs back to Jeroboam son of Nebat, or to David, where the verdict names him.
MeasureReads: Two names come back most often, and they do not do the same work. In Israel, {{tied}} of the {{afterJeroboam}} kings after Jeroboam son of Nebat are tied by name to his way or his sins, down to Pekah, near the kingdom's end. There the name is always a charge. In Judah, {{david}} of the {{judah}} rulers are set against David: {{davidAs}} as David and {{davidNot}} not. So David's name comes back far less often, and it can be praise. A choice made in one reign was still being counted in reigns that had never met him. And length is no verdict: the longest stated reign in either kingdom is Manasseh's {{longest}}.
MeasureCare: The beams show order within each kingdom. They do not show time or overlap, and the two beams do not line up: some reigns overlapped, as 2 Kings 15:5 shows of Azariah and his son Jotham, and this page does not work that out. The strings are the Berean's stated lengths. A dashed string was drawn longer than its scale so that you can see it: {{drawnLong}} reigns are under two years. Athaliah's six years are read from 2 Kings 11:3, where Joash is hidden "for six years while Athaliah ruled the land", and no verse states her reign as the others are stated. The shapes follow the verses: a diamond is any line that charges the ruler, each in its own words, and these differ. Which name a verdict rests on is this study's reading of the verse or the one beside it, and the verse is always one choice away. The verdicts are the writer's, under God. They say what a reign was in the sight of the LORD. They do not say what became of any ruler's soul, and this page does not say either. The plumb line is this study's picture, from 2 Kings 21:13, where the LORD speaks of the line used against Samaria and the house of Ahab. The writer does not call David or Jeroboam a plumb line.

ReadingsLead: Six rulers, six kinds of choice. Each reading opens with the choice in a form of its own, then the three questions: who says so, who paid and what came after. Then comes what the passage says, what this study takes from it, and a question left for you.

ThreadsLead: Do two accounts of one king have to say the same things? Samuel and Kings tell these reigns, and so does Chronicles. Where the two threads touch, both tell it. Where they part, only one does. Choose a king.
ThreadsReads: Chronicles is not simply kinder or simply harsher. It leaves out Uriah and Solomon's turn. It gives Abijah a speech Kings does not have. It adds Asa's prison, Joash's stoning of Zechariah and the reason for Uzziah's leprosy, and Manasseh's prayer in the last of the six readings. Read each account in its own order before you put them together. Then let neither one silence the other.
ThreadsCare: The stops are this study's selection, not every event of these reigns. The script proves the words are in the verses. It does not prove that two verses tell the same event. The writers do not say why one tells what another leaves out, and this page gives no reason for them. This site holds Scripture to be "truth, without any mixture of error", so a stop told only once is an addition to the account and never a correction of it.

SealLead: How does a ruler do harm without lifting a hand? Three times a letter goes out under royal authority and someone dies, or is meant to. Read down each one. Every line is a different hand.
SealReads: No one here can say they only carried it, and no king can say he only signed. David wrote his own letter and had his victim carry it. Jezebel wrote in Ahab's name, and Ahab went down to collect. Xerxes handed over the ring and told Haman to do as he pleased. The writer names each hand. For Xerxes there is no line of verdict at all. The book of Esther never names God, and it still shows what a ruler's signature can reach.
SealCare: Keeping the hands apart does not spread the blame thin. Nathan and Elijah still go to the king. Esther does not end at the first letter. The second goes out under the same ring, because the first "cannot be revoked" in Esther 8:8, and what it permits in Esther 8:11 is as wide in its words as the first, "including women and children". This page states that and does not explain it away. The letters are in the passages. Setting them side by side is this study's own, and none of these passages cites another. The second and third letters name a seal, and David's has none named.

QuestionsLead: Six things readers ask, each opening on the side. Each gives the passage, then this study's reading in the site's own voice, then what others have written, labelled as theirs.

## 01 Accession: what did he ask for?

Slug: accession
Read: 1 Kings 3:3–15; 1 Kings 11:1–13
Ask: What does someone ask for, the day the job becomes theirs?
Hook: God tells him to ask. A new king could name anything. He asks to be able to judge other people well.

### He asked for understanding to judge

> Therefore give Your servant an understanding heart to judge Your people and to discern between good and evil. For who is able to govern this great people of Yours?

1 Kings 3:9 · BSB

Solomon calls himself "only a little child" in 1 Kings 3:7, and he places himself among the people God has chosen. His request is about them. The account says "it pleased the Lord" in 1 Kings 3:10, and God's answer names what he did not ask for: "long life or wealth for yourself or death for your enemies" in 1 Kings 3:11.

[read] When you read how a reign begins, ask more than who took the throne. What duty does this person admit to? What do they reach for? What condition comes with what they are given? Here wisdom is for a task that faces other people. It is not simply a remarkable gift to have. This scene is no promise that everyone who asks for wisdom will get Solomon's riches: the riches in 1 Kings 3:13 are given to him, and they are not a reward held out to every reader.

### Do not let the beginning stand for the whole reign

The same chapter already carries a qualification. Solomon "loved the LORD", and "he sacrificed and burned incense on the high places" in 1 Kings 3:3. God's answer also carries a condition: "if you walk in My ways" in 1 Kings 3:14. Then the account goes on. "his wives turned his heart after other gods" in 1 Kings 11:4, and "Solomon did evil in the sight of the LORD" in 1 Kings 11:6.

Deuteronomy had warned of it long before: "He must not take many wives for himself, lest his heart go astray" in Deuteronomy 17:17. Solomon's later wrongdoing does not make the earlier request false. The earlier request does not hide the later wrongdoing. A life can hold both, and a fair summary keeps both, each with its verse.

[care] The account does not put Solomon's turning on women in general. Its charge is against him: "Solomon did not keep the LORD's command" in 1 Kings 11:10.

Consider: What does Solomon's request show about the responsibility he has received, and what condition stays in God's answer?
Reflect: When you were last given responsibility, did you ask first for what would help you, or for understanding of the people your decisions would reach?

## 02 Counsel: whose advice did he take?

Slug: counsel
Read: 1 Kings 12:1–20
Ask: Whose advice do you take, when the people under you ask for mercy?
Hook: The people ask for a lighter load. He gets two answers and three days to think.

### The people's request comes first

> And the king answered the people harshly. He rejected the advice of the elders

1 Kings 12:13 · BSB

At Shechem the people say, "Your father put a heavy yoke on us", in 1 Kings 12:4. Rehoboam sends them away for three days and asks for advice. The elders who served Solomon say, "If you will be a servant to these people and serve them this day", they will serve you forever, in 1 Kings 12:7. The young men who grew up with him give him a line about his little finger.

[read] The difference is not old against young. It is how each answer treats the people who asked. One kind of king serves them so that they will serve. The other kind adds to the load so that they will fear. The account gives you the request, the advice and the answer, so you can examine the decision and not only call it unwise. No rule about the age of advisers comes from this one scene.

### Keep the writer's explanation beside the choice

Then the writer adds a sentence of his own: "this turn of events was from the LORD" in 1 Kings 12:15. It fulfils what the LORD said through Ahijah. The same verse says "the king did not listen to the people". Both stand. The sentence does not make this a neat lesson about management, and it does not take the harsh answer away from Rehoboam.

The cost falls first on those who have to live under the answer. "To your tents, O Israel!" they say in 1 Kings 12:16. When the king sends his overseer of forced labor, "all Israel stoned him to death" in 1 Kings 12:18. A king's few sentences have split a people, and the writer says "to this day" in 1 Kings 12:19.

Consider: How does each group of advisers propose that Rehoboam should relate to the people?
Reflect: What makes it hard for you to hear counsel that asks you to serve, when you would rather assert your position?

## 03 Worship: what did he give them to follow?

Slug: worship
Read: 1 Kings 12:26–33; 1 Kings 13:33–34
Ask: What are you protecting, when you tell people what is good for them?
Hook: He fears for his throne. He tells the people he is sparing them a long walk.

### The account tells you what he said in his heart

> Jeroboam said in his heart, "Now the kingdom might revert to the house of David.

1 Kings 12:26 · BSB

We do not have to guess his motive. The writer gives it: if the people go up to Jerusalem, "they will kill me", in 1 Kings 12:27. He then makes two golden calves, sets them at Bethel and Dan, adds shrines and priests "who were not Levites" in 1 Kings 12:31, and a feast in "a month of his own choosing" in 1 Kings 12:33.

[read] These are connected decisions and not a catalogue of buildings. Fear for his position shaped what a whole people did on their feast days.

### Hear the verdict as well as the reason given

To the people he says, "Going up to Jerusalem is too much for you", in 1 Kings 12:28. That is his voice. The writer's is different: "this thing became a sin" in 1 Kings 12:30.

[read] An accurate summary keeps both voices. Reporting that a ruler offered people convenience does not mean the account approves. And Jeroboam's choice did not end with him. The later kings on the beam above were still tied to it, and when the northern kingdom falls the writer says, "The Israelites persisted in all the sins that Jeroboam had committed", in 2 Kings 17:22.

[care] This passage is about what Jeroboam did and what it did to Israel. It is no rule against any unfamiliar way of worship today. Take from it what it shows: a leader's fear can bend what he asks others to do.

Consider: How does the passage connect Jeroboam's fear with the worship he sets up?
Reflect: Where might protecting your own position be bending what you encourage others to do?

## 04 Justice: who paid for the vineyard?

Slug: justice
Read: 1 Kings 21:1–29
Ask: Whose loss disappears, when you stand to gain?
Hook: A man says no to a king about his family's land. By the end of the chapter the king is walking in it.

### Follow the vineyard through the whole account

> But Naboth replied, "The LORD forbid that I should give you the inheritance of my fathers."

1 Kings 21:3 · BSB

Ahab offers "a better vineyard" or "its value in silver" in 1 Kings 21:2. Naboth refuses because this land is his family's inheritance. Ahab goes home "sullen and angry" in 1 Kings 21:4. Jezebel writes the letters and the elders obey them. Two men lie, and Naboth is stoned. The section on three letters, further down this page, sets those hands in order.

[read] Those details are not incidental on the way to a king gaining a garden. They show authority, a public court and other people's cooperation being used against one named man. A careful reading neither loads every act onto one person nor lets the number of people make the responsibility vanish.

### The word of the LORD goes to the place of the gain

The LORD sends Elijah to meet Ahab in the vineyard with a question: "Have you not murdered a man and seized his land?" in 1 Kings 21:19. Ahab did not write the letters. The question comes to him all the same, because he went down to take what they won. A later verse names more blood than the chapter does: "the blood of Naboth and the blood of his sons" in 2 Kings 9:26.

Then the chapter goes further than a reader expects. Ahab tears his clothes and fasts. The LORD says, "he has humbled himself before Me", and delays the calamity, in 1 Kings 21:29. That belongs to the account. It gives Naboth nothing back. Ahab's humbling is one of the six questions below.

[read] When you read of a ruler's achievement, ask who else the account records. A building, a territory or a garden looks different when the account names the person who lost it.

Consider: How does the passage share out actions and responsibility among the people involved?
Reflect: What could help you notice another person's loss, when you stand to benefit from a decision?

## 05 Correction: what did he do when the prophet came?

Slug: correction
Read: 2 Samuel 11:1–27; 2 Samuel 12:1–25
Ask: Can you hear a story about someone else, and let it be about you?
Hook: David is angry enough to condemn the rich man. Then Nathan says four words.

### Nathan's story turns judgment around

> Then David said to Nathan, "I have sinned against the LORD." "The LORD has taken away your sin," Nathan replied. "You will not die.

2 Samuel 12:13 · BSB

Nathan tells of a rich man who took a poor man's one lamb. David burns with anger: "the man who did this deserves to die" in 2 Samuel 12:5. Nathan answers, "You are that man!" in 2 Samuel 12:7, and goes straight through what God gave him and what he took: "You put Uriah the Hittite to the sword and took his wife as your own" in 2 Samuel 12:9.

[read] This is more than a ruler caught out by a clever story. The king who judges another so readily is made to read his own conduct. A great name in the wider story does not make this rebuke any lighter.

[care] The rebuke lays the charge on David. He "sent messengers to get her" in 2 Samuel 11:4, and the passage gives no words of hers until she sends to tell him she is pregnant. It does not say what she wanted or what she could have refused, and it does not make her the cause. Where a powerful person has used their position against someone in this way, the wrong is theirs, and it belongs with the people and authorities who can stop it.

### Forgiveness and consequences both remain

David confesses. Nathan says, "The LORD has taken away your sin" in 2 Samuel 12:13, and in the next sentence names what is still coming, in 2 Samuel 12:14. Keep those together. Summing up only the forgiveness forgets Uriah. Summing up only the rebuke forgets the confession and the word of mercy.

Others paid. Uriah died, and "some of David's servants fell" with him in 2 Samuel 11:17. Bathsheba "mourned for him" in 2 Samuel 11:26, and "On the seventh day the child died" in 2 Samuel 12:18. Later David "comforted his wife Bathsheba" in 2 Samuel 12:24. And the account keeps Uriah's name. The writer of Kings says David did right "except in the matter of Uriah the Hittite" in 1 Kings 15:5. Matthew's list of Jesus' own family line says "by Uriah's wife" in Matthew 1:6. He was not written out.

[care] The child died, and the account says so plainly. Nathan's word is about this child and this sin. It tells no parent why any other child has died, and it should never be used to. Nor does the judgment announced in 2 Samuel 12:11 give anyone leave to do what it describes.

Consider: How does Nathan's rebuke connect the gifts David received with the wrongs he did?
Reflect: What is the difference between seeing a wrong in someone else's story and accepting correction about yourself?

## 06 The verdict: what does each writer tell?

Slug: verdict
Read: 2 Kings 21:1–18; 2 Chronicles 33:1–20
Ask: What would one word for this king leave out?
Hook: Fifty-five years, and "more wickedly than the Amorites" in 2 Kings 21:11. One of the two accounts has a prayer in it.

### Read Kings and Chronicles in their own order

> And when he prayed to Him, the LORD received his plea and heard his petition; so He brought him back to Jerusalem and to his kingdom. Then Manasseh knew that the LORD is God.

2 Chronicles 33:13 · BSB

Both accounts tell of his evil, his altars in the LORD's house and his leading the people astray. Kings adds what the prophets said against him and the blood: "Manasseh shed so much innocent blood that he filled Jerusalem from end to end" in 2 Kings 21:16.

Chronicles then goes where Kings does not. The commanders of Assyria take him with "a hook in his nose" in 2 Chronicles 33:11. In his distress he "earnestly humbled himself" in 2 Chronicles 33:12. He comes home and removes "the foreign gods and the idol from the house of the LORD" in 2 Chronicles 33:15. And there is a qualification even there: "the people still sacrificed at the high places" in 2 Chronicles 33:17.

[read] Read each account through before you put them together. The account of the wrong and the account of the return both stay in view. Do not make Kings tell the return. Do not use the return to rub out what both books describe.

### Keep a record, not a score

[read] A short label can help you find a reign. It cannot carry the reign. Keep the verdict's wording with its reference and with the acts it concerns. Note its qualifications, any change of conduct, and any difference between accounts. A stated age or length of reign in Scripture is one kind of thing, and a date in a modern chronology is another, so say which you are using.

Then try it. Choose one ruler and keep a modest record: how the reign began, one decision, worship, how others were treated, a prophet if there was one, and the writer's words. Keep each voice identifiable. The aim is to understand power and responsibility as Scripture presents them, including the people affected and the limits of what the accounts tell.

Consider: What does each account of Manasseh bring forward, and what would a one-word verdict leave out?
Reflect: How might paying attention to both wrongdoing and later response change the way you read a complicated life?

## Question measure: How can David be the measure after Uriah?

Text: 1 Kings 15:5

[read] The writer himself notes the exception, in one verse. He says David did right, and in the same breath adds "except in the matter of Uriah the Hittite" in 1 Kings 15:5. So the measure is not a sinless man. The other verses show what is being measured: a heart turned to the LORD alone, set against kings who turned to other gods. "he was not wholeheartedly devoted to the LORD his God, as his father David had been" in 1 Kings 11:4. God says David "followed Me with all his heart" in 1 Kings 14:8, speaking to Jeroboam of worship. When David was found out, he confessed.

[read] That is this study's reading, from these verses. It does not lighten Uriah's death. Nor is it the writer's whole account of David: 2 Samuel 24:10 records another sin, and 1 Kings 15:5 does not name it. Only One meets every measure with no exception written beside it, and the close of this study comes to Him.

## Question planned: If it was from the LORD, was Rehoboam still to blame?

Text: 1 Kings 12:15

[read] Yes. The verse says both in one sentence, and this site does not choose between them: "the king did not listen to the people, and indeed this turn of events was from the LORD" in 1 Kings 12:15. The account treats the harsh answer as Rehoboam's: "the king answered the people harshly" in 1 Kings 12:13, and Chronicles tells it the same way, "the king answered them harshly" in 2 Chronicles 10:13, with the same two truths in 2 Chronicles 10:15. The Baptist Faith and Message says God's knowledge extends to all things, "including the future decisions of His free creatures". Article 2 is speaking of God, not of Rehoboam, but it holds those decisions to be real and free, as this verse does.

[read] How the two fit together is not spelled out in this passage. Baptists differ on some details of how God's purpose and human choice relate, chiefly on election, and this page does not settle those. It says only what the verse says.

[source:jfb] Jamieson, Fausset and Brown read the phrase as God's governing hand over a real human failure: "That was the overruling cause".

[source:mhcc] Matthew Henry's Concise Commentary says, "God serves his own wise and righteous purposes by the imprudences and sins of men". Neither is a Baptist writer. Only their notes on this passage were read.

## Question census: Who moved David to count the people, God or Satan?

Text: 2 Samuel 24:1

[read] Both accounts are true, and this page takes them together. 2 Samuel 24:1 says the LORD's anger burned and "He stirred up David against them". 1 Chronicles 21:1 says "Satan rose up against Israel and incited David". James 1:13 says God does not tempt anyone. In both accounts David confesses it as his own sin: "I have sinned greatly" in 2 Samuel 24:10 and in 1 Chronicles 21:8.

[source:jfb] Jamieson, Fausset and Brown explain it as permission: "He permitted Satan to tempt David", and God "is frequently described in Scripture as doing what He merely permits to be done".

[source:mhcc] Matthew Henry's Concise Commentary says, "For the people's sin David was left to act wrong". That is close, and it puts the weight on David being left to himself, not on Satan. No Baptist commentary on these verses was read, so these are two older explanations, set side by side, and this study does not choose between their wording.

[read] Whatever the explanation, neither verse corrects the other, and neither takes the sin from David.

## Question ahab: Was Ahab's humbling real?

Text: 1 Kings 21:27–29

[read] This is the place where two old commentators part, and the text leaves more than either says. The LORD Himself says Ahab "has humbled himself before Me" in 1 Kings 21:29, and He acts on it. So this study does not call it nothing. The next chapter shows no changed king: he says of a prophet "I hate him" in 1 Kings 22:8, and sends the man to prison in 1 Kings 22:27. So this study does not call it a settled change of life either. It reads it as a real humbling, which the LORD honoured by delay, and which the account does not say lasted.

[source:jfb] Jamieson, Fausset and Brown say it led, "for a while, to sincere repentance".

[source:mhcc] Matthew Henry's Concise Commentary says, "his heart was unhumbled and unchanged", and that "Ahab's repentance was only what might be seen of men". The verses do not say that in so many words. This commentary reads his conduct in the next chapter back into this one.

[read] Notice what that tells you. God's mercy reached even as far as this man, and even as a delay. It gave Naboth nothing back, and the Baptist Faith and Message's words on repentance, "a genuine turning from sin toward God", are a higher thing than this passage reports of him.

## Question manasseh: Did Manasseh's prayer undo what he had done?

Text: 2 Kings 24:3–4

[read] The account says two things, and both hold. The LORD "received his plea" in 2 Chronicles 33:13. And long after, the writer of Kings says Judah fell "because of the sins of Manasseh", and "for the innocent blood he had shed", in 2 Kings 24:3–4. Mercy to the man did not bring back those who died, or the son in the fire, or undo the people he had led. His own son "did evil in the sight of the LORD, as his father Manasseh had done", in 2 Kings 21:20.

[read] "the LORD was unwilling to forgive" in 2 Kings 24:4 is said of what was coming on Judah in its last days. Nothing there takes back what Chronicles says of the man's prayer. This page does not explain why Kings leaves the prayer out, because the writer gives no reason. Kings does not deny it.

[care] If someone who hurt you has since repented, this passage does not tell you that the harm is over or that you must act as if it is. The account of Manasseh kept the blood on the record after it recorded the prayer.

[source:jfb] Jamieson, Fausset and Brown read it as true repentance, in these words: "He humbled himself, repented, and prayed". They add that some political motive doubtless moved the Assyrian king. This page does not take that up, because the text does not say it.

## Question today: Can I use these verdicts on a leader today?

Text: 1 Timothy 2:1–2

[read] Not as verdicts. The line "in the sight of the LORD", as in 2 Kings 21:2, is written by an inspired writer about a finished reign. None of us holds that seat. You may and should weigh what a leader does by what Scripture says about justice, and you can ask the questions on this page of any account you read, including who paid. But this page compares no present leader with any king, and it has no party.

[read] The New Testament tells you what to do about rulers. Pray: "for kings and all those in authority" in 1 Timothy 2:2. Submit: "Everyone must submit himself to the governing authorities" in Romans 13:1. And when the two collide, Peter and the other apostles answered their rulers: "We must obey God rather than men" in Acts 5:29. The Baptist Faith and Message says it is the duty of Christians "to render loyal obedience thereto in all things not contrary to the revealed will of God". It also calls Christians to provide for "the needy, the abused".

[care] Where a person in authority over you is harming you or someone else, nothing here asks you to stay silent. Tell someone who can act.

## Close: The next reign you read

Close: Nearly every ruler on the two beams has a line beside their name, and {{rightNoted}} of the {{right}} who did right have a note beneath it: the high places stayed. Even the {{rightPlain}} without that note have longer stories than one line tells. Of Josiah the writer says he "did not turn aside to the right or to the left" in 2 Kings 22:2, and that echo of Deuteronomy 17:20 is this study's own hearing of it.
Way: Take three questions to the next reign you read, and to anyone's account of how someone used power. Who says so: the ruler, a prophet or adviser, or the writer? Who paid for it, and does the account name them? What came after, in that reign and in the later books that remember it?
Invite: The kings could not carry the measure. Deuteronomy 17:20 asks for a king whose heart "will not be exalted above his countrymen", and Matthew 20:28 says of Jesus that He "did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many". The angel says "His kingdom will never end" in Luke 1:33. Over Him the voice from heaven says "in You I am well pleased" in Luke 3:22, with no qualification on the line beneath. The elders told Rehoboam to "be a servant to these people" in 1 Kings 12:7, so that they would serve him. Jesus does not cite them, and He goes further: He gives His life. Setting the two side by side is this study's own. The Baptist Faith and Message says, "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord." If you have never turned from your sin and trusted Him, that is the choice this page leaves with you.
