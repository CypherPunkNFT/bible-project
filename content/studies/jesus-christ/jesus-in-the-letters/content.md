# Jesus in the letters

**Study 20 · Jesus Christ · First content draft · 2026-10-13**

The letters speak of Jesus while addressing the life of believing communities. They proclaim his place in creation, his coming in flesh, his death and resurrection, and his continuing work. Those statements ground appeals to humility, perseverance, prayer, love, and hope.

Read each passage within its argument. Ask what it says about Christ, what he has done or continues to do, and why the readers are being reminded of it here. These six readings move from his preeminence to his promised coming, keeping worship and daily faith connected.

## 01 He is before all things

**Main readings:** Colossians 1:9–19; Hebrews 1:1–14.

### By him, and for him

Colossians moves from prayer and thanksgiving into an account of the Son in whose kingdom believers have been placed. Redemption and forgiveness lead into the description of him as the image of the invisible God and the firstborn of every creature. The following explanation matters: all things in heaven and earth, visible and invisible, were created by him and for him.

> And he is before all things, and by him all things consist.

Colossians 1:17 · KJV

Read “firstborn” within this whole statement rather than isolating it from the account of creation. The passage attributes creation’s existence, purpose, and continued coherence to him. It then names him head of the body, the church, and firstborn from the dead, stating the purpose that he have preeminence in all things.

The one to whom this community belongs is therefore not presented as one spiritual power among competing powers. Thrones, dominions, principalities, and powers are themselves included among the things created by him and for him. The scope of the description reaches far beyond the community’s immediate circumstances.

### Spoken unto us by his Son

Hebrews opens with God’s former speech through the prophets and his speaking in these last days by the Son. The Son is named heir of all things and the one through whom God made the worlds. He is described as the brightness of God’s glory, the express image of his person, and the one upholding all things by his powerful word.

This opening also speaks of purging sins and sitting at the right hand of the Majesty on high. Creation and salvation belong in the same introduction. The following Scripture citations distinguish the Son from the angels and speak of his throne, enduring life, and place at God’s right hand.

These descriptions prepare the call at the beginning of chapter 2 to attend closely to what has been heard. Who the Son is makes neglect of the message serious.

**Consider:** How do the statements following “firstborn” explain the Son’s relation to creation? Why does Hebrews begin its appeal by describing the one through whom God speaks?

**Reflect:** Consider how these passages enlarge an understanding of Jesus limited to the circumstances in which you first learned about him.

## 02 Let this mind be in you

**Main readings:** Philippians 2:1–18; Hebrews 2:9–18.

### The form of a servant

Philippians calls readers away from strife and vainglory and toward lowliness of mind and concern for others. The account of Christ follows within that appeal. Paul does not pause his concern for the community to introduce an unrelated subject: Christ’s mind is central to the life he is asking them to share.

The passage speaks of Christ in the form of God, then of making himself of no reputation, taking the form of a servant, and being made in the likeness of men. It continues to his humbling himself in obedience unto death, even the death of the cross.

These are not merely general words of encouragement to be modest. Paul grounds his appeal in Christ’s particular action. The movement reaches the cross, making the community’s rivalry and self-importance answerable to the one it confesses.

### Jesus Christ is Lord

The passage does not end with humiliation. God highly exalts him and gives him the name above every name, so that every knee should bow and every tongue confess Jesus Christ as Lord to the Father’s glory. His service and his lordship belong to the same account.

Paul then returns to his readers’ obedience, speaking of God working in them to will and do his good pleasure. The instruction about life together is sustained by what God does, not only by admiration for an example beyond reach.

Hebrews likewise holds glory and shared humanity together. Jesus tastes death, shares in flesh and blood, and is not ashamed to call those sanctified his brethren. His sharing is connected to deliverance and to being a merciful and faithful high priest. The letters do not treat his exalted place as a reason to regard human suffering as beneath his concern.

**Consider:** What community problem introduces Philippians 2’s account of Christ? How do service, obedience, exaltation, and the readers’ conduct remain connected?

**Reflect:** Identify one relationship in which attention to Christ’s mind would challenge strife or the pursuit of recognition.

## 03 Having made peace through the blood of his cross

**Main reading:** Colossians 1:12–23.

### Yet now hath he reconciled

The description of Christ’s preeminence moves into reconciliation. Colossians names the blood of his cross as the means of making peace, then addresses the readers directly. They were alienated and enemies in their minds by wicked works; now they have been reconciled in the body of his flesh through death.

The change is more than a new attitude toward an inspiring teacher. The text locates reconciliation in Christ’s death and describes an altered relation to God. Its purpose is their presentation as holy, unblameable, and unreproveable in his sight.

Keep the concrete language of flesh, blood, and death beside the earlier statements about creation. The one before all things is the one through whose cross peace is made. The passage does not separate a lofty account of his identity from a bodily death of little consequence.

### Grounded and settled

The statement about the readers’ presentation leads into a condition: continuing in faith, grounded and settled, and not moved away from the hope of the gospel. Assurance concerning Christ’s work and the call to continue both belong to the paragraph.

Paul identifies himself as a minister of this gospel. The readers are being directed to remain with the message they have heard, rather than to treat reconciliation as permission to disregard its hope. The instruction follows the work of Christ; it is not presented as a replacement for that work.

The opening prayer helps explain the practical aim. Paul asks for a worthy walk, fruitfulness in good work, growth in knowing God, and strength for patience with joy. The account of reconciliation gives substance to the life for which he prays. It is a message to people whose conduct and endurance still matter.

**Consider:** Where does the passage locate the making of peace? How does the call to continue relate to the description of what Christ has already done?

**Reflect:** Consider which part of the gospel’s hope you need to attend to in order to remain grounded rather than easily moved.

## 04 We have a great high priest

**Main readings:** Hebrews 2:14–18; 4:14–5:10.

### Yet without sin

Hebrews speaks of Jesus sharing flesh and blood and being made like his brethren in order to be a merciful and faithful high priest. Because he has suffered being tempted, he is able to help those who are tempted. His relation to weakness is part of the explanation of his help.

Chapter 4 names him Jesus the Son of God, passed into the heavens, and calls readers to hold fast their profession. It does not present his heavenly place as remoteness from their need. He can be touched with the feeling of their infirmities; he was tempted as they are, yet without sin.

The qualification matters. His compassion does not depend upon having committed the same wrongs as those he helps. The passage holds tested humanity and sinlessness together.

### Come boldly unto the throne of grace

> Let us therefore come boldly unto the throne of grace, that we may obtain mercy, and find grace to help in time of need.

Hebrews 4:16 · KJV

The invitation follows the account of the high priest. Confidence in approaching God rests on who he is, and the purpose is mercy and timely help. It is not a demand to demonstrate strength before drawing near.

Chapter 5 explains priestly appointment, then identifies Christ as called by God. It speaks of his prayers, tears, suffering, and learned obedience. Read that language alongside the explicit statement that he is without sin. Learning obedience here should not be turned into a story of Jesus first being disobedient and then correcting a moral fault.

The passage leads to his being the author of eternal salvation and a priest after the order of Melchisedec. The fuller priestly argument develops later in Hebrews; this reading establishes the reason for holding fast and coming near.

**Consider:** What grounds the invitation to approach? How does the account distinguish sharing weakness from committing sin?

**Reflect:** Consider whether a sense of need has become a reason to stay away from the very mercy this passage invites you to seek.

## 05 Who also maketh intercession for us

**Main readings:** Romans 8:26–39; Hebrews 7:23–28.

### Who is he that condemneth?

Romans 8 moves from the Spirit’s help in weakness and God’s purpose to a series of questions about opposition, accusation, condemnation, and separation. Paul answers the question about condemnation by naming Christ’s death, resurrection, heavenly place, and intercession:

> Who is he that condemneth? It is Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again, who is even at the right hand of God, who also maketh intercession for us.

Romans 8:34 · KJV

Christ’s work is not described only in the past tense. The one who died and rose also intercedes. The surrounding argument names the Father giving the Son and God justifying. It does not depict Christ persuading an otherwise unwilling God to begin caring for his people.

The passage also distinguishes the Spirit’s intercession earlier in the chapter from Christ’s intercession here. Both appear in the account of God’s help; the references should not be silently treated as the same sentence about the same person.

### He ever liveth

Hebrews contrasts priests prevented by death from continuing with Christ’s enduring priesthood. It states the consequence:

> Wherefore he is able also to save them to the uttermost that come unto God by him, seeing he ever liveth to make intercession for them.

Hebrews 7:25 · KJV

The following verses distinguish his holiness and once-given self-offering from repeated sacrifices. His continuing intercession is not presented as his having to die again. The passage grounds confidence in the living priest and the offering he has made.

Return to Romans’ list of tribulation, distress, persecution, famine, danger, and death. Paul’s assurance does not depend upon those things never happening. His conclusion is that no creature can separate believers from God’s love in Christ. The comfort concerns their relation to him within and beyond suffering, rather than exemption from every hardship.

**Consider:** What past and present actions appear in Romans 8:34? Why does Hebrews connect continuing life with the ability to save?

**Reflect:** Consider how this assurance differs from measuring God’s love solely by whether present circumstances are easy.

## 06 So shall we ever be with the Lord

**Main reading:** 1 Thessalonians 4:13–5:11.

### Concerning them which are asleep

Paul addresses sorrow over believers who have died. He does not simply forbid grief; he distinguishes it from the sorrow of those without hope. The reason begins with Jesus’ death and resurrection. The future of those who sleep in Jesus is connected to the risen Lord.

Paul says that believers alive at the Lord’s coming will not precede those who have died. He describes the Lord descending, the dead in Christ rising first, and the living being caught up together with them to meet him. The culminating promise is continuing presence with the Lord.

The instruction that follows identifies the use of this teaching:

> Wherefore comfort one another with these words.

1 Thessalonians 4:18 · KJV

Keep that concern in view when reading the details. Paul is assuring the community about its dead and its shared future, not merely supplying material for a timetable.

### Let us watch and be sober

The following passage speaks of the day of the Lord coming as a thief and calls the readers children of light. The response is watchfulness and sobriety, with faith, love, and the hope of salvation. The future shapes present conduct without supplying a date to calculate.

Paul again grounds hope in Christ, who died so that they should live together with him. He closes this unit with renewed instruction to comfort and build one another up. Expectation is a shared responsibility: believers use the promise to strengthen others.

Read the return of the Lord alongside the other descriptions in this study. The one awaited is the one through whom creation exists, who humbled himself, made peace through the cross, and lives to intercede. Hope concerns belonging to him, rather than fascination with future events detached from his person.

**Consider:** What concern begins the passage and what instruction concludes each unit? How does expectation become something believers do for one another?

**Reflect:** Consider how you could speak these promises with patience and care to someone grieving, without treating sorrow as failure.

The letters direct readers to Christ as creator, servant, reconciler, high priest, intercessor, and returning Lord. Each description enters the life of a community: how it loves, continues, prays, faces accusation, and comforts its members. Read who he is together with what the passage asks his people to receive and do.
