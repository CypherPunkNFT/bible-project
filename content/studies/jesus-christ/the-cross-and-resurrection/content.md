# The cross and resurrection

**Study 19 · Jesus Christ · First content draft · 2026-10-13**

The accounts of Jesus’ death and resurrection contain actions, testimony, and explanations of what these events mean. Jesus speaks of his body and blood before his arrest. The narrators describe his death and burial, the discovery at the tomb, and encounters with him alive. The apostles proclaim these events as the gospel they received and preached.

Follow each account before drawing its details together with another. Notice who acts, who sees, who reports, and who explains. This study moves through six readings, from the final meal to Paul’s account of why resurrection matters for faith, forgiveness, and hope.

## 01 This is my body

**Main readings:** Mark 14:12–31; Luke 22:14–20.

### With you before I suffer

Mark describes the preparation of the Passover and Jesus’ coming with the twelve. During the meal he announces that one who eats with him will betray him. The disciples’ sorrowful questions place the coming betrayal within their own company. Jesus speaks both of what is written concerning the Son of man and of the betrayer’s responsibility.

Luke records Jesus’ desire to eat this Passover with the apostles before he suffers. The meal is therefore not detached from what follows. His words anticipate suffering while also looking toward fulfilment in the kingdom of God. The impending loss is real, but it is not the only future of which he speaks.

### Shed for many

Jesus takes, blesses, breaks, and gives the bread, identifying it with his body. He gives the cup with thanksgiving and explains it:

> And he said unto them, This is my blood of the new testament, which is shed for many.

Mark 14:24 · KJV

Luke records the body given for them, the command to do this in remembrance of him, and the cup as the new testament in his blood. Keep the words of each account visible. Together they show Jesus giving his approaching death meaning before the disciples witness it.

After the meal in Mark, Jesus speaks of the disciples scattering and of meeting them in Galilee after he is risen. Peter insists he will not deny him, and the others also pledge loyalty. Read these promises beside the events that follow: the narrative will expose the distance between their confident words and their strength under pressure.

**Consider:** How does Jesus connect the meal to both suffering and the kingdom? What does he say about his death before anyone at the table has witnessed it?

**Reflect:** Attend to the words Jesus gives the meal rather than allowing familiarity with the scene to replace listening.

## 02 Nevertheless not what I will

**Main reading:** Mark 14:32–52.

### Abba, Father

In Gethsemane, Jesus takes Peter, James, and John with him and speaks of sorrow unto death. He goes forward to pray that the hour might pass if possible. Mark gives the content of the prayer:

> And he said, Abba, Father, all things are possible unto thee; take away this cup from me: nevertheless not what I will, but what thou wilt.

Mark 14:36 · KJV

The request and the submission belong within the same prayer. Jesus’ obedience is not narrated as an absence of anguish. Nor does addressing the Father make the hour unreal. Read his stated sorrow and willingness together without supplying an imagined account of every thought behind them.

He returns to find the disciples sleeping, urges watchfulness and prayer, and goes again to pray the same words. Their inability to watch stands beside his continued prayer. Mark lets the repeated actions show the difference.

### The hour is come

Judas arrives with an armed company from the chief priests, scribes, and elders. His kiss identifies Jesus. Someone standing nearby strikes the high priest’s servant; Mark does not name the sword-bearer in this account. Jesus contrasts the armed arrest with his daily public teaching in the temple and speaks of the Scriptures being fulfilled.

The disciples then forsake him and flee. Their earlier assurances have not prevented their failure. The scene moves from the prayer concerning the hour to Jesus’ announcement that it has come, and from promised loyalty to abandonment.

This reading concerns Jesus’ particular path to arrest and death. Its application begins by attending to his prayer and the disciples’ need for watchfulness, not by treating every threatening demand made by another person as though it were the Father’s command.

**Consider:** What changes between the prayer about the hour and the announcement that it has come? How does the repeated sleeping relate to the disciples’ earlier promises?

**Reflect:** Consider whether confidence in your own resolve has displaced the watchfulness and prayer Jesus tells his disciples they need.

## 03 The King of the Jews

**Main readings:** Mark 14:53–72; 15:1–41; John 19:28–37.

### Accused and condemned

Mark describes conflicting testimony before the high priest and council. When asked whether he is the Christ, the Son of the Blessed, Jesus answers and speaks of the Son of man at the right hand of power and coming with heaven’s clouds. The council condemns him. Outside, Peter denies knowing him and then remembers Jesus’ warning and weeps.

Before Pilate, the question concerns the King of the Jews. Mark names accusations, the choice of Barabbas, the crowd’s demand, and Pilate’s willingness to satisfy them. Pilate orders Jesus scourged and delivered for crucifixion. The soldiers then use royal clothing, a crown, and salutes in their mockery.

Keep responsibility attached to the people and actions the account identifies. The narrative does not make an entire people across generations responsible for what particular authorities, participants, and soldiers do.

### Gave up the ghost

The accusation above the cross reads “THE KING OF THE JEWS.” The mockers call for Jesus to come down so they may see and believe. Mark then records darkness, Jesus’ cry of forsakenness, his death, the torn temple veil, and the centurion’s declaration that he was the Son of God.

John gives another saying at the point of death:

> When Jesus therefore had received the vinegar, he said, It is finished: and he bowed his head, and gave up the ghost.

John 19:30 · KJV

Read these sayings in their respective accounts rather than constructing an unlabelled sequence from several Gospels. John also reports the soldiers finding Jesus already dead, the piercing of his side, and testimony given so readers may believe. He identifies the fulfilment of Scripture in what occurred.

The accounts present an actual death, not merely an apparent defeat or a lesson about courage. Their explanations of fulfilment and identity stand alongside the narrated suffering, rather than replacing it.

**Consider:** How is kingship used in accusation, mockery, and the inscription? What does each narrator place around the moment of death?

**Reflect:** Read one account through without interruption, noticing details that a familiar combined retelling may have caused you to overlook.

## 04 He is risen; he is not here

**Main readings:** Mark 15:42–16:8; Luke 24:1–12; John 19:38–20:10.

### Beheld where he was laid

Joseph of Arimathaea asks Pilate for Jesus’ body. In Mark, Pilate inquires of the centurion concerning the death before granting the body. Joseph wraps it in linen and lays it in a rock-hewn tomb. Mary Magdalene and Mary the mother of Joses see where he is laid.

John includes Nicodemus bringing spices and describes a new tomb in a garden near the place of crucifixion. These details belong to John’s account; they should not be attributed to Mark simply because the narratives can be read alongside one another.

Burial connects the death with the later discovery. The accounts identify people who act and places they observe. The subsequent announcement concerns the same Jesus who was crucified and laid in the tomb.

### Remember how he spake

In Mark, the women arrive with spices and find the stone rolled away. A young man in white tells them that Jesus, who was crucified, has risen. They are sent to tell the disciples and Peter about Galilee, as Jesus had said. Their immediate response is fear and flight.

Luke names two men in shining garments and records the call to remember Jesus’ words about death and rising. The women remember and report to the apostles, who initially disbelieve. John follows Mary Magdalene’s report that the body has been taken, then Peter and the other disciple’s visit to the tomb.

Do not force these discoveries into a detailed itinerary the individual accounts do not supply. Nor should the empty place alone be made to carry every part of the announcement: the words spoken there interpret the absence, recall Jesus’ promise, and direct the witnesses onward. Confusion and fear appear in the accounts before fuller understanding.

**Consider:** Who observes the burial in Mark? How does remembering Jesus’ earlier word contribute to understanding the discovery?

**Reflect:** Distinguish what a witness first observes from the explanation subsequently given. Let that distinction guide your reading of each account.

## 05 It is I myself

**Main readings:** Luke 24:13–49; John 20:11–31.

### He opened to us the scriptures

On the road to Emmaus, two disciples recount hopes that seem disappointed and reports that have astonished them. Jesus, whom they do not recognise, addresses their slowness to believe the prophets and explains the things concerning himself in the Scriptures. Recognition comes at the meal, and they return to Jerusalem.

Luke does not give a complete list of the passages Jesus expounded. Preserve the claim that he explained the Scriptures without presenting a reconstructed list as a transcript of that conversation.

When Jesus stands among the gathered disciples, they think they see a spirit. He directs attention to his hands and feet, invites touch, and eats before them. He then opens their understanding of Scripture and speaks of suffering, rising, and the preaching of repentance and remission of sins. The witnesses receive both an encounter and instruction concerning its meaning.

### My Lord and my God

John follows Mary’s recognition when Jesus speaks her name, her report to the disciples, and his appearance to them. Thomas is absent and later refuses their testimony unless he sees and touches the wounds. When Jesus appears again, he addresses Thomas’s stated demands.

> And Thomas answered and said unto him, My Lord and my God.

John 20:28 · KJV

John reports the invitation to touch, but does not explicitly say Thomas did so. His confession and Jesus’ blessing upon those who believe without seeing lead into the Gospel’s stated purpose for its written signs.

These appearances are not narrated merely as the disciples finding renewed courage or keeping a memory alive. Jesus speaks, is recognised, shows his wounds, and commissions witnesses. Their fear, hesitation, joy, and confession remain part of the testimony rather than being replaced with effortless certainty from the beginning.

**Consider:** How do the encounters and the explanation of Scripture work together in Luke? What does John actually report Thomas doing?

**Reflect:** Consider how the written witness addresses someone who was not present at these encounters.

## 06 According to the scriptures

**Main reading:** 1 Corinthians 15:1–28.

### That which I also received

Paul recalls the gospel he preached, which the Corinthians received and in which they stand. He identifies what he delivered as something he also received:

> For I delivered unto you first of all that which I also received, how that Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures;

1 Corinthians 15:3 · KJV

Death, burial, resurrection on the third day, and appearances belong to his summary. “For our sins” interprets the death; “according to the scriptures” places the events within Scripture’s witness. The appearances include Cephas, the twelve, more than five hundred at once, James, all the apostles, and finally Paul himself.

This is Paul’s reported witness list, not a complete chronology of every Gospel appearance. His own place in it leads him to speak of persecuting the church and of God’s grace. The message is shared: whether preached by him or by the other apostles, this is what the Corinthians believed.

### Christ the firstfruits

Paul then addresses the claim that there is no resurrection of the dead. He argues through its consequences: if Christ has not risen, the preaching and faith are vain, the witnesses misrepresent God, believers remain in their sins, and those who have died in Christ are lost.

His answer is a declaration, not merely a wish:

> But now is Christ risen from the dead, and become the firstfruits of them that slept.

1 Corinthians 15:20 · KJV

He connects Christ’s resurrection with those who belong to him at his coming. The argument reaches toward the defeat of enemies, including death, and the kingdom delivered to the Father. Resurrection is therefore essential both to the gospel already preached and to the future hope of believers.

The good news concerns what Christ has done for sins and what God has done in raising him. Its hope reaches beyond the present life. Paul does not allow resurrection to become an optional illustration attached to a message that would mean the same without it.

**Consider:** Which statements report events, and which explain their significance? Why does denying resurrection unravel more than one part of Paul’s message?

**Reflect:** Consider what changes when Christian hope is grounded in the risen Christ rather than only in encouragement for the present.

The meal anticipates the death; the burial follows it; the tomb announcement and appearances testify to resurrection. The apostles proclaim these events as saving news and the beginning of a hope that includes the defeat of death. Read the testimony and its explanation together, attending to what each writer actually says.
