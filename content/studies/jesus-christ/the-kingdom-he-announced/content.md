# The kingdom he announced

Subtitle: God's rule, here now and still to come [proposed: the row leaves the subtitle blank]

Concepts: Reign · Nearness · Seed · Delay

Motto: A small seed. A shut door. / Near now, coming still.

Lead: In the Bible a kingdom is not mainly a piece of land. It is a reign: God being King and his will being done. Jesus' first preaching in Mark is that this reign has come near. This study teaches one habit. Before you decide what a kingdom saying means, listen for its tense.

> “The time is fulfilled,” He said, “and the kingdom of God is near. Repent and believe in the gospel!”

Mark 1:15 · BSB

Frame: People hear the phrase *kingdom of God* in many ways. Some take it as another name for heaven after death. Some take it as the church. Some take it as a fairer world that good people build. Each of these holds a piece of the truth. None of them is the whole. Jesus speaks of the kingdom in more than one tense. It *is near*, it *has come upon you*, it *is in your midst*, and at the end of the age it *will be like* a door that opens and then shuts. If you notice the tense first, many sayings that seem to clash turn out to fit together.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. The Gospels speak of the kingdom many times. This study walks seven passages, chosen because together they show the King, the announcement, the present reign and the future one, and the way a person comes in. It does not walk the Sermon on the Mount (read *Following Jesus and the Sermon on the Mount*), the Lord's Prayer (read *Prayer across Scripture: learning to pray*), Jesus' night talk with Nicodemus (read *Encounters with Jesus*), the sheep and the goats (read *The hope to come*), or the book of Daniel (read *Reading Daniel and Revelation*). It quotes them only where it needs a verse.

## 01 "Your God reigns": the King and the hope

**Main readings:** Isaiah 52:7–10; with Psalms 93, 97 and 99, and Daniel 2 and 7

Hook: A runner crosses the hills toward a ruined city, shouting four words.

### God already reigns

The kingdom does not begin with Jesus. The Bible's songbook calls God King over everything he has made. Three psalms open with the same shout:

> The LORD reigns! He is robed in majesty; the LORD has clothed and armed Himself with strength. The world indeed is firmly established; it cannot be moved.

Psalm 93:1 · BSB

> The LORD reigns, let the earth rejoice; let the distant shores be glad.

Psalm 97:1 · BSB

> The LORD reigns; let the nations tremble! He is enthroned above the cherubim; let the earth quake!

Psalm 99:1 · BSB

(LORD in small capitals stands for God's personal name, which the Hebrew Bible gives him; *cherubim* are the winged creatures pictured around his throne.) These psalms say God reigns *now*, over the whole world. So when the Bible longs for God's kingdom to come, it does not mean God is not yet King. It means the world does not yet live as if he were. His rule is disputed. Psalm 96, which belongs here too, is read in *God: who He is and what He does*.

### The runner on the mountains

Isaiah 40–55 speaks comfort to God's people after Jerusalem has fallen and many have been carried away to Babylon. In chapter 52 a messenger runs toward the city:

> How beautiful on the mountains are the feet of those who bring good news, who proclaim peace, who bring good tidings, who proclaim salvation, who say to Zion, “Your God reigns!”

Isaiah 52:7 · BSB

Zion is a name for Jerusalem, and for the people of God who belong there. The good news is not advice. It is news that something has happened: God is acting as King again. The city's watchmen pick up the cry, because "every eye will see when the LORD returns to Zion" (Isaiah 52:8). The ruins themselves are told to sing, "for the LORD has comforted His people; He has redeemed Jerusalem" (Isaiah 52:9). To *redeem* is to buy back or set free. Then the circle widens:

> The LORD has bared His holy arm in the sight of all the nations; all the ends of the earth will see the salvation of our God.

Isaiah 52:10 · BSB

A bared arm is an arm rolled up for work. God's reign shows itself in what he does, and the whole world will see it.

[read] Jewish readers have heard this passage first as God's promise to bring his people home and to reign from Zion, a hope still awaited. That hope runs through Jewish prayer. The Kaddish, a prayer said in synagogue services and by mourners, asks that God establish his kingdom in the lifetime of those praying [check: wording of the Kaddish line in a standard prayer book]. The Mishnah, an early collection of rabbinic teaching, calls the daily saying of the Shema ("Hear, O Israel") accepting "the yoke of the kingdom of heaven" [check: Mishnah Berakhot 2:2, exact wording]. Among the Dead Sea Scrolls, a text known as 11QMelchizedek quotes Isaiah 52:7 [check: confirm citation and what the text says of the messenger]. In Jesus' day, then, Jewish people were already longing for the reign Isaiah announced.

### A kingdom that will stand forever

Daniel, written to Jews living under foreign empires, sees the empires fall and God's reign remain:

> In the days of those kings, the God of heaven will set up a kingdom that will never be destroyed, nor will it be left to another people. It will shatter all these kingdoms and bring them to an end, but will itself stand forever.

Daniel 2:44 · BSB

In another vision Daniel sees "One like the Son of Man coming with the clouds of heaven", brought before God, the Ancient of Days:

> And He was given dominion, glory, and kingship, that the people of every nation and language should serve Him. His dominion is an everlasting dominion that will not pass away, and His kingdom is one that will never be destroyed.

Daniel 7:14 · BSB

*Son of Man* will matter. It is the title Jesus uses for himself more than any other. Read more in *Reading Daniel and Revelation*.

[read] Christians read Isaiah's good news and Daniel's everlasting kingdom as fulfilled in Jesus. That is a Christian reading, given by the New Testament: Paul quotes Isaiah 52:7 of those who preach about Christ (Romans 10:15). Isaiah 52 itself does not name Jesus, and the passage should first be heard as what it says to its own hearers.

**Consider:** What does a runner announce that a teacher does not? Why does Isaiah widen the scene from Jerusalem to "all the ends of the earth"?

**Reflect:** If God already reigns, where in your life do you act as if he did not?

## 02 "The time is fulfilled": the announcement

**Main readings:** Mark 1:14–15

Hook: John is in prison. Jesus begins to preach anyway.

### News first, then a call

Mark's account of Jesus' public work begins in two verses:

> After the arrest of John, Jesus went into Galilee and proclaimed the gospel of God.

Mark 1:14 · BSB

*Gospel* is an old English word for "good news". It is the same idea as Isaiah's runner. Jesus is not first giving a lesson or a rule. He is announcing what God is doing.

The setting is not gentle. John the Baptist, who prepared the way, has been arrested. The announcement of God's reign begins while the powers of this world are still locking up God's messengers. The kingdom is near, and opposition is real, in the same sentence.

Then the announcement itself. It has two statements and two commands:

- "The time is fulfilled": the moment the prophets waited for has arrived.
- "The kingdom of God is near": God's reign has come close, right up to the hearers.
- "Repent": to repent is to turn around, a change of mind that becomes a change of direction.
- "Believe in the gospel": to believe here is to trust the news and rest your weight on it.

Notice the order. The news comes before the command. People are not told to repent so that the kingdom will come. They are told the kingdom has come near, and so they should turn and trust.

[read] Mark goes straight on to Jesus calling four fishermen, who leave their nets to follow him (Mark 1:16–20). The kingdom is announced, and people's lives move. Read more in *Following Jesus and the Sermon on the Mount*.

### What the King came to do

Luke tells how Jesus, in the synagogue at Nazareth, read from Isaiah and said the words were fulfilled that day:

> “The Spirit of the Lord is on Me, because He has anointed Me to preach good news to the poor. He has sent Me to proclaim liberty to the captives and recovery of sight to the blind, to release the oppressed, to proclaim the year of the Lord’s favor.”

Luke 4:18–19 · BSB

"The year of the Lord's favor" echoes the Jubilee of Leviticus 25, the year when debts were cancelled and land returned. Read more in *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice*.

[open] What does "near" mean in Mark 1:15: already arrived, or about to arrive? Readers differ. The rest of this study suggests why both are true: in Jesus the reign has come close enough to touch, and its fullness is still ahead.

**Consider:** What does Jesus announce before he commands anything? Why might Mark mention John's arrest first?

**Reflect:** Have you heard Christianity mainly as rules, or as news? What would change if you heard it as news first?

## 03 "The kingdom of heaven is like": seeds, weeds and treasure

**Main readings:** Matthew 13:1–58

Hook: The crowd wanted a king. Jesus told them about a farmer.

### Why Matthew says "heaven"

Matthew usually writes *kingdom of heaven* where Mark and Luke write *kingdom of God*. The two mean the same reign. Many explain Matthew's wording by the Jewish custom of speaking reverently about God, using "heaven" in place of his name. That is a common explanation, not something Matthew states.

### The sower

Jesus sits in a boat while a crowd stands on the shore (Matthew 13:1–2), and tells "many things in parables" (Matthew 13:3). A *parable* is a short story taken from ordinary life that makes a point about God's ways. A farmer scatters seed. Some falls on a path, some on rocky ground, some among thorns, and some on good soil that produces "a hundredfold, sixtyfold, or thirtyfold" (Matthew 13:8).

Jesus explains it himself. The seed is "the message of the kingdom" (Matthew 13:19). The soils are hearers. Some do not understand, and "the evil one comes and snatches away what was sown". Some receive it with joy and fall away "when trouble or persecution comes". In others "the worries of this life and the deceitfulness of wealth choke the word" (Matthew 13:19–22). The good soil is "the one who hears the word and understands it" (Matthew 13:23).

So the kingdom does not arrive in the story like an army. It arrives like seed, and it can be ignored, choked or snatched away.

### Why parables?

The disciples ask why he speaks this way. His answer is hard:

> He replied, “The knowledge of the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven has been given to you, but not to them.

Matthew 13:11 · BSB

A *mystery* in the New Testament is not a puzzle. It is something God had kept hidden and now makes known. Jesus quotes Isaiah 6: "You will be ever hearing but never understanding" (Matthew 13:14). Parables open the kingdom to those who come close and ask, and hide it from those who have already decided. The disciples are not cleverer. They are told "blessed are your eyes because they see" (Matthew 13:16), and that prophets longed to see what they see (Matthew 13:17).

### Weeds among the wheat

An enemy sows weeds in a man's field. The servants want to pull them up. The owner says no: "Let both grow together until the harvest" (Matthew 13:30).

Later, in the house, Jesus explains. The sower is "the Son of Man", and

> The field is the world, and the good seed represents the sons of the kingdom. The weeds are the sons of the evil one,

Matthew 13:38 · BSB

The harvest is "the end of the age" (Matthew 13:39). Then "the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father" (Matthew 13:43).

[read] Two things follow. First, the field is the world, as Jesus says; it should not quietly be turned into the church. Second, God's reign in this age is patient. Good and evil grow side by side for now, and it is not the servants' job to sort the world. That patience is not indifference. The harvest is coming.

### Mustard seed and leaven

> He put before them another parable: “The kingdom of heaven is like a mustard seed that a man planted in his field.

Matthew 13:31 · BSB

It is tiny, and it becomes a tree where birds nest (Matthew 13:32). Leaven (yeast) hidden in "three measures of flour" works through all of it (Matthew 13:33). A Galilean preacher and a dozen followers do not look like an everlasting kingdom. These two parables say: do not judge the end by the beginning.

[other] Some readers take the leaven as a picture of evil spreading through the professing church, since leaven elsewhere often stands for corruption. The note at Matthew 13:33 in the Scofield Reference Bible reads it so [check: Scofield note at Matthew 13:33, edition and wording]. Most readers take the plain sense: the kingdom grows from small to great, as with the mustard seed beside it. This study follows that reading because the two parables are paired and both open with "The kingdom of heaven is like".

### Treasure and pearl

A man finds treasure in a field, "and in his joy he went and sold all he had and bought that field" (Matthew 13:44). A merchant finds "one very precious pearl" and sells everything to buy it (Matthew 13:46). The point is worth, and joy. Neither man feels he is making a sacrifice. These parables do not put a price on entry. They show what the kingdom is worth to someone who has seen it.

### The net

A net catches "all kinds of fish". When it is full, the good are kept and the bad thrown away. "So will it be at the end of the age" (Matthew 13:47–49). As with the weeds, the sorting is done by angels, at the end, not by us now.

### Old and new treasure

Jesus asks, "Have you understood all these things?" (Matthew 13:51). Then he says that every scribe (a teacher of God's law) who has been taught about the kingdom is like a homeowner who "brings out of his storeroom new treasures as well as old" (Matthew 13:52). The old is the Scriptures and the hope you met in part 01. The new is that the King has come.

### The chapter ends on hard ground

Matthew closes the chapter in Jesus' own town. People are astonished and then offended: "Isn't this the carpenter's son?" (Matthew 13:55). "And He did not do many miracles there, because of their unbelief" (Matthew 13:58). The parable of the sower has just happened in front of the reader. The seed fell, and the ground was hard.

[read] The parables here are read more fully, one by one, in the site's study of the parables.

**Consider:** Which parables are about hearing, which about growth or worth, and which about the end? Where does Jesus give the explanation himself?

**Reflect:** Which soil describes what usually happens to God's word in you this week: snatched, scorched, choked, or rooted?

## 04 "The kingdom of God has come upon you": the present reign

**Main readings:** Luke 11:14–23; Luke 17:20–21

Hook: A man who could not speak starts talking. Now the crowd has to explain it.

### Two explanations for one healing

Jesus drives out a demon (an evil spirit) that kept a man mute, and the man speaks (Luke 11:14). The crowd is amazed. Some offer an explanation: "It is by Beelzebul, the prince of the demons, that He drives out demons" (Luke 11:15). Beelzebul is a name for Satan, the chief of evil spirits. Others demand "a sign from heaven" (Luke 11:16), as if what they had just seen were not one.

Jesus answers with plain logic. "Every kingdom divided against itself will be laid waste" (Luke 11:17). If Satan were throwing out his own servants, his kingdom would be falling apart. Then the real explanation:

> But if I drive out demons by the finger of God, then the kingdom of God has come upon you.

Luke 11:20 · BSB

"The finger of God" echoes Egypt, where Pharaoh's magicians, beaten by the plagues, said, "This is the finger of God" (Exodus 8:19). Matthew's account of the same saying speaks of "the Spirit of God" (Matthew 12:28). Each Gospel keeps its own wording; both point to God's own power.

Notice the tense. Not "will come". *Has come upon you.*

### The stronger one

A strong man guards his house (Luke 11:21). But "when someone stronger attacks and overpowers him", he takes away the man's armour "and then he divides up his plunder" (Luke 11:22). Satan is the strong man. Jesus is the stronger one. Every person set free is plunder taken back.

> He who is not with Me is against Me, and he who does not gather with Me scatters.

Luke 11:23 · BSB

[read] There is no neutral ground here. Jesus does not let the crowd stand back and admire. If his works are God's reign breaking in, then a person is either gathering with him or scattering.

[care] This passage tells of one deliverance and the argument about it. It gives no one the right to label an illness, a disability or a mental health struggle as demonic, or to diagnose anyone from their symptoms. Nor does it promise that every sickness will be healed now; the kingdom's fullness is still to come. If you or someone near you is unwell, a doctor is no betrayal of faith. Read more in *What Scripture promises the sick* and *Angels, demons and spiritual powers*.

### "In your midst"

Some Pharisees (a Jewish group devoted to careful keeping of God's law) ask Jesus when the kingdom of God will come.

> When asked by the Pharisees when the kingdom of God would come, Jesus replied, “The kingdom of God will not come with observable signs. Nor will people say, ‘Look, here it is,’ or ‘There it is.’ For you see, the kingdom of God is in your midst.”

Luke 17:20–21 · BSB

They are watching for signs. Jesus tells them the kingdom is already standing among them, because the King is.

[open] The Greek phrase behind "in your midst" can also be translated "inside you", and some older translations read it so. The Berean's "in your midst" fits the setting well: Jesus is answering people who were not his followers, and he does not usually say God's reign is inside those who reject him. Either way the point holds. Do not look for the kingdom somewhere else. Look at Jesus.

[read] Straight after this, Jesus tells his disciples that days are coming when they will long to see "one of the days of the Son of Man" (Luke 17:22). The present reign does not cancel the future one. Luke puts them side by side.

**Consider:** What two explanations for the healing does the crowd give? What does Jesus say the deliverance means?

**Reflect:** Is there something Jesus has plainly done that you have decided in advance not to let mean anything?

## 05 "Like a little child": how the kingdom is received

**Main readings:** Luke 18:15–17; with John 3:3–5

Hook: The disciples were guarding the door. Jesus opened it to the smallest people in the room.

### Babies at the door

> Now people were even bringing their babies to Jesus for Him to place His hands on them. And when the disciples saw this, they rebuked those who brought them.

Luke 18:15 · BSB

Luke's word is *babies*. They cannot recite anything, achieve anything or promise anything. The disciples seem to think Jesus' time is for more important people. Jesus calls the children to him:

> But Jesus called the children to Him and said, “Let the little children come to Me, and do not hinder them! For the kingdom of God belongs to such as these.

Luke 18:16 · BSB

Then he turns the scene into a lesson for adults:

> Truly I tell you, anyone who does not receive the kingdom of God like a little child will never enter it.

Luke 18:17 · BSB

### What "like a little child" means

Look at the verb: *receive*. A baby does not earn being held. A baby receives. The point is not that children are sinless or always sweet. The passage says neither. The point is how they come: empty-handed, dependent, taking what is given.

Luke puts this scene straight after the story of a Pharisee who listed his good deeds and a tax collector who could only ask for mercy (Luke 18:9–14). The tax collector went home right with God. Read more in *Saved by grace: how God forgives and changes us*. Both scenes say the same thing from two sides: the kingdom is not entered by credentials.

The Baptist Faith and Message 2000 uses the very picture. Its article IX (the kingdom) calls the kingdom "the realm of salvation into which men enter by trustful, childlike commitment to Jesus Christ" [check: exact wording at bfm.sbc.net].

### A new birth

John's Gospel says it another way. To Nicodemus, a respected teacher, Jesus says:

> Jesus replied, “Truly, truly, I tell you, no one can see the kingdom of God unless he is born again.”

John 3:3 · BSB

Nicodemus asks how a grown man can be born (John 3:4). Jesus answers, "no one can enter the kingdom of God unless he is born of water and the Spirit" (John 3:5). A child receives; a newborn has done nothing at all. Both pictures put the entry in God's hands. Read more in *Encounters with Jesus*.

**Consider:** Who blocks the way in Luke 18, and who clears it? Why might Luke place this scene after the Pharisee and the tax collector?

**Reflect:** What are you still bringing to God as a qualification: knowledge, reputation, effort? What would it mean to come empty-handed?

## 06 "Keep watch": the kingdom still to come

**Main readings:** Matthew 25:1–30; with Matthew 25:31–46

Hook: The bridegroom is late. The master is gone a long time. What are you doing while you wait?

### Setting

Matthew 24–25 is Jesus' teaching on the Mount of Olives, answering his disciples' question about his coming and "the end of the age" (Matthew 24:3). Chapter 25 is three pictures of that end. This study walks the first two.

### Ten young women and a shut door

> “At that time the kingdom of heaven will be like ten virgins who took their lamps and went out to meet the bridegroom.

Matthew 25:1 · BSB

At a wedding of the time, young women of the household would light the way for the bridegroom's arrival. Here the tense has changed. Not *is near*, not *has come*. "At that time … will be like."

Five bring extra oil and five do not (Matthew 25:3–4). "When the bridegroom was delayed, they all became drowsy and fell asleep" (Matthew 25:5). All of them. The difference is not who stayed awake. It is who was ready.

At midnight the cry comes. The foolish ask to borrow oil, and the wise refuse, "or there may not be enough for both us and you" (Matthew 25:9). This is part of the picture: readiness for the King's coming cannot be borrowed from someone else at the last minute. While they go to buy, "the bridegroom arrived. Those who were ready went in with him to the wedding banquet, and the door was shut" (Matthew 25:10).

> But he replied, ‘Truly I tell you, I do not know you.’

Matthew 25:12 · BSB

> Therefore keep watch, because you do not know the day or the hour.

Matthew 25:13 · BSB

[read] The lesson Jesus draws is not "work out the date". It is the opposite: you cannot know it, so be ready. What the oil stands for, Jesus does not say, and this study will not decide for him. The point is the readiness, and the door.

### Three servants and a long absence

> For it is just like a man going on a journey, who called his servants and entrusted them with his possessions.

Matthew 25:14 · BSB

A *talent* was a large sum of money, a weight of silver worth many years of a labourer's wages [check: value of a talent, commonly given as about 6,000 denarii]. Our English word for an ability comes from this story. The master gives five, two and one, "each according to his own ability" (Matthew 25:15). Two servants put the money to work and double it. The third digs a hole and hides it (Matthew 25:16–18).

"After a long time the master of those servants returned" (Matthew 25:19). To both faithful servants he says the same words, though one gained twice as much as the other:

> His master replied, ‘Well done, good and faithful servant! You have been faithful with a few things; I will put you in charge of many things. Enter into the joy of your master!’

Matthew 25:21 · BSB

The third servant explains himself: "I knew that you are a hard man … So I was afraid" (Matthew 25:24–25). His fear came from a false picture of his master, and it froze him. The master answers him from his own words, and he is thrown "into the outer darkness, where there will be weeping and gnashing of teeth" (Matthew 25:30).

[read] In the story the talents are money. Applying it to time, gifts and opportunities is a fair application, and it is an application. The story does not promise that faithful giving will be repaid in wealth. The confession teaches stewardship, that what we have is entrusted by God, in article XIII (stewardship); it does not teach riches as the reward of faith.

### The King on his throne

The chapter's third picture is the Son of Man, Daniel's figure, now seated "on His glorious throne" with the nations before him (Matthew 25:31–32). The King says to those on his right:

> Then the King will say to those on His right, ‘Come, you who are blessed by My Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world.

Matthew 25:34 · BSB

> And the King will reply, ‘Truly I tell you, whatever you did for one of the least of these brothers of Mine, you did for Me.’

Matthew 25:40 · BSB

Who "the least of these brothers" are, and how this scene fits with salvation by grace, is read in *The hope to come*.

[read] Matthew 25 speaks plainly of a real end: a shut door, outer darkness, "eternal punishment" and "eternal life" (Matthew 25:46). The site holds with article X (last things) of the Baptist Faith and Message 2000 that Christ will return personally and visibly, that the dead will be raised, and that he will judge all people, the unrighteous to everlasting punishment and the righteous to eternal life with him.

[care] If a passage like this leaves you afraid that you are one of the foolish, notice who the warning is for. It is spoken to people who still have time to get oil. Those who have trusted Christ are not kept by their own watchfulness alone; article V of the confession holds that true believers "will never fall away from the state of grace". Read more in *Chosen and kept: can I be sure?* and, if fear will not let go, *Fear, anxiety and despair*.

**Consider:** What do the waiting virgins and the absent master have in common? What false belief froze the third servant?

**Reflect:** If the King came this week, what would he find you doing with what he has entrusted to you?

## Question: Is the kingdom the same thing as the church?

Text: Matthew 13:38

[read] Not quite. The kingdom is God's reign; the church is the people who have come under it and gather to live by it and announce it. In the parable of the weeds Jesus says "The field is the world" (Matthew 13:38), not the church. The Baptist Faith and Message 2000 treats the two in separate articles: article VI (the church) describes local congregations of baptized believers, and article IX (the kingdom) describes God's rule over all and especially over those who willingly own him as King. The church lives under the kingdom and points to it. It is not the whole of it, and it does not bring it in by its own power.

[source:augustine] Augustine of Hippo, in *The City of God* (book 20, chapter 9), says the church even now is in some sense the kingdom of Christ [check: wording of City of God 20.9 in a standard translation]. Roman Catholic teaching today speaks of the church as the seed and beginning of the kingdom on earth (Second Vatican Council, *Lumen Gentium*, section 5) [check: exact wording in the Vatican's English text]. These are their teachings, shown as theirs.

[source:rauschenbusch] Walter Rauschenbusch, a Northern Baptist pastor and professor, taught in *A Theology for the Social Gospel* (1917) that the kingdom is chiefly a just social order that Christians work to bring about [check: summary against the book]. Article IX does call Christians to "pray and to labor that the Kingdom may come" [check: wording], and article XV (the Christian and the social order) calls them to care for the poor and oppressed. But the confession places the kingdom's fullness in Christ's return, not in human progress.

## Question: Is the kingdom here now, or still to come?

Text: Luke 11:20; Matthew 25:13

[read] Both, and the passages say so. "The kingdom of God has come upon you" (Luke 11:20). "Keep watch, because you do not know the day or the hour" (Matthew 25:13). Article IX of the Baptist Faith and Message 2000 says the same: God reigns now, people enter his kingdom now, and "The full consummation of the Kingdom awaits the return of Jesus Christ and the end of this age" [check: exact wording].

[source:scholars] Scholars have pulled this in different directions. Albert Schweitzer, in *The Quest of the Historical Jesus* (German 1906), read Jesus' kingdom as wholly future and expected at once. C. H. Dodd, in *The Parables of the Kingdom* (1935), argued that the kingdom had wholly arrived in Jesus' ministry, a view often called "realized eschatology" (eschatology means teaching about the last things). George Eldon Ladd, a Baptist scholar, argued in *The Presence of the Future* (1974) that the kingdom is both present and future, a view often summed up as "already and not yet" [check: titles, dates and Ladd's denominational standing]. The confession's article IX stands closest to the last of these, because the Gospels keep both tenses.

[open] How the future kingdom unfolds, and whether there is a thousand-year reign of Christ on earth before the final state (the "millennium" of Revelation 20), is a question on which Baptists differ, and the confession does not choose. The views are set out in *The hope to come*.

## Question: Did Jesus expect the end to come at once?

Text: Matthew 25:19

[read] Some have argued that Jesus said the kingdom was "near" (Mark 1:15) and expected the world to end in his own lifetime, and was wrong. The passages walked here answer that. In Luke 17 he says the kingdom is already in their midst. In Matthew 13 the wheat and the weeds grow together until a harvest, and a seed has to grow into a tree. In Matthew 25 "the bridegroom was delayed" (Matthew 25:5) and the master returned "After a long time" (Matthew 25:19). Jesus' own stories build delay into the waiting. What is near is the King, not a date. [check: name the site's apologetics answer on this question, if one exists, and link it here]

## Close: Listening for the tense

Way: You have heard the King announced in the psalms and by Isaiah's runner, the kingdom proclaimed as near, sown as seed, breaking in against the strong man, standing in the Pharisees' midst, given to babies, and waiting behind a door that will one day shut. Here is the habit to carry to the next passage. When you meet a kingdom saying, ask its tense. Is it *has come*, *is near*, *is in your midst*, or *will be like*? Then ask what it asks of you in that tense: to trust what God has already done, to turn toward what is near, or to be ready for what is coming. Most confusion about the kingdom comes from hearing one tense and forgetting the others.

Invite: The announcement has not changed. The time is fulfilled. God's reign has come near in Jesus, who died for sinners and rose again, and will come again as King. You do not enter by credentials. You enter like a child: turn from your sin and trust him. "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord", as article IV (salvation) of the confession puts it. Read more in *The good news: how a person is made right with God*.

Question: Which tense of the kingdom do you live as if it were the only one: the reign that has come, or the King who is coming? And what would change this week if you lived in both?
