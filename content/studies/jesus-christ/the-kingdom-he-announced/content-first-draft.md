# The kingdom he announced

**Study 15 · Jesus Christ · First content draft · 2026-10-13**

Jesus announces the kingdom, teaches its mysteries, welcomes those his disciples would turn away, and speaks of a coming judgment. These passages belong together. The kingdom is not only something awaiting a distant future: Jesus says it has come upon those witnessing his works. Yet he also teaches his disciples to await what they have not seen fulfilled.

Follow the words each passage uses: at hand, receive, enter, like, come upon you, inherit. They show different aspects of the same proclamation without making every saying answer the same question.

## 01 The kingdom of God is at hand

**Main reading:** Mark 1:14–20.

### Repent ye, and believe the gospel

Mark places Jesus’ proclamation after John is put in prison. Jesus comes into Galilee preaching the gospel of the kingdom of God. His announcement concerns a fulfilled time and a kingdom at hand; his hearers are called to respond.

> And saying, The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand: repent ye, and believe the gospel.

Mark 1:15 · KJV

Repentance and belief belong within the announcement itself. Jesus does not present the kingdom merely as information about events elsewhere. Those hearing him must reconsider the direction of their lives and believe the gospel he proclaims. The verse gives urgency without supplying a timetable that a reader can calculate independently of the rest of his teaching.

### Come ye after me

The calling of Simon and Andrew follows immediately. Jesus calls them away from their nets to follow him; James and John then leave their father in the ship with the hired servants. Mark brings the announcement and these responses close together. The kingdom’s nearness is followed by a call that changes what people do.

These particular departures should retain their particular shape. The account does not say every hearer must become a fisherman who leaves a boat. It shows these men responding to Jesus’ authority and promise. Nor does John’s imprisonment disappear when the kingdom is announced. Opposition and the proclamation of good news occupy the same opening scene.

**Consider:** What does Jesus announce before he commands a response? How does the calling of the fishermen develop the announcement?

**Reflect:** Consider where hearing the gospel has become detached from repentance, belief, or following Jesus in practice.

## 02 Receive the kingdom

**Main readings:** Luke 18:15–17; John 3:1–8.

### As a little child

People bring infants to Jesus that he might touch them, and the disciples rebuke them. Jesus calls the children to him and forbids this hindrance. His welcome becomes instruction about receiving the kingdom.

> Verily I say unto you, Whosoever shall not receive the kingdom of God as a little child shall in no wise enter therein.

Luke 18:17 · KJV

The children are not presented with a record of achievement qualifying them to approach. The scene challenges the disciples’ decision about whom to admit. Jesus speaks of receiving, and makes the child the comparison for those who would enter. The passage does not explain this through a claim that children never sin, nor does it catalogue supposedly childlike personality traits. Begin with the welcome Jesus gives and the obstruction he removes.

### Born of the Spirit

Nicodemus approaches Jesus as a teacher who recognises that his signs point to God’s presence. Jesus answers with the necessity of being born again to see the kingdom of God. Nicodemus asks how this can happen; Jesus speaks of being born of water and of the Spirit, distinguishing what is born of flesh from what is born of the Spirit.

This conversation moves the question beyond recognised standing or accumulated knowledge. Nicodemus cannot simply add the kingdom to the things he already possesses. Jesus speaks of a birth he needs. The wind comparison emphasises the Spirit’s working beyond Nicodemus’s ability to explain or control it. Read John’s language alongside Luke’s welcome without treating the two scenes as interchangeable. One concerns children whom disciples hinder; the other a teacher being told of the birth necessary to enter.

**Consider:** Who prevents access in Luke, and who corrects them? What does Nicodemus’s opening statement leave unanswered?

**Reflect:** Examine whether knowledge, reputation, or achievement has become your assumed qualification for receiving what God gives.

## 03 The kingdom of heaven is like

**Main reading:** Matthew 13:1–52.

### Hearing and understanding

Matthew 13 begins with a sower and different receptions of the word. In Jesus’ explanation, trouble, persecution, the care of this world, and the deceitfulness of riches affect what is heard. The fruitful hearer hears and understands. The chapter therefore asks more than whether a story is memorable: what happens to the word in those who hear it?

The disciples also ask why Jesus speaks in parables. His answer includes both the gift of knowing the mysteries of the kingdom and the failure to hear and understand. Parables are not described simply as illustrations that make everything immediately easier. Read the stories with Jesus’ explanations, his stated audiences, and his distinction between hearing and understanding.

### Growth, worth, and the end of the world

The mustard seed and leaven show a small beginning and a far-reaching result. The treasure and pearl show a value that changes the place of everything else a person possesses. These comparisons invite attention to growth and worth; they do not provide a price at which salvation can be bought.

Other comparisons include a separation still to come. Jesus identifies the field in the wheat and tares as the world and the harvest as the end of the world. The net also leads to a separation and judgment. Present mixture does not mean final indifference. Nor should the field silently become the church when Jesus has named it otherwise.

The chapter holds patient growth, costly response, fruitful hearing, and judgment together. No single image should erase the others. Attend first to the comparison the passage makes before assigning a spiritual identity to every incidental detail.

**Consider:** Which parables concern hearing, which concern worth or growth, and which explicitly describe judgment? Where does Jesus supply the interpretation himself?

**Reflect:** Identify one response these comparisons call for: patient trust, reordered priorities, attentive hearing, or taking judgment seriously.

## 04 The kingdom of God is come upon you

**Main readings:** Luke 11:14–23; Matthew 12:22–30.

### A divided kingdom

In Luke, Jesus casts out a devil and the person who had been dumb speaks. Some accuse him of casting out devils through Beelzebub; others seek a sign from heaven. Jesus answers the accusation through the image of a kingdom divided against itself. If Satan is divided against himself, how shall his kingdom stand?

The dispute concerns the source and meaning of what Jesus has done. The crowd has witnessed a work, but witnessing does not produce a single agreed response. The accusation attempts to attribute deliverance to the very power being overcome. Jesus exposes the contradiction and presses the question of God’s action among them.

### The stronger one

> But if I with the finger of God cast out devils, no doubt the kingdom of God is come upon you.

Luke 11:20 · KJV

Matthew’s corresponding saying names the Spirit of God. Keep each account’s wording visible: Luke says “the finger of God”; Matthew says “the Spirit of God.” Both connect Jesus’ casting out devils with the kingdom coming upon his hearers.

The strong man and the stronger one develop the account of overcoming a guarded power. Jesus’ works are not merely advertisements for a kingdom unrelated to them. In his answer they bear witness to God’s kingdom already confronting the power from which people are delivered. His concluding call permits no detached neutrality about him.

This scene does not authorise readers to label every illness demonic or to diagnose people from their symptoms. Its subject is this narrated deliverance and the accusation Jesus answers. Neither should its present language be used to cancel his teaching about a future gathering and judgment.

**Consider:** What alternative explanations are offered for Jesus’ work? What does Jesus himself say the deliverance means?

**Reflect:** Notice the difference between asking what Jesus has done and deciding in advance what his works are allowed to mean.

## 05 How hardly shall they that have riches enter

**Main reading:** Luke 18:9–30.

### He that humbleth himself

Luke introduces the Pharisee and publican as a parable addressed to people who trust in themselves that they are righteous and despise others. One prayer rehearses the speaker’s distinction from other people; the other asks God for mercy. Jesus declares that the publican goes down to his house justified rather than the other.

This is followed by the children’s welcome and then a ruler’s question about inheriting eternal life. Read the sequence without making every person the same example. Self-exaltation, obstructing children, and attachment to riches differ, but each scene unsettles assumptions about who stands well before God. Humility is not another achievement with which to despise someone else.

### Possible with God

The ruler says he has kept the commandments Jesus names from his youth. Jesus tells him of what he still lacks: sell all, distribute to the poor, have treasure in heaven, and follow him. He becomes very sorrowful because he is very rich. The difficulty Jesus names is not imaginary. His comparison of a camel passing through a needle’s eye leads the hearers to ask who can be saved.

> And he said, The things which are impossible with men are possible with God.

Luke 18:27 · KJV

Do not soften the scene into advice to feel differently about possessions while ignoring the command Jesus actually gives this ruler. Equally, do not replace God’s possibility with the notion that someone can purchase entry through a sufficiently large gift. Jesus calls this man to follow him, and his wealth has become a point of sorrow at that call.

Peter then speaks of what the disciples have left. Jesus answers with a promise concerning loss for the kingdom’s sake, present receiving, and everlasting life in the world to come. This is assurance to followers, not a financial return that can be demanded in exchange for giving.

**Consider:** How do the publican’s prayer and the ruler’s question differ? Why does Jesus’ saying about riches provoke the question about salvation?

**Reflect:** Consider both attachment to possessions and the desire to establish your standing by comparison with others.

## 06 Watch therefore

**Main reading:** Matthew 25:1–46.

### Ready when he comes

The ten virgins wait for a bridegroom whose arrival is delayed. All slumber and sleep; the distinction is their readiness when the cry comes. The story’s warning cannot therefore mean that faithful people never literally sleep. It concerns preparation that cannot be supplied at the last moment by another person’s readiness.

> Watch therefore, for ye know neither the day nor the hour wherein the Son of man cometh.

Matthew 25:13 · KJV

The entrusted talents develop another aspect of waiting. A man leaves goods with his servants and returns after a long time to reckon with them. What they do while he is absent matters when he returns. In the story, talents are entrusted money. Applications to other responsibilities should be identified as applications, rather than substituted for the story’s actual goods and accounting.

### Inherit the kingdom

The chapter’s final scene places the Son of man in glory with the nations gathered before him. The King speaks of inheriting the kingdom prepared from the foundation of the world. Feeding, welcoming, clothing, visiting, and their omission figure in his judgment. The people addressed are surprised by his identification with those they served or neglected.

The identity of “the least of these my brethren” requires careful treatment. Readers differ over whether the phrase particularly identifies Jesus’ disciples or has a wider reference to people in need. Keep that question visible without removing the scene’s force: the King regards treatment of those he identifies with as treatment of himself. Acts easily overlooked are not overlooked by him.

Matthew 25 makes waiting answerable to the coming Lord. The kingdom Jesus announces calls for readiness, faithful service, and serious attention to his judgment. Its future remains consequential for the present.

**Consider:** What distinguishes readiness from trying to calculate the arrival? What does the final scene reveal about the King’s attention?

**Reflect:** Consider a responsibility or a person you are tempted to overlook while treating watchfulness as merely thinking about future events.

The announcement begins with repentance and belief. Its teaching leads through receiving, hearing, valuing, following, and waiting. Read these responses together with Jesus’ works and promises: the kingdom has come upon his hearers, and the King still speaks of what is to come.
