# Christ in the Old Testament

**Study 41 · Jesus Christ · First content draft · 2026-11-05**

Two disciples walk away from Jerusalem with their hopes buried. A stranger joins them, and before they know who He is, He takes them back to the books they already own.

> And beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, He explained to them what was written in all the Scriptures about Himself.

Luke 24:27 · BSB

That evening He says it again to the eleven, and names the three parts of their Bible: "Everything must be fulfilled that is written about Me in the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms" (Luke 24:44 · BSB). Luke does not list the passages He used (Study 27, Scripture and its witness, walks the chapter). But the rest of the New Testament keeps returning to the same pictures: a lamb, a temple, a priest, a king, a servant. This study walks those five, then the limits of the method.

The Baptist Faith and Message says it plainly: "All Scripture is a testimony to Christ, who is Himself the focus of divine revelation" (Article I). The question is how to see that testimony without inventing it. Carry three questions through every part:

1. **Where does the New Testament itself draw the line?** Start from a passage where Jesus or an apostle connects the two.
2. **What did it mean for those who first saw it?** A Passover lamb fed a real family on a real night before it pointed anywhere else.
3. **Where does the picture stop?** Every figure is also unlike Him. Jesus said "something greater than the temple is here" (Matthew 12:6 · BSB). The difference matters as much as the likeness.

This is a selection, not a census. Each of the five is a figure the New Testament itself connects with Christ; others are named in part 06.

## 01 The lamb: blood on the door

**Main readings:** Exodus 12:1–13; John 1:29; 1 Corinthians 5:7.

Why would a family in Egypt paint their doorframe red? Because God told them that judgment was coming that night, and that the blood would mark the house He would pass over.

### The first night

Each household killed "an unblemished year-old male" (Exodus 12:5 · BSB), put its blood on the doorposts and ate it dressed to leave. Then came the promise:

> The blood on the houses where you are staying will distinguish them; when I see the blood, I will pass over you. No plague will fall on you to destroy you when I strike the land of Egypt.

Exodus 12:13 · BSB

It was rescue from judgment and the first step out of slavery, kept every year so that children would ask what it meant.

### How the New Testament reads it

John the Baptist sees Jesus and says, "Look, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29 · BSB). Paul is plainer still: "Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7 · BSB). Peter speaks of redemption "with the precious blood of Christ, a lamb without blemish or spot" (1 Peter 1:19 · BSB). John notes that the soldiers did not break Jesus' legs, "so that the Scripture would be fulfilled: 'Not one of His bones will be broken'" (John 19:36 · BSB).

### Where the picture stops

Exodus calls it "the Passover sacrifice to the LORD" (12:27 · BSB), but never a sin offering; its blood marked a house for protection. A lamb that takes sin away draws several pictures together: the Passover, the sacrifices, and the servant "led like a lamb to the slaughter" (Isaiah 53:7 · BSB). Interpreters differ on which John the Baptist chiefly meant; the text does not say. John 19:36 may echo Psalm 34:20, about the righteous man whose bones God protects, as well as the Passover rule (Exodus 12:46); the Berean's own note names both. The connection is sure; the single source is not. Study 12, Names and titles of Jesus, follows the title "Lamb of God" itself.

**Consider:** What did the blood do in Exodus 12, and what does the New Testament say Christ's blood does?

**Reflect:** Israel's children were to ask, "What does this service mean to you?" (Exodus 12:26 · BSB). How would you answer that about the cross?

## 02 The temple: God among His people

**Main readings:** Exodus 25:8–9; 40:34; John 1:14; John 2:19–22.

Where does God live? Israel's answer for centuries was a tent, then a house of stone. John's answer is a person.

### A tent in the camp

At Sinai God gave a reason for the whole tabernacle: "They are to make a sanctuary for Me, so that I may dwell among them" (Exodus 25:8 · BSB). When it was finished, "the cloud covered the Tent of Meeting, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (Exodus 40:34 · BSB). It was not a mistake to be corrected; it was God's own provision for living among a sinful people.

### How the New Testament reads it

> The Word became flesh and made His dwelling among us. We have seen His glory, the glory of the one and only Son from the Father, full of grace and truth.

John 1:14 · BSB

The Berean's note gives another rendering: "and tabernacled among us". John's verb recalls the tent, and the glory that filled it. Later Jesus says, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up again," and John explains: "Jesus was speaking about the temple of His body" (John 2:19, 21 · BSB). Notice when they understood: "After He was raised from the dead, His disciples remembered that He had said this. Then they believed the Scripture" (John 2:22 · BSB). As on the Emmaus road, the resurrection opened their eyes to read.

### Where the picture stops

The line does not end at Jesus. Paul tells a church, "you yourselves are God's temple" (1 Corinthians 3:16 · BSB), and John's last vision sees "no temple in the city, because the Lord God Almighty and the Lamb are its temple" (Revelation 21:22 · BSB). The temple is fulfilled in Christ, then in His people joined to Him, and at last in God's presence filling everything. Keep all three.

**Consider:** What did the tabernacle exist for, according to Exodus 25:8? How does John 1:14 answer the same need?

**Reflect:** If God's dwelling is now among His people, what does that ask of the church you belong to?

## 03 The priest: one who goes in for the people

**Main readings:** Leviticus 16:15–16; Psalm 110:4; Hebrews 7:23–27; 10:1–12.

Once a year one man went behind the curtain, carrying blood that was not his own. Everyone else waited outside.

### The day of atonement

On that day Aaron was to "slaughter the goat for the sin offering for the people and bring its blood behind the veil" (Leviticus 16:15 · BSB), to make atonement for the sanctuary and for "all their sins" (16:16). The priest stood between a holy God and a sinful people, and did it again the next year.

### How the New Testament reads it

Hebrews is the one book that calls Jesus high priest, and it argues from two Old Testament texts. The first is the law itself, which it calls "only a shadow of the good things to come, not the realities themselves" (Hebrews 10:1 · BSB), adding that "it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins" (10:4). The second is Psalm 110:4: "You are a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek" (BSB), the king-priest who met Abraham with bread and wine (Genesis 14:18).

> Unlike the other high priests, He does not need to offer daily sacrifices, first for His own sins and then for the sins of the people; He sacrificed for sin once for all when He offered up Himself.

Hebrews 7:27 · BSB

### Where the picture stops

Look at the first word of that verse: "Unlike". Hebrews reads the priesthood as much by contrast as by likeness. The old priests died; Jesus "lives forever" (7:24). They offered again and again; He offered once and "sat down" (10:12). They brought an animal's blood; He brought His own. Hebrews also argues from what Genesis leaves unsaid: Melchizedek appears "without father or mother or genealogy", and so is "like the Son of God" (7:3 · BSB). Some readers have taken Melchizedek to be an appearance of the Son Himself; Hebrews says "like", and the safer reading keeps its word.

**Consider:** List the differences Hebrews 7:23–27 draws between Jesus and Aaron's line. Which one matters most to you?

**Reflect:** Hebrews says He "always lives to intercede" (7:25 · BSB). What would change if you prayed as someone who has a priest?

## 04 The king: a son for David's throne

**Main readings:** 2 Samuel 7:12–16; Matthew 22:41–46; Hebrews 1:5.

David wanted to build God a house. God answered that He would build David one: a family, and a throne that would not end.

### The promise to David

> And when your days are fulfilled and you rest with your fathers, I will raise up your descendant after you, who will come from your own body, and I will establish his kingdom.

2 Samuel 7:12 · BSB

The son would build a house for God's Name, and God would "establish the throne of his kingdom forever" (7:13). Solomon rightly saw the first fulfilment in himself: "I have built the house for the Name of the LORD" (1 Kings 8:20 · BSB). But Solomon died, the kingdom split, and by the exile the throne was empty. The prophets and psalms kept the promise alive.

### How the New Testament reads it

Gabriel tells Mary that God will give her son "the throne of His father David" and that "His kingdom will never end" (Luke 1:32–33 · BSB). Jesus Himself presses the Pharisees with Psalm 110:1: "If David calls Him 'Lord,' how can He be David's son?" (Matthew 22:45 · BSB). The promised son is greater than his father. Peter makes the same argument from David's psalms in Acts 2, which Study 37, Long Memory, walks in full.

### Where the picture stops

Read Hebrews 1:5 beside 2 Samuel 7:14. Hebrews quotes, "I will be His Father, and He will be My Son" (BSB). It does not quote the next words in 2 Samuel: "When he does wrong, I will discipline him" (7:14 · BSB). That clause belonged to David's sons who sinned, and it was fulfilled in them. The New Testament does not apply every line to Christ; it shows which part reaches Him.

**Consider:** What did Solomon fulfil in 2 Samuel 7, and what could he not fulfil?

**Reflect:** Jesus is David's son and David's Lord. Which of those do you find easier to live under?

## 05 The servant: wounded for others

**Main readings:** Isaiah 52:13–53:12; Acts 8:30–35; 1 Peter 2:22–25.

A traveller from Ethiopia is reading Isaiah in his chariot, and cannot tell who it is about.

### The servant in Isaiah

The servant songs in Isaiah 42 to 53 move in a surprising direction. In one, God calls the servant "Israel" (Isaiah 49:3 · BSB); a few lines later the servant is sent "to bring Jacob back to Him" and to be "a light for the nations" (49:5–6 · BSB). Then the last song describes one who suffers for the very people who turned away:

> But He was pierced for our transgressions, He was crushed for our iniquities; the punishment that brought us peace was upon Him, and by His stripes we are healed.

Isaiah 53:5 · BSB

His life is made "a guilt offering" (53:10), and He "bore the sin of many" (53:12).

### How the New Testament reads it

The Ethiopian asks Philip, "who is the prophet talking about, himself or someone else?" and Philip "began with this very Scripture and told him the good news about Jesus" (Acts 8:34–35 · BSB). Peter writes Isaiah's words straight into his letter: "He Himself bore our sins in His body on the tree... 'By His stripes you are healed'" (1 Peter 2:24 · BSB). Matthew also uses Isaiah 53:4 for Jesus' healing of the sick (Matthew 8:17), so the song is read for His ministry as well as His death.

### Where the picture stops

Not everyone reads the song this way. Many Jewish interpreters, including the medieval commentator Rashi, read the servant as the people of Israel suffering among the nations. The Christian reading follows Philip, and rests on the song itself: the servant suffers for "us", and "we all like sheep have gone astray" (53:6 · BSB); He "had done no violence, nor was any deceit in His mouth" (53:9 · BSB). Isaiah says it of one who is not like the rest.

**Consider:** Who are "we" and who is "He" in Isaiah 53:4–6?

**Reflect:** Peter uses this song to teach household servants how to bear unjust suffering (1 Peter 2:18–23). Where are you being asked to entrust yourself to Him who judges justly?

## 06 The line and its limits: shadow, pattern, example

**Main readings:** Colossians 2:16–17; Romans 5:14; 1 Corinthians 10:1–11; 2 Kings 18:4.

If these five point to Christ, does everything? The New Testament gives its own words for how far to go.

### The New Testament's own words

> These are a shadow of the things to come, but the body that casts it belongs to Christ.

Colossians 2:17 · BSB

A shadow is real and has the shape of what casts it. Paul calls Adam "a pattern of the One to come" (Romans 5:14 · BSB); the Greek word there, *typos*, is where "type" comes from. He says Israel's wilderness years "happened to them as examples and were written down as warnings for us" (1 Corinthians 10:11 · BSB). Peter says the water of Noah's flood "symbolizes the baptism that now saves you" (1 Peter 3:21 · BSB). Jesus Himself draws lines from Jonah (Matthew 12:40), from the bronze snake (John 3:14) and from the manna (John 6:32–35). The New Testament names more than five figures.

### When a picture becomes the point

The bronze snake shows the danger. God gave it to heal (Numbers 21:8–9). Centuries later Hezekiah "demolished the bronze snake called Nehushtan that Moses had made, for up to that time the Israelites had burned incense to it" (2 Kings 18:4 · BSB). A figure that points to God is not to be worshipped for itself, or loved more than what it shows.

### Readings that go further

Early Christians often went beyond the lines the New Testament draws. The letter known as First Clement, written from Rome and usually dated to the end of the first century, says the scarlet cord Rahab hung from her window showed "that through the blood of the Lord there shall be redemption" (1 Clement 12:7, J. B. Lightfoot's translation). It is a moving thought; Joshua 2 does not say it. Later interpreters argued over the boundary. The Anglican bishop Herbert Marsh, as Fairbairn quotes him, held that only what Christ or the apostles expressly called a type should be called one. The Scottish theologian Patrick Fairbairn answered that Scripture gives principles as well as cases, and that readers may follow those principles further, with care. Baptist preachers and teachers today stand on both sides of that question, and this study does not choose between them. Both sides share this study's first question: begin where the New Testament draws the line, and label anything further as your own reading.

**Consider:** What is the difference between a shadow (Colossians 2:17) and a hidden code?

**Reflect:** Is there a good gift of God, a habit, a tradition or a symbol, that you have begun to love more than the One it points to?

## Read the Old Testament with Him

The disciples on the Emmaus road said afterwards, "Were not our hearts burning within us as He spoke with us on the road and opened the Scriptures to us?" (Luke 24:32 · BSB). You can read the same books with the same three questions: where the New Testament draws the line, what the passage meant first, and where Christ is greater.

**Your question:** When you next read a passage of the Old Testament, which of these five, the lamb, the temple, the priest, the king or the servant, do you most need to see fulfilled in Him today, and why?

Read the passage again before you answer.
