## Direction

Teach one habit: **draw only the lines the New Testament draws, then look for where each picture stops.** The page draws those lines literally. One direction was built, because the text already existed.

The shape comes from the text, not from a layout. On the evening of the resurrection Jesus names the three parts of His disciples' Bible: "the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms" (Luke 24:44). The five figures the study walks reach all three: the lamb, the tabernacle and the day of atonement lie in the Law; the promise to David and the servant song lie in the Prophets (Samuel and Kings stand among the Prophets in the order the Hebrew Bible keeps); Psalm 110 and Psalm 2 lie in the Psalms. Paul's word for the whole method is a shadow: "the body that casts it belongs to Christ" (Colossians 2:17). A shadow has the shape of what casts it, and it also ends somewhere. So the page draws lines, and marks where they stop.

## The page

- **Opening.** The study trail (Study / Jesus Christ / Christ in the Old Testament). The concepts in gold capitals are the three parts Jesus names: The Law of Moses · The Prophets · The Psalms. The title in ink, *Old Testament* as the gold italic turn. The lead is the study's own opening, the two disciples walking away from Jerusalem, with Luke 24:27 printed in full. Beside it, in a faint circle and dotted orbit: the road to Emmaus at evening, a village on a rise with one lit window, and in front an unrolled scroll whose columns fall into three groups. No figure on the road: no face or figure of Christ. The motto, from Luke 24:27: "Beginning with Moses / and all the Prophets". Under the hero, a bar of four true counts, each with what it means: the five figures; the number of lines the New Testament itself draws on this page; the three parts of Luke 24:44, all reached; the verses printed in full.
- **00 The lines it draws.** The frame paragraphs (Luke 24:44, the Baptist Faith and Message's "All Scripture is a testimony to Christ"). The three questions are drawn as the anatomy of one line: question 2 (what it meant first) sits at the Old Testament end, question 1 (where the New Testament draws the line) on the arc, question 3 (where the picture stops) at the bar where the line ends. Then the map: the Old Testament across the top in Luke 24:44's three parts, the New Testament along the bottom in canonical order, and one curve for each place where a New Testament verse draws a line to an Old Testament one. Five buttons pick a figure; a sixth shows the lines part 06 names. Pointing at a curve shows both verses' words on the line beneath; choosing it opens the verse. Where the New Testament's source is uncertain the curve is dashed and says so (John 19:36 may echo Exodus 12:46 or Psalm 34:20; John 1:29 may draw on more than one lamb). "This is a selection, not a census" sits under it, in the study's words. On a phone the map is left out and the same lines are listed by figure.
- **01–05 The five figures.** Each part opens with its number, a small drawing (a doorframe marked on the posts and lintel; the tent with the cloud over it; the veil with the censer; a crown; a chariot wheel with an open scroll), its title, the hook in italic and its main readings. Then the reading in one column, with the passages printed in full, and beside it **the line** for that figure: the Old Testament phrase at the top ("Then"), the New Testament phrases that draw the line, and the stop at the end, each opening its verse. The stops are proved by script: Exodus 12 never calls the Passover a sin offering; Hebrews 7:27 begins "Unlike"; Hebrews 1:5 quotes 2 Samuel 7:14 and stops before "When he does wrong"; the servant song's last word is "the light of life". The temple's line does not stop at Christ: it runs on to the church (1 Corinthians 3:16) and to the city with no temple (Revelation 21:22), and is drawn that way. The sub-heads carry the same three marks as the anatomy in 00. Part 05 adds "How Jewish readers read the song" under its own mark, before the Christian reading, as the text orders it. Consider and Reflect close each part.
- **06 The line and its limits.** The New Testament's own words for the method (shadow, pattern, example), the six further lines it draws (Adam, the sea, the flood, Jonah, the bronze snake, the manna), the bronze snake that became an idol (2 Kings 18:4), and First Clement's scarlet cord drawn as a dotted line that reaches no New Testament verse, labelled as a reading beyond the text. Marsh's narrower rule and Fairbairn's wider principle stand side by side, not chosen.
- **Close.** The three questions once more, Luke 24:32, the question for the reader's own life, "Read the passage again before you answer.", and Luke 24:13–35 printed in full beside them. Then a table at evening (a lamp, a broken loaf, the scroll), the sources, and a link back to Jesus Christ.
- **Every reference** opens its verses in a sheet, in the Berean; the reference goes in the address.

## Colour

The Prophets' blue. The study's question is how the Old Testament speaks ahead of Christ; Jesus names the Prophets in both Luke 24:27 and 24:44, and two of the five figures lie among them. The site's gold marks the concepts and the notes still to be checked.

## Doctrine and care

- The site's voice follows the Baptist Faith and Message 2000, Article I: "All Scripture is a testimony to Christ, who is Himself the focus of divine revelation." Christ's once-for-all sacrifice (Article IV) stands behind parts 01, 03 and 05.
- Only lines the New Testament itself draws are drawn as solid lines. Where its source is uncertain the line is dashed and labelled. A reading beyond the New Testament (First Clement) is dotted and labelled as such. The page never draws a line of its own.
- Part 05 gives the Jewish reading of Isaiah 53 (the servant as Israel, after Rashi) first and on its own terms, then the Christian reading with its reasons from the song. The guilt for Christ's death is never laid on the Jewish people; the page keeps the study's sentence that Philip and Peter were Jews reading their own Scriptures.
- Where Baptists differ (how far typology may go beyond what the New Testament names) both views are shown and neither is chosen.
- No face or figure of God, of Christ or of the Father is drawn. Melchizedek and the servant are not drawn either.
- Two claims in part 05 still carry the writer's "[check: …]" notes (David Kimchi's reading, and the Targum's wording at Isaiah 52:13). They are not link notes, so content.md was not changed; the page shows them as notes still to be checked. They need checking before release, with the open items in [passage-notes.md](passage-notes.md).
