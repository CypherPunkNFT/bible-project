# The life of Jesus across the Gospels

Four accounts. Shared events. Distinct witnesses.

Matthew, Mark, Luke and John tell of Jesus' ministry, death and resurrection, but they do not all begin at the same point or give every event the same space. Reading across them can reveal shared material and details that one account develops more fully. Reading each account continuously also matters: the position of an event and what surrounds it contribute to its meaning.

This study follows six broad movements. They are a guide to reading, not a claim that every scene can be placed on one certain calendar. Where accounts differ in arrangement or leave an interval unstated, keep that visible rather than silently supplying a sequence.

## 01 Birth and beginnings

**Read:** Matthew 1:18–2:23; Luke 2:1–20, 41–52; Mark 1:1–8; John 1:1–18.

### Matthew and Luke

Matthew's account attends to Joseph's response to Mary's pregnancy, the naming of Jesus and the interpretation of Emmanuel. Chapter 2 follows the wise men, Herod's hostility, the flight into Egypt and the family's later settlement in Nazareth. The narrative repeatedly connects what happens with Scripture. — Matthew 1:18–2:23

Luke's birth scene includes the journey to Bethlehem, the manger, the shepherds and the angelic announcement. Later in the chapter, the twelve-year-old Jesus remains at the temple during a visit to Jerusalem; Mary and Joseph find him among the teachers. Luke concludes that episode with his return to Nazareth and growth. — Luke 2:1–20, 41–52

These are distinct selections. The shepherds in Luke and the wise men in Matthew should not automatically be placed in one scene. Matthew describes the wise men entering a house; Luke describes the newborn laid in a manger. Neither account supplies every detail needed to date the interval between those visits.

### Mark and John begin differently

Mark begins with the gospel of Jesus Christ and the appearance of John baptising in the wilderness. It moves rapidly toward Jesus' baptism and public ministry. It does not supply a birth narrative. — Mark 1:1–9

John begins with the Word, creation, life and light, and the Word becoming flesh. John the Baptist appears as a witness rather than the source of the light. This opening tells the reader how the Gospel presents Jesus before the first encounters unfold. — John 1:1–18

The absence of a birth narrative in Mark or John is not an account of a different birth. It is a difference in what each text chooses to narrate. Begin by identifying what is present before explaining what is absent.

**Consider:** Which people and events receive attention in Matthew and Luke? What do Mark and John place at their beginnings instead?

**Reflect:** Read one opening without importing details from the others. Note what it particularly asks you to understand about Jesus.

## 02 Baptism and wilderness

**Read:** Mark 1:9–13; Matthew 3:13–4:11; Luke 3:21–22; 4:1–13.

### At the Jordan

Mark records Jesus coming from Nazareth to be baptised by John, the opened heavens, the descending Spirit and the heavenly voice. Matthew also includes John's objection and Jesus' answer about fulfilling righteousness. Luke mentions that Jesus was praying when the heavens opened. — Mark 1:9–11; Matthew 3:13–17; Luke 3:21–22

These details can be compared without requiring each narrator to say everything the others say. The shared scene includes testimony concerning Jesus and the Spirit; each account gives its own telling of the actions around it.

### In the wilderness

Mark's account is brief: the Spirit drives Jesus into the wilderness, where he is tempted by Satan for forty days, is with wild beasts and is ministered to by angels. Matthew and Luke narrate exchanges between Jesus and the tempter, including Jesus' answers from Scripture. — Mark 1:12–13; Matthew 4:1–11; Luke 4:1–13

The order of the narrated temptations differs. Both Matthew and Luke begin with bread. Matthew then gives the temple scene before the mountain and kingdoms; Luke gives the kingdoms before the temple in Jerusalem. Preserve the order in each account when reading it. A merged sequence would conceal a visible difference.

The baptism and wilderness stand at the threshold of the public ministry in these accounts. The testimony concerning the Son is followed by testing, not an immediate account of uninterrupted public acclaim.

**Consider:** What does each baptism account add? How do the lengths and sequences of the wilderness accounts differ?

**Reflect:** Compare the temptation passages in their own order. Identify what each ending leaves before the reader, without claiming to know an unstated reason for the arrangement.

## 03 Galilee

**Read:** Mark 1:14–39; Luke 4:16–30; John 2:1–13.

### Proclamation, calling and works

Mark places Jesus' arrival preaching in Galilee after John's imprisonment. The proclamation concerns the kingdom of God and calls for repentance and belief. It is followed by the calling of Simon, Andrew, James and John. — Mark 1:14–20

> And saying, The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand: repent ye, and believe the gospel.

Mark 1:15 · KJV

In Capernaum, Mark brings together teaching, deliverance, healing and prayer. The crowd's attention grows, but Jesus tells the disciples that he will go to other towns to preach. The passage gives a picture of ministry that cannot be reduced to popularity or to one kind of activity. — Mark 1:21–39

### Different scenes of beginning

Luke gives extended attention to Jesus reading in the Nazareth synagogue and announcing fulfilment of the Scripture. The response moves through wonder and questioning into hostility as the exchange continues. Early ministry already includes rejection. — Luke 4:16–30

John narrates the wedding at Cana, where water becomes wine, as the beginning of Jesus' signs, manifesting his glory and prompting the disciples' belief. It then records a stay at Capernaum and a journey to Jerusalem for Passover. Galilee is important, but John's account is not confined there until a single final journey. — John 2:1–13

These passages offer entry points into a much larger ministry. The teaching, encounters and works deserve their own close readings; the purpose here is to recognise how each account introduces their movement and significance.

**Consider:** What activities does Mark place together? How do the responses in Nazareth and Cana differ?

**Reflect:** Follow one short sequence through a Gospel rather than selecting only its best-known event. What do the scenes before and after it contribute?

## 04 The journey to Jerusalem

**Read:** Luke 9:51–56; 13:22; 19:1–10. Compare John 7:1–10; 10:22–23; 12:1–16.

### A direction named by Luke

Luke marks a deliberate turn toward Jerusalem as the time approaches for Jesus to be received up. The following scene involves a Samaritan village that does not receive him and Jesus' rebuke of the disciples' proposed response. The journey includes instruction about how they are to act, not merely changes of location. — Luke 9:51–56

> And he went through the cities and villages, teaching, and journeying toward Jerusalem.

Luke 13:22 · KJV

Near the end of this movement, Luke narrates the encounter with Zacchaeus in Jericho. It connects Jesus' visit with salvation and the Son of Man's purpose to seek and save the lost. A person met on the way becomes part of the narrative's account of his mission. — Luke 19:1–10

### More than one visit in John

John names journeys and locations in connection with different feasts. Chapter 7 describes Jesus going to the feast of tabernacles; chapter 10 places him in Jerusalem at the feast of dedication, in winter. Chapter 12 approaches the Passover and the entry into Jerusalem. — John 7:1–10; 10:22–23; 12:1–16

A broad reading route toward the final events must leave room for these earlier visits. Luke's journey section should not be turned into proof that Jesus had never been in Jerusalem during his ministry before. Nor do its movement notices supply a date and route for every intervening scene.

**Consider:** How does Luke use the journey to carry encounters and teaching? What does John's mention of feasts contribute to the account of Jesus' movements?

**Reflect:** Mark the explicit location and time notices in one passage. Keep them separate from any connecting steps you would have to infer.

## 05 The final week

**Read:** Mark 11:1–19; 14–15. Compare John 12:1–16; 13:1–5.

### Entry and conflict

Mark describes Jesus' entry amid public praise, his arrival at the temple and a return to Bethany because it is late. The temple action follows on the next day in Mark's narration. John records the entry after the Bethany meal and Mary's anointing, looking back on how the disciples understood these events after Jesus was glorified. — Mark 11:1–19; John 12:1–16

The existing Gospel comparisons also draw attention to the placement of the anointing: John narrates it before the entry, while Matthew and Mark narrate it later. Narrative placement and a reconstructed date must not silently be treated as the same thing. Read each account's transitions before combining them.

### The meal, arrest, death and burial

Mark's account of the final meal includes Passover preparation, the announcement of betrayal, bread and the cup, and words concerning Jesus' body and blood. John gives extended attention to the meal's setting in Jesus' love for his own and his washing of the disciples' feet. These selections do not exhaust either account. — Mark 14:12–26; John 13:1–5

Follow Mark 14–15 through Gethsemane, arrest, questioning, Peter's denial, the hearing before Pilate, crucifixion, death and burial. The narrative does not move directly from acclaim to resurrection; it gives sustained attention to abandonment, violence and Jesus' death.

Mark identifies women who had followed and served Jesus, watching from a distance. It describes Joseph of Arimathaea requesting the body, Pilate checking the death with the centurion, and the burial in a rock-cut tomb. The women see where Jesus is laid. These people and actions connect the death and burial with what follows. — Mark 15:40–47

**Consider:** Which transitions in Mark explicitly indicate sequence? Who remains present or takes action after Jesus' death?

**Reflect:** Read the burial account slowly. Notice what it reports before moving to the next chapter, and distinguish its details from those remembered from another Gospel.

## 06 Resurrection appearances

**Read:** Mark 16:1–8; Matthew 28; Luke 24; John 20–21.

### Discovery, message and response

Mark records the women finding the stone moved and hearing that Jesus has risen. The selected passage ends with trembling, amazement and fear. Matthew records fear and great joy, the women meeting Jesus, and the instruction concerning Galilee. John develops Mary's discovery, the disciples' visit and her encounter with Jesus. — Mark 16:1–8; Matthew 28:1–10; John 20:1–18

> Mary Magdalene came and told the disciples that she had seen the Lord, and that he had spoken these things unto her.

John 20:18 · KJV

The responses unfold within each account. Do not erase surprise, misunderstanding or doubt by giving every person knowledge the narrator only reports later. Nor should every difference in which people are named be silently replaced by one combined list.

### Encounters that lead to witness

Luke develops the Emmaus journey, recognition in the breaking of bread and the return to Jerusalem. Jesus then appears among the gathered disciples, addresses their fear, shows his hands and feet, eats before them and opens their understanding of Scripture. The account connects suffering and resurrection with the proclamation of repentance and forgiveness. — Luke 24:13–49

> And ye are witnesses of these things.

Luke 24:48 · KJV

Matthew ends with the eleven in Galilee, Jesus' authority, the commission to the nations and his promise to be with them. Luke closes with blessing, Jesus being carried up and the disciples returning to Jerusalem in joy. John includes the appearances to the gathered disciples, Thomas, and the later scene beside the sea, with Jesus' charge to Peter. — Matthew 28:16–20; Luke 24:50–53; John 20:19–21:25

These are distinct narrated selections, not a complete combined itinerary. Their shared movement is from Jesus' death to the proclamation that he is risen, encounters with him and the calling of witnesses. Questions about the exact interleaving of all appearances require more than any one account supplies.

**Consider:** How does each Gospel develop discovery into response or witness? What does each choose to place at its close?

**Reflect:** Choose one resurrection encounter and read it as a complete scene. Trace what the participants understand at the beginning and what changes by the end.

## Read across, then return to each account

Comparison helps us notice; continuous reading helps us understand a narrator's movement. Use both. Follow the shared events, attend to material particular to an account, and distinguish an explicit sequence from a proposed reconstruction. Let the four Gospels retain the words and emphases through which they bear witness to Jesus.
