# Who do you say that I am

The question. The confessions. The witness of the Gospels.

> He saith unto them, But whom say ye that I am?

Matthew 16:15 · KJV

The Gospels do more than report that people noticed Jesus. They record questions about who he is, answers offered by followers, disputes over his claims and testimony concerning him. Some speakers recognise something important while still misunderstanding what it means. Others oppose what he says. The writers also tell readers things that the people within a scene have yet to understand.

Read each passage with its speakers and setting in view. Ask who is making a claim, how Jesus responds and what happens next. This study follows six connected questions and responses, allowing each Gospel's wording to remain distinct.

## 01 The question to the disciples

**Read:** Matthew 16:13–20; Mark 8:27–30; Luke 9:18–22.

### What people are saying

In Matthew, Jesus asks the disciples whom people say the Son of Man is. Their answers name John the Baptist, Elijah, Jeremiah or one of the prophets. Mark and Luke also report answers involving John, Elijah and a prophet, but do not give the identical list. Luke places the conversation in a setting of Jesus praying, with the disciples present. — Matthew 16:13–14; Mark 8:27–28; Luke 9:18–19

These are the disciples' reports of other people's opinions. They are not all endorsed by the narrator as adequate identifications. Notice the difference between reporting what has been said and deciding what the narrative itself affirms.

### What the disciples say

Jesus then directs the question to the disciples themselves. They cannot answer only by repeating the range of public opinions. Peter responds with a confession that identifies Jesus as the Christ. Matthew, Mark and Luke preserve that answer with different wording. — Matthew 16:15–16; Mark 8:29; Luke 9:20

The movement from public report to the disciples' own answer gives the scene its force. Jesus asks about their recognition of him, not simply their ability to describe what everyone else thinks.

For a present reader, the question invites careful attention to the Gospel's account before settling for a familiar label. Knowing what others say about Jesus and understanding why a Gospel presents him as it does are related but different tasks.

**Consider:** Which opinions are reported, and by whom? What changes when Jesus asks the second question?

**Reflect:** Distinguish an idea about Jesus that you have inherited from a claim you have examined in a Gospel passage. What would you need to read more closely to understand its grounds?

## 02 Peter's confession

**Read:** Matthew 16:13–28; compare Mark 8:27–38 and Luke 9:18–27.

### The Christ, the Son of the living God

> And Simon Peter answered and said, Thou art the Christ, the Son of the living God.

Matthew 16:16 · KJV

Matthew records Jesus blessing Peter and attributing this recognition to the Father rather than flesh and blood. It then records words about Peter, the rock, the building of the church and the keys of the kingdom. These belong to Matthew's account and should not disappear when it is compared with the shorter wording in Mark and Luke. — Matthew 16:17–19

Christians differ over the implications of the rock and keys for Peter's role and the church's authority. This study does not settle that question. Its immediate focus is the confession, Jesus' response and the further instruction that follows. In all three accounts, Jesus also restricts the disciples from announcing the identification at this point. — Matthew 16:20; Mark 8:30; Luke 9:21

### A confession still needing instruction

Jesus begins to teach that he must suffer, be rejected or opposed, be killed and rise again. Matthew and Mark then record Peter objecting and Jesus sharply rebuking him. The disciple who has named Jesus as Christ resists the path Jesus describes. — Matthew 16:21–23; Mark 8:31–33; Luke 9:22

The accounts continue with the call to deny oneself, take up the cross and follow. Luke includes the word “daily.” The identity confessed is connected to Jesus' death and resurrection and to the life of those who follow him. — Matthew 16:24–28; Mark 8:34–38; Luke 9:23–27

This sequence matters. A correct title does not mean Peter already understands everything Jesus will do. The narrative asks the reader to let Jesus' teaching give content to the confession rather than fit him into an expectation the story itself challenges.

**Consider:** How does Jesus respond to Peter's confession and then to his objection? What does the sequence reveal about recognition and understanding?

**Reflect:** Consider whether your picture of Jesus leaves room for the suffering, resurrection and call to follow described here. Identify what the passage adds to a title you already use.

## 03 Jesus before his opponents

**Read:** Mark 14:53–65; John 5:16–47.

### The question before the council

Mark describes testimony against Jesus that does not agree. Jesus remains silent before the initial questioning. The high priest then asks whether he is the Christ, the Son of the Blessed. Jesus answers directly and speaks of the Son of Man at the right hand of power and coming with the clouds of heaven. — Mark 14:53–62

> And Jesus said, I am: and ye shall see the Son of man sitting on the right hand of power, and coming in the clouds of heaven.

Mark 14:62 · KJV

The high priest calls the answer blasphemy, and the council condemns Jesus. Mark then describes mockery and violence. Keep the voices distinct: the charge is the high priest's judgment within the scene, while the answer is attributed to Jesus. Reporting an accusation is not the same as endorsing it. — Mark 14:63–65

### The Son's works and authority

John 5 presents a different dispute, arising over Jesus' action on the Sabbath and his speech about his Father. The narrator describes the opponents' intensified hostility in connection with equality with God. Jesus' response speaks of the Son doing what the Father does, giving life, exercising judgment and receiving honour as the Father is honoured. It also speaks of the Son's dependence on and obedience to the Father. Both strands belong to the discourse. — John 5:16–30

The argument proceeds through testimony: John, the works given by the Father, the Father himself and the Scriptures. Jesus accuses his hearers of refusing to come to him for life, despite the Scriptures' witness. The discourse culminates in his claim that Moses wrote of him. — John 5:31–47

These scenes present substantial claims about identity and authority. Reading the whole exchanges preserves more than selecting either the accusations or a single sentence in reply. The opponents are particular people in these narratives; their hostility must not be turned into collective blame against Jewish people.

**Consider:** What does Jesus affirm in Mark's scene, and how do others react? What kinds of testimony does John 5 place before the hearers?

**Reflect:** Choose one claim in John 5 and read the verses around it. Note how the passage connects the Son's authority with his relationship to the Father.

## 04 The Father's testimony

**Read:** Matthew 3:13–17; 17:1–9. Revisit John 5:31–40.

### At the baptism

Matthew records Jesus coming to John for baptism. John objects, and Jesus answers concerning the fulfilment of righteousness. After the baptism, the heavens open, the Spirit descends like a dove, and a voice from heaven identifies Jesus as the beloved Son. — Matthew 3:13–17

> And lo a voice from heaven, saying, This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.

Matthew 3:17 · KJV

Within the narrative, this testimony is given before the disciples' later confession. The reader is not limited to the uncertainties of people who subsequently encounter Jesus. Matthew has already supplied an identification through the heavenly voice.

### Hear him

At the transfiguration, Peter, James and John see Jesus transformed in appearance, with Moses and Elijah speaking with him. A voice from the cloud again identifies the beloved Son and adds the instruction to hear him. The disciples fall in fear; Jesus touches them and tells them not to be afraid. — Matthew 17:1–8

As they descend, Jesus tells them not to report the vision until the Son of Man has risen from the dead. The episode's glory is thus connected within the narrative to the resurrection still ahead. It should not be separated from the suffering Jesus has just begun to teach about. — Matthew 17:9; 16:21

John 5 also speaks of the Father's witness, but its discourse should be read on its own terms. It does not explicitly identify the witness in that passage with one particular scene from Matthew. Comparing the accounts need not mean filling every unspecified detail in one Gospel from another.

**Consider:** What does the voice say in each Matthean scene? What does the instruction to hear Jesus add to the identification?

**Reflect:** Read a saying of Jesus that these surrounding chapters record. Consider what it means to attend to his teaching as well as repeat a confession about him.

## 05 Thomas's confession

**Read:** John 20:24–29.

### The testimony he would not receive

Thomas is absent when Jesus first appears to the assembled disciples in this account. They tell him they have seen the Lord, but he demands to see the marks of the nails and to touch Jesus' wounds before believing. John records a particular refusal to accept their testimony, not merely an abstract question about evidence. — John 20:24–25

When Jesus comes again with Thomas present, he offers peace and addresses Thomas's stated demands. He calls him to believe. The story then records Thomas's answer:

> And Thomas answered and said unto him, My Lord and my God.

John 20:28 · KJV

John says that Thomas speaks to Jesus. The wording should remain visible rather than being reduced to a general expression of surprise. The narrative does not explicitly state that Thomas touches the wounds after the invitation; his confession follows Jesus' words.

### Believing without that encounter

Jesus connects Thomas's belief with seeing him and pronounces a blessing on those who have not seen and yet believe. The next verses turn toward the written signs supplied for the reader. The contrast is not between Thomas having grounds for belief and every later reader being asked to believe without any witness. — John 20:29–31

The encounter answers Thomas while also addressing the position of people who receive the story without having been in the room. Keep both parts in view: the personal confession and the Gospel's movement toward its readers.

**Consider:** To whom does Thomas speak? What does the narrator say happened, and what detail about touching is left unstated?

**Reflect:** Distinguish Thomas's encounter from your position as a reader receiving testimony. What does John invite you to consider as the basis for belief?

## 06 The purpose of John's Gospel

**Read:** John 20:30–31; compare John 1:1–18.

### Written that you might believe

John states that Jesus performed other signs before the disciples that are not recorded in the book. The account is selective, and the writer names its purpose:

> But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name.

John 20:31 · KJV

The purpose includes both an identification of Jesus and life through his name. The signs are not collected only to provoke wonder. They are presented as witness directed toward belief in the one they reveal.

### Read the ending with the beginning

John opens by speaking of the Word, who was with God and was God, through whom all things were made. It describes life and light, witness, rejection and reception, then says the Word became flesh and dwelt among us. Grace and truth are connected with Jesus Christ, and the Son makes the Father known. — John 1:1–18

These opening statements belong to the narrator's presentation. Read alongside the later scenes, they help distinguish what a character presently understands from the fuller account the Gospel gives its readers. Thomas's confession and the stated purpose near the end are encountered within that presentation.

The reader's task is therefore more than assembling a list of titles. Follow what Jesus says and does, the testimony offered and the responses recorded. Some responses recognise him, some resist, and some require further instruction. John states the response toward which his selected account is directed.

**Consider:** Why does John say these signs were written? How does the opening prepare the reader for the claims and responses encountered later?

**Reflect:** Choose one scene in John and reread it in light of 20:31. Identify what it reveals about Jesus and what response the narrative places before the reader.

## Returning to the question

The Gospels' answers emerge through testimony, confession, dispute, suffering and resurrection. Attend to those accounts before letting a familiar label stand for more than you have considered. Jesus' question remains a reason to read closely: who is the one these narratives present, and how will you respond to him?
