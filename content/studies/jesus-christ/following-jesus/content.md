# Following Jesus

**Study 16 · Jesus Christ · First content draft · 2026-10-13**

Jesus calls people to follow him. His words concern where they place their trust, what they are willing to lose, how they learn, and how they love one another. Following is more than standing among a crowd that travels with him, and more than agreeing with a title given to him.

Yet the call is not a command to become sufficient without him. The one who speaks of the cross also promises rest. The one who commands fruitfulness says that without him his disciples can do nothing. Read the cost alongside the promise, and the individual response alongside the people Jesus gathers and sends.

## 01 Come ye after me

**Main readings:** Mark 1:16–20; Mark 2:13–17.

### I will make you to become

Simon and Andrew are casting a net when Jesus calls them. James and John are mending their nets when he calls them in turn. Mark names their work, their response, and what they leave. The invitation interrupts an existing life rather than waiting for them to create a different one first.

> And Jesus said unto them, Come ye after me, and I will make you to become fishers of men.

Mark 1:17 · KJV

Jesus’ promise matters as much as the departure. He does not only say what they must leave; he says what he will make them become. Their new work depends upon the one they follow. Mark records their immediate response without supplying an account of everything they understood or every question they might have had.

These are particular calls to named people. Their nets and boats belong to their own circumstances. The passage gives a concrete account of following without turning every occupation or family connection into something every disciple must abandon in precisely the same way.

### Sinners to repentance

Levi is sitting at the receipt of custom when Jesus says, “Follow me.” He rises and follows. The next scene places Jesus and his disciples at a meal with many publicans and sinners. Some observers question this company; Jesus answers with the physician and those who need him.

His stated purpose is to call sinners to repentance. Welcome and repentance appear together. The meal does not portray people repairing themselves before Jesus will approach them, nor does his answer make repentance unnecessary. His call creates a gathering that others question.

Read these scenes as the beginning of relationships, not proof that the called already possess complete understanding. Mark will later show disciples needing correction. The response is real, and learning still lies ahead.

**Consider:** What does Jesus promise the fishermen? How does his answer about the physician explain his company at the meal?

**Reflect:** Consider whether you think of following primarily as proving your readiness, or as responding to Jesus and learning from him.

## 02 Take up his cross

**Main reading:** Mark 8:27–38.

### The things that be of God

Peter confesses that Jesus is the Christ. Jesus then teaches that the Son of man must suffer, be rejected, be killed, and rise again. Peter begins to rebuke him. Jesus’ answer exposes a disagreement about what being the Christ involves, even after Peter has spoken the right confession.

The call to deny oneself and take up the cross follows this exchange. Jesus gathers the people with his disciples to hear it. Following him cannot mean accepting his title while refusing the way he says he must go. The instruction confronts the desire to direct Jesus according to expectations he has just corrected.

### For my sake and the gospel’s

> For whosoever will save his life shall lose it; but whosoever shall lose his life for my sake and the gospel’s, the same shall save it.

Mark 8:35 · KJV

The reason for the loss is specified. Jesus speaks of losing life for his sake and the gospel’s, not of seeking pain as though suffering were valuable simply because it hurts. His preceding announcement of rejection and death gives weight to the cross saying. It should not be reduced to a name for every inconvenience.

Jesus then asks what someone gains by obtaining the whole world while losing his own soul. The passage measures success by more than what can be secured now. His final saying concerns being ashamed of him and his words, and his coming in the Father’s glory. Following has a public allegiance and a future reckoning.

Self-denial here is directed toward following Jesus. It does not make another person’s every demand equivalent to his command. Keep his identity, his words, and the gospel’s sake at the centre of an application.

**Consider:** Why does the cross saying follow Peter’s rebuke? What qualifies the loss Jesus describes?

**Reflect:** Consider an expectation you bring to Jesus that his own teaching challenges. What would it mean to receive his correction?

## 03 Counteth the cost

**Main readings:** Luke 14:25–35; compare Matthew 10:37–39.

### He cannot be my disciple

Great multitudes are travelling with Jesus when he turns and addresses them. He speaks of family, one’s own life, bearing the cross, and forsaking possessions. The repeated saying that someone cannot be his disciple prevents the crowd’s size from becoming the measure of its understanding.

Luke’s wording about hating father, mother, wife, children, siblings, and one’s own life is severe. Matthew’s related saying speaks of loving father or mother, son or daughter, more than Jesus. Read the comparison carefully: it supports understanding the demand in terms of allegiance that takes precedence over even the closest relationships. It does not justify cultivating malice toward a family. Preserve Luke’s strong wording rather than pretending the cost is slight.

### Sitteth not down first

Jesus describes someone considering whether he can finish a tower and a king considering whether he can face a larger army. Both sit down first and reckon with what lies ahead. The unfinished tower brings mockery; the outnumbered king sends to seek conditions of peace.

The comparisons interrupt an unexamined willingness to join a crowd. Jesus does not conceal the demand and reveal it only after people have followed. He speaks plainly, concluding that whoever does not forsake all he has cannot be his disciple.

This conclusion should retain its force. It is not merely advice to add a religious interest while everything else keeps its former claim. Neither does the passage supply a single detailed household or property arrangement for every subsequent believer. Its explicit demand and the question of how readers enact that demand should remain distinguishable.

The closing image of salt that has lost its savour adds a warning about becoming unfit for use. The final summons is to hear. Counting the cost is an invitation to take Jesus seriously, not a method of calculating how little allegiance will suffice.

**Consider:** What does the repeated phrase “cannot be my disciple” do to the crowd’s assumptions? What do both comparisons require before action?

**Reflect:** Name a loyalty or possession you tend to treat as beyond Jesus’ claim. Consider what this passage asks you to reconsider.

## 04 Learn of me

**Main reading:** Matthew 11:25–30.

### Come unto me

Jesus thanks the Father for revealing to babes what is hidden from the wise and prudent. He then speaks of the Father’s knowledge of the Son and the Son’s revelation of the Father. The invitation to the weary follows these statements. Its promise rests in the identity of the one inviting them.

He calls those who labour and are heavy laden to come to him, and promises rest. The passage does not first ask them to show that they can carry still more. Nor does it identify one narrowly defined burden as the only kind his invitation addresses. Begin with the people he names and the rest he promises.

### Meek and lowly in heart

> Take my yoke upon you, and learn of me; for I am meek and lowly in heart: and ye shall find rest unto your souls.

Matthew 11:29 · KJV

Rest and learning belong within the same invitation. Jesus does not describe rest as having no relation to him and no instruction to receive. He speaks of taking his yoke, learning of him, and finding rest. His own description—meek and lowly in heart—tells the hearer something about the one whose teaching they receive.

Hold this beside the cost of following rather than using one passage to silence the other. The call is demanding, but the teacher is not presented as indifferent to the burdened. Dependence on him belongs to discipleship from the beginning.

The promise of rest unto the soul should be allowed its own wording. It is not a claim that a follower will never experience weariness, grief, or opposition. It directs the weary toward Jesus and the kind of teacher he declares himself to be.

**Consider:** What statements about the Father and Son precede the invitation? How does Jesus describe himself when calling people to learn?

**Reflect:** Consider whether your understanding of learning from Jesus makes room for both his instruction and his promised rest.

## 05 Abide in me

**Main reading:** John 15:1–17.

### Without me ye can do nothing

Jesus names himself the true vine and his Father the husbandman. The branches do not produce fruit independently of the vine. His instruction to abide therefore concerns continuing dependence, not merely remembering an earlier decision.

> I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing.

John 15:5 · KJV

The passage includes both tending fruitful branches and warning about not abiding. Neither should be removed to make the image easier. Jesus also speaks of his words abiding in his disciples and of their asking. The promise concerning prayer belongs to this relation: it is not detached permission to treat every desire as something God must fulfil.

Fruitfulness glorifies the Father. Its explanation continues through words, commandments, love, joy, and the disciples’ relation to Jesus. Do not reduce fruit to an isolated measure of visible success.

### Love one another

Jesus moves from the Father’s love for him to his love for his disciples, telling them to continue in his love. He commands them to love one another as he has loved them. The branches image leads into the life of a people whose relationships matter.

He speaks of laying down life for friends, calls the disciples friends, and says he has made known what he heard from his Father. Friendship does not cancel his commandments; these statements appear together. He also reminds them that he chose and appointed them to bear lasting fruit.

The repeated command to love one another prevents abiding from becoming only a private inward experience. The disciple depends on Jesus and is addressed alongside other disciples. His joy and his love belong to the account of obedience, not as rewards added to an otherwise unrelated task.

**Consider:** Which statements describe dependence, and which describe relationships among disciples? How does the command to love develop the vine image?

**Reflect:** Consider how continuing in Jesus’ words should affect one actual relationship, rather than remaining only an intention about inward devotion.

## 06 Teaching them to observe

**Main reading:** Matthew 28:16–20.

### All nations

The eleven go to the mountain Jesus appointed. Matthew records worship and also doubt. Jesus approaches and speaks of the authority given to him in heaven and earth. The commission rests upon that authority, not upon an account of the disciples having become independently sufficient.

The KJV says to teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost. The following verse specifies teaching people to observe all that Jesus commanded. The commission includes more than passing on information: those taught are to learn a life of obedience.

All nations widens the destination beyond this gathering of eleven. Baptism and continued teaching belong in the same commission. The passage does not present following as a solitary achievement that has no relation to a gathered people or to others who still need to hear.

### I am with you alway

> Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world. Amen.

Matthew 28:20 · KJV

Jesus’ promise of presence accompanies his sending. The ending does not leave the disciples with commands and no assurance about the one who gives them. Their work proceeds under his authority and with his promised presence.

The immediate hearers are the eleven. A reader’s participation in the continuing work should be explained from that commission rather than by pretending every reader stood on the mountain or received an identical personal assignment. The scope reaches all nations; the instruction reaches all that Jesus commanded. Different responsibilities can serve that shared work.

Following thus leads toward others: calling them to learn, baptizing, and teaching obedience. The promise of presence keeps the ending connected to the beginning. Those sent by Jesus continue to depend upon Jesus.

**Consider:** What surrounds the command to go—before it and after it? What does “teaching them to observe” add to the idea of communicating information?

**Reflect:** Consider how you can participate in helping someone learn Jesus’ teaching while remaining a learner yourself.

Come after him; take up the cross; count the cost; learn of him; abide in him; teach what he commanded. These words describe a life whose allegiance is costly and whose strength is received. The one who calls his disciples also promises to make them, give them rest, and remain with them.
