# Following Jesus and the Sermon on the Mount

Concepts: Blessing · Hearing · Doing · Remaining

Motto: Hear the words. / Build on the rock.

Lead: A *disciple* is a learner who walks behind a teacher and lives the way that teacher lives. *Following Jesus* means being His disciple. The fullest record of what He taught His followers is the *Sermon on the Mount*. That is the church's name for the long teaching in Matthew 5 to 7. A *sermon* is a speech that teaches; Matthew himself does not use the name. Many of its lines are famous: turn the other cheek, do not judge, the narrow gate. They are often quoted on their own, as if each were a separate proverb. This study teaches one habit to put right that reading: **read the line inside the whole.** Before you take a saying away, ask three questions. Who is listening? What came just before? Where is the passage going?

> Therefore everyone who hears these words of Mine and acts on them is like a wise man who built his house on the rock.

Matthew 7:24 · BSB

This is a selection, not a census. The study reads the Sermon on the Mount through, except its teaching on prayer (Matthew 6:5–15). That teaching has its own home in *Prayer across Scripture: learning to pray*. Then it reads four other passages that say what following Jesus costs and where the strength for it comes from: Luke 12:13–34, Luke 14:25–35, Matthew 11:25–30 and John 15:1–17. Some well-known passages about following Jesus are only quoted here and linked to the studies that read them in full. These include Jesus calling the fishermen (Mark 1:16–20), "take up his cross" (Mark 8), the servant who is great (Mark 10) and the sending of the disciples (Matthew 28). Luke has a shorter sermon of his own, which has its own wording (Luke 6:20–49). It is mentioned but not read through here.

## 01 Blessed: what Jesus says before He asks anything

**Main readings:** Matthew 5:1–16

Matthew sets the scene carefully. Jesus has just called His first followers from their fishing nets (Matthew 4:18–22). Now He sees the crowds, goes up the mountain and sits down, the usual posture of a Jewish teacher. "His disciples came to Him, and He began to teach them" (Matthew 5:1–2). The disciples are the ones being taught, and the crowd is listening too. When the sermon ends, it is "the crowds" who are astonished (Matthew 7:28). So the teaching is addressed to followers, but anyone may overhear it.

The first words are not commands. They are nine sayings that begin "Blessed are". In Scripture, *blessed* means being in a good place before God and under His favour, whether or not it looks that way. The church calls these sayings the Beatitudes, from the Latin word for "blessed".

> Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.
> Blessed are those who mourn, for they will be comforted.

Matthew 5:3–4 · BSB

*The kingdom of heaven* is Matthew's usual phrase for God's kingdom: His rule, arriving in Jesus. Matthew's Jewish readers often spoke of "heaven" out of reverence for God's name, and that may be why he uses the phrase. Read more in *The kingdom he announced*.

Look at who is called blessed. The poor in spirit are those who know they bring nothing to God. Then come those who mourn, the meek, the hungry for what is right, the merciful, the peacemakers and the persecuted (Matthew 5:3–12). Each blessing comes with a promise. Most of these people would not look fortunate to anyone watching.

**Its limits.** These sayings are not a ladder of steps by which to earn the kingdom. They describe people on whom God's favour rests. Nor do they make grief or persecution good in themselves. The comfort is promised to mourners; mourning is not praised for its own sake.

Then Jesus says what His hearers already are: "You are the salt of the earth", "You are the light of the world" (Matthew 5:13–14). He does not tell them to become salt and light; He says they are.

> In the same way, let your light shine before men, that they may see your good deeds and glorify your Father in heaven.

Matthew 5:16 · BSB

The point of the light is the Father's glory, not the disciple's. Keep that in mind, because chapter 6 will warn against good deeds done to be seen.

**Consider:** What comes first in the sermon, commands or blessings? Whose glory are the good deeds in verse 16 meant to show?

**Reflect:** Which of the people Jesus calls blessed is hardest for you to see as blessed? Why might that be?

## 02 Not to abolish but to fulfill: Jesus and the Law

**Main readings:** Matthew 5:17–48

*The Law and the Prophets* is a name for the Hebrew Scriptures, taken from two of their parts. Jewish tradition calls the whole the Tanakh: Torah (the Law), Prophets and Writings. Jesus begins with a denial:

> Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets. I have not come to abolish them, but to fulfill them.

Matthew 5:17 · BSB

Not "a single jot, not a stroke of a pen" will disappear "until everything is accomplished" (Matthew 5:18). The jot stands for the smallest Hebrew letter, and the stroke for a tiny mark on a letter. Then comes the shock: "unless your righteousness exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees, you will never enter the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 5:20). *Righteousness* means being and doing what is right before God.

It helps to know who these people were. The scribes were trained teachers of the Law. The Pharisees were a movement of devout Jews who worked hard to keep the Law in everyday life. Jesus is not saying they were careless. He is saying that God looks for a righteousness that goes deeper, down to the heart.

Six times He says, "You have heard that it was said… But I tell you". Each time He takes His hearers past the visible act to its root:

- **Murder and anger.** Anger and contempt already stand under judgment (Matthew 5:21–22). "Raca" was an Aramaic insult, roughly "empty-head". The Sanhedrin was the Jewish high council. The word translated "hell" is *gehenna*, named after a valley outside Jerusalem; Jesus uses it for final judgment. Reconciliation comes before worship, even in the middle of offering a gift at the temple altar: "First go and be reconciled to your brother; then come and offer your gift" (Matthew 5:24).
- **Adultery and the heart.** Jesus says "anyone who looks at a woman to lust after her has already committed adultery with her in his heart" (Matthew 5:28). Then come eye and hand, and divorce (Matthew 5:29–32). Read more in *Sexuality, singleness and divorce*, which reads verses 27–32 for its own questions.
- **Oaths.** "Simply let your ‘Yes’ be ‘Yes,’ and your ‘No,’ ‘No.’" (Matthew 5:37). The point is truthfulness that needs no extra props.
- **Eye for eye.** This rule in the Law (Exodus 21:24) set a limit on what judges could impose. Jewish teachers came to apply it as payment for injury, not as literal maiming (Mishnah, Bava Kamma 8:1). Jesus speaks to the person who has been wronged: "But I tell you not to resist an evil person" (Matthew 5:39). He gives three examples: the slap, the lawsuit, the soldier who forces you to carry his load a mile.
- **Enemies.** "Love your neighbor" is in the Law (Leviticus 19:18). "Hate your enemy" is not; it was a common way of putting things. Jesus says: "But I tell you, love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44). The reason is the Father, who "causes His sun to rise on the evil and the good, and sends rain on the righteous and the unrighteous" (Matthew 5:45).

The section ends: "Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect" (Matthew 5:48). The word means complete, whole and undivided. It echoes the Law's "be holy, for I am holy" (Leviticus 19:2).

## 03 Seen by your Father: giving, praying and fasting in secret

**Main readings:** Matthew 6:1–4; Matthew 6:16–18 (with 6:9–13 quoted)

In Jewish devotion, three practices stood together: giving to the poor, prayer and fasting. *Fasting* means going without food for a time in order to seek God. Jewish teachers prized quiet giving. The Mishnah describes a "chamber of secrets" in the temple, where the devout left gifts in secret and the poor took from them in secret [check: Mishnah, Shekalim 5:6]. Jesus takes all three practices for granted. He says "when you give", "when you fast". He does not say "if". What He warns against is performing them for an audience.

> Be careful not to perform your righteous acts before men to be seen by them. If you do, you will have no reward from your Father in heaven.

Matthew 6:1 · BSB

A *hypocrite*, in the Greek of Jesus' day, was a stage actor. A *synagogue* was the local meeting place for Jewish prayer and the reading of Scripture. The warning is not against Jewish worship; any disciple can play to the crowd. Give so quietly that you "do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing" (Matthew 6:3). When you fast, wash your face and look ordinary. "And your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you" (Matthew 6:18).

Does this contradict "let your light shine" (Matthew 5:16)? Read the two together and the answer is no. In both, the question is who you want the glory to go to. Good deeds may be seen. They are not to be done in order to be seen.

Between giving and fasting stands the prayer Jesus taught:

> So then, this is how you should pray: ‘Our Father in heaven, hallowed be Your name. Your kingdom come, Your will be done, on earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread. And forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors. And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one.’

Matthew 6:9–13 · BSB

Read more in *Prayer across Scripture: learning to pray*.

**Its limits.** The reward is the Father's gift. It is not a wage that buys salvation, and the passage does not say what the reward will be. Nor does Jesus set a calendar for fasting.

**Consider:** Who is the audience in each of the three practices? How do Matthew 5:16 and 6:1 fit together?

**Reflect:** What good thing do you do that you would find hard to keep doing if no one ever noticed?

## 04 Treasure, worry and the kingdom

**Main readings:** Matthew 6:19–34; Luke 12:13–34

Luke records a scene that prompts this teaching. A man in the crowd wants Jesus to settle his inheritance dispute. Jesus refuses to act as judge and speaks instead about what lies underneath the request:

> Watch out! Guard yourselves against every form of greed, for one’s life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions.

Luke 12:15 · BSB

Then He tells a parable about a rich man whose land produced a bumper crop. The man has not stolen anything. But listen to how he talks: *I* will do this, *my* barns, *my* grain, *myself*. God answers him: "You fool! This very night your life will be required of you" (Luke 12:20). In Scripture, a fool is someone who lives as if God were not there. The parable ends with the man who "stores up treasure for himself but is not rich toward God" (Luke 12:21).

Matthew's sermon makes the same turn. Treasure on earth decays; "For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Matthew 6:21). Next comes a saying about the eye as the body's lamp (Matthew 6:22–23). Many readers take the "good" eye as a Hebrew way of saying generous, because the Law calls a stingy look a "bad eye" (Deuteronomy 15:9) [check: the idiom in the Hebrew of Deuteronomy 15:9]. Then a plain line: "You cannot serve both God and money" (Matthew 6:24).

Only after that does Jesus say "do not worry". In both Gospels He points to birds and flowers. Luke's version names ravens, birds the Law counted unclean (Leviticus 11:15), and still "God feeds them" (Luke 12:24). The argument runs from the lesser to the greater: if God cares for these, how much more for you, "O you of little faith". *Faith* means trust in God.

> But seek first the kingdom of God and His righteousness, and all these things will be added unto you.

Matthew 6:33 · BSB

Luke adds the tenderest line: "Do not be afraid, little flock, for your Father is pleased to give you the kingdom" (Luke 12:32). It is followed at once by "Sell your possessions and give to the poor" (Luke 12:33). Notice the order. Reassurance comes first, and it makes generosity possible.

[care] Anxiety can be an illness, not only a habit of thought. Seeing a doctor or a counsellor does not go against these verses. Jesus does not say a worried person has failed, and He admits "Today has enough trouble of its own" (Matthew 6:34). If worry has turned into despair, please use the help lines in part 02. Read more in *Fear, anxiety and despair*.

[care] "All these things will be added" is not a promise of wealth. It is no reproach to anyone who is poor, and the passage says nothing to back prosperity teaching. The confession teaches stewardship: giving "cheerfully, regularly, systematically, proportionately, and liberally" (article XIII, stewardship). It does not promise riches as a reward for faith.

**Its limits.** Jesus forbids worry and greed, not planning. Scripture also praises the ant that stores food in summer (Proverbs 6:6–8). "Sell your possessions" was said to these disciples. The early church shared freely (Acts 2:45), yet believers still had homes (Acts 12:12). How to obey this today is a matter of the heart's treasure, worked out in conscience. Read more in *Money, possessions and generosity*.

**Consider:** What does the rich man forget? In Luke, what comes between "do not be afraid" and "sell your possessions"?

**Reflect:** Where does your heart go first when you think about the future? What would seeking the kingdom first change this week?

## 05 The speck, the door and the golden rule

**Main readings:** Matthew 7:1–12

"Do not judge, or you will be judged" (Matthew 7:1) is perhaps the most quoted line in the sermon. Read it with what comes next. Jesus gives a comic picture: a man with a beam of wood in his own eye, offering to remove a speck from his brother's.

> You hypocrite! First take the beam out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to remove the speck from your brother’s eye.

Matthew 7:5 · BSB

The aim is still to help the brother. What Jesus forbids is condemning others while refusing to look at yourself. He does not forbid all moral judgement. The very next verse requires discernment: "Do not give dogs what is holy; do not throw your pearls before swine" (Matthew 7:6). This is a hard saying. Many readers take it to mean: do not keep pressing holy things on people who openly scorn them. A few verses later Jesus tells His hearers to recognise false prophets "by their fruit" (Matthew 7:16).

Then come asking, seeking and knocking (Matthew 7:7–8). The ground for this is the Father's character, not a method:

> So if you who are evil know how to give good gifts to your children, how much more will your Father in heaven give good things to those who ask Him!

Matthew 7:11 · BSB

[care] This promise is about a good Father giving good things. It is not a formula that obliges God to give whatever we name. Prosperity teaching (often called the Word of Faith movement) reads it that way, and this site does not. Prayers that seem unanswered are not proof that you asked wrongly.

Then comes the line the church calls the golden rule:

> In everything, then, do to others as you would have them do to you. For this is the essence of the Law and the Prophets.

Matthew 7:12 · BSB

The rabbi Hillel, a generation before Jesus, is remembered for a negative form: "What is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbour" (Babylonian Talmud, Shabbat 31a) [check: wording of the translation used]. Jesus puts it positively: do the good you would want done to you.

Here is a shape worth noticing, and you can check it yourself. "The Law and the Prophets" appears in Matthew 5:17 and again in 7:12. The two mentions frame the whole middle of the sermon. Everything between them shows what it looks like for the Law to be fulfilled, and 7:12 sums it up.

**Consider:** Why must the beam come out first? Where else in the sermon have you met "the Law and the Prophets"?

**Reflect:** Think of a person you find easy to criticise. What is the beam you would need to deal with first?

## 06 Two gates, two trees, two builders

**Main readings:** Matthew 7:13–29

The sermon ends with a series of pairs: two gates, two kinds of tree, two kinds of people who say "Lord", and two builders. Each pair asks the same question: has the hearing turned into a life?

"But small is the gate and narrow the way that leads to life, and only a few find it" (Matthew 7:14). False prophets look like sheep. They are known "By their fruit", meaning what their teaching and their lives produce (Matthew 7:15–20). Then comes the most searching warning:

> Not everyone who says to Me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ will enter the kingdom of heaven, but only he who does the will of My Father in heaven.

Matthew 7:21 · BSB

Some even point to prophecy and miracles done in His name. He answers: "I never knew you; depart from Me, you workers of lawlessness!" (Matthew 7:23). Notice the word *never*. He did not once know them and then lose them. They were never His.

[care] This warning strips away false confidence. It is not written to make a trusting believer afraid. Scripture also says "I write these things to you who believe" so that "you may know that you have eternal life" (1 John 5:13). Read more in *Chosen and kept: can I be sure?*

Both builders hear. Both houses face the same rain, torrents and wind. The storm does not make the difference; it shows the difference, and the difference is in acting on what was heard (Matthew 7:24–27). The storm here points to the final judgement. It does not say that someone who suffers hardship must have built badly.

The ending is the crowd's reaction: "the crowds were astonished at His teaching, because He taught as one who had authority, and not as their scribes" (Matthew 7:28–29). Teachers usually quoted earlier authorities. Jesus said "But I tell you". Who speaks like that? Read more in *Who do you say that I am? Who Jesus really is*.

**Consider:** What do the two builders have in common, and what is the one difference? Why does "I never knew you" matter more than "I stopped knowing you"?

**Reflect:** Which saying in this sermon have you admired without doing?

## Question: Can anyone actually live this?

Read honestly, the sermon is impossible to keep by willpower. Anger counts as murder, and the call is to be "perfect" (Matthew 5:22, 48). So is it a ladder to heaven, or something else?

**Where the confession speaks, this site says it plainly.** No one is made right with God by keeping the sermon. Salvation is by *grace*, God's undeserved kindness, received by faith. "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord" (article IV, salvation). The sermon itself agrees. It opens with blessing on the poor in spirit, those who know they have nothing to bring. And the same Jesus who sets the standard invites the weary to rest in Him (Matthew 11:28; part 08 below). Baptists read the sermon as the life of those who already belong to the kingdom. It is lived by God's strength as believers are made holy over time, and failure sends them back to Christ. Read more in *Saved by grace: how God forgives and changes us*.

**Other Christians read it differently, and here they are in brief.** Anabaptists, in the Schleitheim Confession (1527), took its sayings on the sword and on oaths as binding, word for word, on every believer. Martin Luther, in the Lutheran tradition, distinguished what Christ asks of a person in private life from that person's duties in public office [check: where Luther sets this out, for example his sermons on Matthew 5–7]. Some dispensational teachers once read the sermon as the rule of a future kingdom rather than direct instruction for the church today [check: which writer and edition to cite]. Dietrich Bonhoeffer, a Lutheran pastor, warned in *The Cost of Discipleship* (1937) against "cheap grace", forgiveness received without following. This page has not read any of these sources directly for this study, so they are reported here rather than quoted at length.

## 07 Count the cost

**Main readings:** Luke 14:25–35 (with Mark 8:34–35 quoted)

"Large crowds were now traveling with Jesus, and He turned and said to them" (Luke 14:25). What He says to them seems designed to thin the crowd:

> If anyone comes to Me and does not hate his father and mother and wife and children and brothers and sisters—yes, even his own life—he cannot be My disciple.

Luke 14:26 · BSB

"Hate" here is a Hebrew way of speaking about which loyalty comes first. Matthew's parallel says it without the sharp edge: whoever "loves" family "more than Me" (Matthew 10:37). Jesus elsewhere rebukes people who dodge caring for their parents (Mark 7:10–13), so this is not permission to despise one's family. But do not soften Luke's word to nothing. Christ's claim comes before every other claim.

[care] If someone uses this verse to cut you off from your family or to control you, that is a misuse of it. Allegiance to Christ never requires you to stay under someone else's abuse.

"And whoever does not carry his cross and follow Me cannot be My disciple" (Luke 14:27). The *cross* was the Roman means of execution. A condemned man carried his own crossbeam to the place where he would die. Jesus had not yet been crucified when He said this, so the picture would have shocked His hearers.

> Which of you, wishing to build a tower, does not first sit down and count the cost to see if he has the resources to complete it?

Luke 14:28 · BSB

A builder sits down and calculates. So does a king facing an army twice the size of his own (Luke 14:28–32). Then the conclusion: "In the same way, any one of you who does not give up everything he has cannot be My disciple" (Luke 14:33). Salt that has lost its flavour is useless, and the passage ends with a summons: "He who has ears to hear, let him hear" (Luke 14:34–35).

Mark records the same call at the turning point of the Gospel, right after Peter names Jesus the Christ:

> If anyone wants to come after Me, he must deny himself and take up his cross and follow Me. For whoever wants to save his life will lose it, but whoever loses his life for My sake and for the gospel will save it.

Mark 8:34–35 · BSB

The *gospel* is the good news about Jesus. Read more in *Who do you say that I am? Who Jesus really is*.

**Its limits.** "Give up everything" puts everything under Christ's claim. It does not set one plan for every household's goods. When Zacchaeus gave half his goods to the poor, Jesus said salvation had come to his house (Luke 19:8–9). The cross is loss for Christ's sake and the gospel's, not suffering sought for its own sake.

**Consider:** Why does Jesus speak this way to a large crowd? What do the tower-builder and the king both do first?

**Reflect:** What loyalty or possession do you treat as off limits to Jesus?

## 08 Come to Me: rest for the weary

**Main readings:** Matthew 11:25–30

Just before this, Jesus has grieved over towns that saw His works and did not *repent*, that is, did not turn from sin to God (Matthew 11:20–24). Then He prays: "You have hidden these things from the wise and learned, and revealed them to little children" (Matthew 11:25). He goes on to make a claim no ordinary teacher makes: "No one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son and those to whom the Son chooses to reveal Him" (Matthew 11:27). The invitation that follows stands on that claim.

> Come to Me, all you who are weary and burdened, and I will give you rest. Take My yoke upon you and learn from Me; for I am gentle and humble in heart, and you will find rest for your souls. For My yoke is easy and My burden is light.

Matthew 11:28–30 · BSB

A *yoke* was a wooden beam that joined two animals for work. Jewish teachers spoke of taking on "the yoke of the kingdom of heaven" and "the yoke of the commandments" [check: Mishnah, Berakhot 2:2]. Jesus offers *His* yoke. The rest He gives is not idleness. It comes from walking with Him and learning from Him, and from finding that the one who asks everything is "gentle and humble in heart".

Read this beside part 07, not instead of it. The Lord who says "count the cost" is the same Lord who says "come to Me". The cost is real, and so is the rest.

**Its limits.** Jesus does not promise that His followers will never be tired, grieved or opposed. He promises "rest for your souls", found in Him.

**Consider:** What does Jesus say about Himself before He invites the weary? How is a yoke different from no work at all?

**Reflect:** What burden are you carrying that you have not yet brought to Him?

## 09 Remain in Me: the vine and the branches

**Main readings:** John 15:1–17 (with Mark 10:45 quoted)

It is the night before the cross. Jesus has eaten with His disciples and said "Get up! Let us go on from here" (John 14:31). Then He speaks of a vine. Israel had been pictured as God's vine (Psalm 80:8; Isaiah 5:1–7). Jesus says, "I am the true vine" (John 15:1).

> I am the vine and you are the branches. The one who remains in Me, and I in him, will bear much fruit. For apart from Me you can do nothing.

John 15:5 · BSB

*Remain* means keep living in, keep depending on. Fruit is not something a branch manufactures; it grows from the vine. The Father "prunes to make it even more fruitful" (John 15:2). That is hard to bear, but the cutting is for growth.

[care] What about the branch that "is thrown away and withers" (John 15:6)? Baptists, with the confession, hold that true believers "will never fall away from the state of grace" (article V, God's purpose of grace). They read verse 6 as describing those who are attached to Jesus outwardly but never truly united to Him. Judas, who had just left the room, is the example many readers give. Christians in the Wesleyan and Arminian tradition read it as showing that a true believer can finally fall away. That view is theirs, not the confession's; no source arguing it has been read for this page. Read more in *Chosen and kept: can I be sure?*

"If you remain in Me and My words remain in you, ask whatever you wish, and it will be done for you" (John 15:7). The asking belongs to people whose wishes are being shaped by His words. It is not an open cheque.

Then the vine becomes a people: "This is My commandment, that you love one another as I have loved you. Greater love has no one than this, that he lay down his life for his friends" (John 15:12–13). He calls them friends, not servants, and reminds them: "You did not choose Me, but I chose you" (John 15:16). The love that sets the measure is His death. Mark gives the same reason when the disciples argue about greatness: "For even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45). *Son of Man* was Jesus' favourite name for Himself. A *ransom* is a price paid to set someone free. Read more in *The cross, the empty tomb and the throne: what Jesus' death and rising did*.

**Consider:** Where does the fruit come from? How does the vine picture lead into the command to love one another?

**Reflect:** Which one relationship would change if you loved that person "as I have loved you"?

## Closing: hear, and build

Matthew's Gospel ends on a mountain, as the sermon began on one. The risen Jesus sends His disciples:

> Therefore go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey all that I have commanded you. And surely I am with you always, even to the end of the age.

Matthew 28:19–20 · BSB

"All that I have commanded you" includes the sermon you have just read. Disciples make disciples by teaching people to obey. They do it with the presence of Jesus promised to them, not on their own strength. Read more in *Witness and the nations: telling others about Jesus*.

Here is the habit to carry to the next passage: **read the line inside the whole.** Ask who is listening, what came just before and where the passage is going. You have seen what it does. "Do not judge" turned out to be about the beam in your own eye. "Do not worry" came straight after "where your treasure is". "Count the cost" was spoken by the same Lord who says "come to Me". Every command in the sermon sits under the blessing that opens it.

If you have not yet trusted Christ, none of this begins with trying harder. It begins with Him: "Come to Me, all you who are weary and burdened" (Matthew 11:28). Turn from your sin and trust Him as Lord. Read more in *The good news: how a person is made right with God*.
