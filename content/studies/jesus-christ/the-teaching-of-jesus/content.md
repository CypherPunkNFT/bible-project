# The teaching of Jesus

**Study 17 · Jesus Christ · First content draft · 2026-10-13**

Jesus teaches disciples with crowds listening, answers questions, corrects ambitions, and tells stories that expose what his hearers value. His sayings belong within these movements. A command may follow a promise; a parable may answer a dispute; a warning may conclude teaching the hearer has already received.

Read these six units by asking who is listening, what prompted the teaching, and how its argument develops. Notice the reasons Jesus gives as well as the actions he commands. His teaching repeatedly directs attention to the Father: his mercy, his knowledge of human need, and his will.

## 01 He opened his mouth, and taught them

**Main reading:** Matthew 5–7, with particular attention to Matthew 5:1–37. **Comparison:** Luke 6:20–26.

### Blessed are

Matthew begins with Jesus seeing the multitudes, going up a mountain, and sitting with his disciples coming to him. By the sermon’s end, the people are astonished at his teaching. The disciples are addressed within the hearing of a wider company.

The opening blessings name the poor in spirit, those who mourn, the meek, those hungering and thirsting after righteousness, the merciful, the pure in heart, peacemakers, and those persecuted for righteousness’ sake. Each blessing has a promise. These are not descriptions of a life that everyone will admire or that avoids sorrow. Jesus names people whose circumstances may appear far from fortunate and speaks of what is theirs or what they shall receive.

Luke’s corresponding teaching must retain its own wording. He speaks to the poor and those hungry now, and includes woes to the rich, full, laughing, and universally praised. Do not silently add Matthew’s “in spirit” to Luke or remove it from Matthew. Comparing them should make each account clearer rather than produce a third, blended text.

### But I say unto you

Jesus calls his hearers salt and light, directing their good works toward the Father’s glory. He then says he came to fulfil the law and prophets, not destroy them. Read the following instructions beneath that declaration.

The sayings about murder and anger move into reconciliation with a brother. The sayings about adultery address the heart as well as the outward act. The teaching on oaths calls for truthful speech. Jesus repeatedly moves beyond a claim to have avoided the most visible offence and addresses the person’s relations, desires, and words.

The sermon then develops giving, prayer, fasting, treasure, judgment, asking, and the response to Jesus’ teaching. It is more than a list of admirable sayings. Its movement brings the hearer from blessing and vocation to searching instruction and finally to the question of doing what has been heard.

**Consider:** How do the promises shape the opening blessings? What does the instruction about reconciliation require beyond avoiding murder?

**Reflect:** Choose one passage in the sermon and read its whole paragraph before deciding what it asks of you.

## 02 After this manner therefore pray ye

**Main readings:** Matthew 6:1–18; 7:7–11.

### Your Father knoweth

Prayer appears between teaching about alms and fasting. Each can become a performance directed toward observers. Jesus contrasts being seen by others with being seen by the Father in secret. The issue is not simply whether an action is visible, since the previous chapter speaks of visible good works. The desired audience and reward matter: whose recognition is being sought?

He also rejects vain repetitions grounded in the assumption that much speaking secures a hearing. His reason directs the disciple away from trying to supply information God lacks.

> Be not ye therefore like unto them: for your Father knoweth what things ye have need of, before ye ask him.

Matthew 6:8 · KJV

The Father’s knowledge does not lead Jesus to abolish asking. He immediately teaches his hearers to pray. Dependence is expressed before one who already knows their need.

### Our Father

The prayer begins with the Father’s name, kingdom, and will. It then asks for daily bread, forgiveness, and deliverance from evil. These requests bring daily need into relation with God’s purposes. The repeated plural words—our, us, we—also prevent the prayer from being only an account of one person’s private concerns.

Jesus returns to forgiveness immediately afterward, warning against withholding it. That connection belongs to the teaching of prayer itself. A disciple cannot treat the request for mercy as unrelated to the treatment of other people.

In Matthew 7, asking is illustrated through parents giving children bread and fish rather than harmful substitutes. The conclusion concerns the heavenly Father giving good things. The invitation rests upon his goodness, rather than on a technique that obliges him to grant whatever the petitioner imagines.

**Consider:** What false assumptions about prayer does Jesus address before giving the prayer? Which requests concern shared needs?

**Reflect:** Pray through the requests slowly, attending to those you usually hurry past and to the people included in “us.”

## 03 Love your enemies

**Main readings:** Matthew 5:38–48; Luke 6:27–42. **Supporting reading:** Matthew 6:14–15.

### Do good, and pray

Jesus names responses to hostility: blessing, doing good, and praying for those who mistreat his hearers. Loving only those who return love does not distinguish the response he commands. Luke develops this through doing good and lending without making repayment the condition of kindness.

The reason concerns the Father. Matthew points to sun and rain given to the evil and good, the just and unjust. Luke describes God as kind to the unthankful and evil, then says:

> Be ye therefore merciful, as your Father also is merciful.

Luke 6:36 · KJV

Enemy-love therefore has content. It is not merely the absence of an angry feeling, nor does it depend upon pretending the enemy’s action was good. Jesus names hostility as hostility and commands a response shaped by the Father’s mercy.

### First cast out the beam

Luke continues with judgment, condemnation, forgiveness, and giving. The image of measure warns hearers to consider how they treat others. Read the promise about giving within this sequence of mercy rather than lifting it out as a calculation of financial return.

The blind guide and the beam in the eye expose a failure in the would-be corrector. Someone intent on removing a brother’s mote has neglected a much greater obstruction in himself. Jesus says to remove the beam first and then see clearly to help the brother. The correction addresses hypocrisy; it does not declare that truth and error are indistinguishable.

Matthew’s warning about withholding forgiveness adds seriousness to these relations. Receiving mercy and extending it are not separate topics in Jesus’ teaching. At the same time, these paragraphs do not narrate every step by which a broken relationship is restored. Forgiveness should not be made to mean that wrongdoing never happened or that every question of trust has already been answered.

**Consider:** What actions does Jesus name as expressions of love? Why does he address the corrector before the brother’s fault?

**Reflect:** Consider a concrete act of prayer, truthful self-examination, or kindness that would interrupt your usual response to hostility.

## 04 A man’s life consisteth not in the abundance

**Main reading:** Luke 12:13–34. **Comparison:** Matthew 6:19–34.

### Rich toward God

A request about dividing an inheritance prompts Jesus’ warning against covetousness. He does not settle the dispute as requested. Instead, he tells of a rich man whose ground brings forth plentifully and who plans larger barns, stored goods, and years of ease.

God’s answer interrupts the assumed years with this night. The man has considered where to put his goods but has treated his future life as though it were already secured. Jesus concludes by distinguishing laying up treasure for oneself from being rich toward God.

The harvest is not described as stolen, and the story does not identify every act of storing food as its target. The introductory warning and concluding sentence govern its meaning: possessions cannot supply the life their owner assumes they guarantee. Read the parable as part of the longer teaching, following the movement from covetousness to the disciples’ own concerns about provision.

### Fear not, little flock

Jesus then addresses his disciples about food, clothing, and anxious concern. Ravens and lilies direct attention to God’s provision and to the limits of human control. The Father knows these needs. Seeking his kingdom is placed before the pursuit that treats food and clothing as the whole horizon of life.

> Fear not, little flock; for it is your Father’s good pleasure to give you the kingdom.

Luke 12:32 · KJV

The reassurance is followed by a command to sell and give alms, and by teaching about lasting treasure. It therefore leads toward generosity rather than withdrawal from other people’s needs. Jesus concludes that the heart will be where the treasure is.

Matthew similarly joins treasure, the impossibility of serving God and mammon, and concern about daily provision. Both passages challenge what possessions can promise and direct the disciple toward the Father. Neither reduces the teaching to simply having fewer thoughts about money while leaving every use of it untouched.

**Consider:** What assumption does God’s answer overturn in the parable? How does the little flock’s reassurance prepare for the instruction to give?

**Reflect:** Consider what your use of possessions says about where you expect security and what you regard as lasting treasure.

## 05 Whosoever will be great among you

**Main reading:** Mark 10:32–45.

### Ye know not what ye ask

Jesus is going toward Jerusalem and tells the twelve of his coming condemnation, mistreatment, death, and resurrection. James and John then request places at his right and left in glory. Their request should be read beside the announcement that precedes it. Jesus has described suffering; they ask about position.

He responds with the cup he drinks and the baptism with which he is baptized. Their confident answer does not make them masters of the places they requested. He tells them they will share his cup and baptism, while the seats belong to those for whom they are prepared.

The other ten become displeased. Jesus gathers them all for instruction. The correction is not left as a private lesson for the two brothers while everyone else assumes the question of greatness is settled.

### Servant of all

Jesus contrasts the exercise of lordship among Gentile rulers with what is to be among his disciples. Greatness is expressed in ministering, and being chief in becoming servant of all. This reverses the brothers’ way of pursuing distinction.

> For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many.

Mark 10:45 · KJV

His own purpose supplies the reason. The verse speaks both of service and of giving his life a ransom for many. Do not reduce it to a leadership slogan that leaves out the death toward which the passage is moving. Nor should the service instruction become a subtler method of obtaining the highest seat.

The authority of Jesus is not used to excuse self-serving domination among those who follow him. His teaching directs ambition toward the good of others, with his own giving at its centre.

**Consider:** How does the journey announcement expose the problem with the request? Why does Jesus address all twelve?

**Reflect:** Consider a place where you want recognition. What would serving the people involved require if recognition did not follow?

## 06 Heareth these sayings of mine, and doeth them

**Main readings:** Matthew 7:13–29; Luke 6:43–49.

### Lord, Lord

Matthew’s sermon moves toward warnings about ways, false prophets, fruit, and claims to belong to Jesus. Those who say “Lord, Lord” do not enter merely by making the address. Even reported mighty works in his name do not substitute for the Father’s will. The passage places profession under judgment rather than allowing a claim to certify itself.

Luke’s teaching similarly moves from trees and fruit to the heart and speech, then to the question:

> And why call ye me, Lord, Lord, and do not the things which I say?

Luke 6:46 · KJV

The question makes the listener responsible for the relation between confession and conduct. It is not enough to admire the teaching or identify its speaker correctly while refusing what he says.

### Founded upon a rock

In both accounts, Jesus compares hearing and doing with building securely. Both kinds of builders hear. The difference is what follows hearing. Matthew describes rock and sand; Luke describes digging deep to lay a foundation and building without one. Retain those details within their respective accounts.

The storm does not create the distinction; it reveals the consequence of how the house was built. Read this after Matthew’s warning about the reckoning in that day. Applying the image to present difficulties can be useful, but must not displace its warning about the ultimate consequence of responding to Jesus. Neither account says that suffering itself proves a person failed to obey.

Matthew ends with the people astonished because Jesus teaches with authority. Their astonishment is the reported response; the builders have already made clear that hearing with amazement is not the whole response required. The sermon calls its audience to do what it has heard.

**Consider:** What do the two builders share? How does the preceding warning shape the meaning of the collapse?

**Reflect:** Return to one command in this study. Identify what doing it would involve beyond agreeing that it is true.

Jesus’ teaching connects the heart with speech, prayer with forgiveness, possessions with trust, and greatness with service. Read the argument, receive the reasons he gives, and let the closing question remain personal: what follows hearing?
