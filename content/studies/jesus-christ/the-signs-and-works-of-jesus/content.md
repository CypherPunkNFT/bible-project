# The signs and works of Jesus

Concepts: The deed · The word · The witnesses
Motto: The deed is short. / The account is not.
Lead: A storm stops in {{stormDeed}} verse. A blind man sees in two, and the chapter runs to {{blindVerses}}. The Gospel writers tell what Jesus did, and around it they set what He said, what they say it shows, and who believed or turned away. This study reads {{accounts}} accounts whole, and teaches one habit: do not read only the deed.

> But these are written so that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that by believing you may have life in His name.

John 20:31 · BSB

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. The site's [Miracles] page keeps the catalogue: {{catalogue}} works of Jesus, after A. T. Robertson's list. These {{accounts}} accounts hold {{works}} of them, one or more from each of its {{catalogueKinds}} kinds, because the first draft of this study chose them. Mark folds {{foldWorks}} works together in a single telling, so it is counted once. A verse is sorted by what it mostly does, and that is this study's judgment. Verse numbers were added long after the Gospels were written, so they are a ruler laid on the text, not the writers' own measure.

ThreadLead: Where does the deed fall, and how much stands around it? Each cord is one account, and each length of cord is one verse, all to one scale. Its stroke says what that verse mostly does, and a star marks the deed. Point at a verse or tap it to read it here, then open the whole account. Choose a kind to see where it falls.
ThreadReads: {{verses}} verses, and {{deed}} of them tell the deed. Mark's feeding ends right on it, and John 9 has {{blindAfter}} verses after it. That does not make the deed small. Everything else stands on it: with no sight there is no dispute in John 9, and with no man out of the tomb there is no council in John 11. In these accounts, the writers do not stop at the deed.
ThreadCare: Which kind a verse belongs to is this study's reading, by one rule. If the work happens in the verse, it is the deed: "Lazarus, come out!" (John 11:43) is Jesus speaking and the deed itself. Otherwise, if the writer steps out to explain, it is the writer. If Jesus speaks, moves or His heart is shown, it is Jesus. If anyone else speaks or acts, it is others. Anything left is the setting. Many verses do two things, so open any account and judge for yourself.
LensDeed: The deed: {{deed}} verses of {{verses}}. In Cana the change is not narrated at all. We are told only of "the water that had been turned into wine" (John 2:9).
LensJesus: Jesus speaks, asks or shows His heart: {{jesus}} verses. They crowd around Lazarus, and they include the shortest verse here: "Jesus wept." John 11:35
LensWriter: The writer explains: {{writer}} verses. They are few, and they do heavy work: "He thus revealed His glory" (John 2:11), and "they had not understood about the loaves" (Mark 6:52).
LensOthers: Others speak and respond: {{others}} verses, the largest kind. In John 9 Jesus is not on the scene for {{absent}} verses in a row, and the man He healed is left to answer for himself.
LensSetting: The setting and the need: {{setting}} verses. They say how bad it was: twelve years, chains that broke, four days in the tomb.

ReadingsLead: The same accounts in {{readings}} readings. Each opens with its own cord from above and a form its own passage gives. What the passage says, how this study reads it and what it may ask of you stay apart.

RoadsLead: The same deed did not do the same thing to everyone who saw it. Here is what each account says people did next, in its own words. Read across.
RoadsReads: A sign does not force anyone. Fear turns up on every side: in the woman who came forward, in the town that sent Him away, and in the disciples in the boat. Amazement is not yet faith. After Lazarus, John draws the fork plainly: many "believed in Him" (John 11:45), and "some of them went to the Pharisees" (John 11:46). Then the council plotted. John says it outright: "they still did not believe in Him" (John 12:37).
RoadsCare: These are the responses of particular people: disciples, neighbours, parents, some Pharisees and a council. John tells of many Jews who believed (John 11:45), and Jesus and all His first followers were Jews. The parents in John 9:22 feared "the Jews" and were Jews themselves, so there the word means the authorities. Nothing here lays blame on a people, and "every person of every race possesses full dignity and is worthy of respect and Christian love" (Article 3).

ScriptureLead: Jesus speaks about them, older Scripture stands behind them, and the apostles preach them. Over each older line it says who draws the link: the Gospel writer, the people in the scene or this study.
ScriptureReads: Jesus points to His works, and He refuses to perform on demand. Both are true. A sign points beyond itself, and someone who wants only the bread has missed where it points: "it is not because you saw these signs that you are looking for Me, but because you ate the loaves" (John 6:26).

TryLead: {{Tries}} accounts that are not on the cords above, with no marks yet. Choose the verse or verses that tell the deed. The page then marks that account, so you can see what the writer did next. There is no score, and your choice is not kept.

QuestionsLead: {{Questions}} questions a careful reader will ask. Each opens on its passage, then this study's reading, then what others have written, labelled as theirs. Alexander Maclaren was a Baptist, and Albert Barnes a Presbyterian; they are older writers held in the site's library, read only at these notes.

## 01 Go in peace

Slug: peace
Account: fold
Read: Mark 5:25–34
Ask: Why does the account go on after she is healed?
Hook: She is healed in {{womanDeed}} verse. Jesus will not let it end there.

### After the bleeding stopped

Mark gives her history first: twelve years, many physicians, everything she had spent, and worse at the end of it. She comes behind Jesus, touches His cloak, and "immediately her bleeding stopped" (Mark 5:29). If the story were only about a cure, it could end at that verse.

It does not. Jesus turns and asks who touched His garments. His disciples point at the crowd. He keeps looking. She comes trembling, falls down before Him and tells "the whole truth" (Mark 5:33). {{AfterWoman}} more verses go to what the healing had left undone: she has been cured, and she has not yet been spoken to.

> "Daughter," said Jesus, "your faith has healed you. Go in peace and be free of your affliction."

Mark 5:34 · BSB

[read] He names her faith, and He calls her "Daughter" (Mark 5:34). Mark gives her no other name, and nowhere else in Mark does Jesus call anyone that. This study reads the extra verses as the point: Jesus wanted more for her than a secret cure, and gave it to her in public, where she could hear it.

[care] A cloak is not a method, and faith here is not a quantity. Mark records twelve years of suffering and does not call them proof of weak faith. Her physicians are part of her history, not a rule against doctors. If you are ill, see the first hard question below.

### Someone else was waiting

Mark tells this while Jesus is on the way to a dying child, and "while He was still speaking" (Mark 5:35) the news comes that she is dead. Keep that sequence. Mark puts the news here and does not say the pause changed anything. [Encounters] reads this fold for the people in it. Here it is read for the deed and what follows it.

Consider: What does the woman have at Mark 5:29, and what does she have by Mark 5:34? What does Jesus add?
Reflect: When Jesus has helped you, have you stopped at the help? Is there something you have not yet told Him, the whole truth of it?

## 02 How much the Lord has done

Slug: told
Account: legion
Read: Mark 5:1–20
Ask: Who asks, and who is told no?
Hook: Everyone in this story begs Jesus for something. Only one is plainly told no, and he is the one who asked to stay with Him.

### The strength no one had

Mark says plainly that this man has "an unclean spirit" (Mark 5:2). Chains had not held him, and "no one" had "the strength to subdue him" (Mark 5:4). He lives among tombs and cuts himself with stones. The disciples have just asked who Jesus is. On the far shore, the voice in this man says it: "Jesus, Son of the Most High God" (Mark 5:7).

The deed takes {{legionDeed}} verse. Jesus gives permission, and the man who could not be held is found "sitting there, clothed and in his right mind" (Mark 5:15). Nothing here is a struggle of equals. The spirits ask, and He allows.

[care] Mark names a spirit in this man. That gives no one a way of naming spirits in the people around them. Scripture can list the demon-possessed apart from the sick (Matthew 4:24) and can name a spirit behind a bodily trouble (Luke 13:11). Only the text can say which, and this page does not diagnose anyone. If you are hurting yourself, tell someone today and ask for medical help, and if you are in danger now, call emergency services. Doing so does not contradict this passage.

### Who begged, and what came of it

[read] The form above comes from the text. In the Berean the word beg comes five times, and the answers run the opposite way from what we might expect. The spirits get leave. The town gets its wish, and He goes. The freed man asks to be with Him and is refused, and this study does not hear that as a rebuke. Jesus gives him another work: home, and his own people.

[source:maclaren] Maclaren noticed the same pattern in the older English: "The word 'besought' occurs four times in it, and we may group the details round each instance." The Berean's fifth is in Mark 5:7, where the older English says something else.

> But Jesus would not allow him. "Go home to your own people," He said, "and tell them how much the Lord has done for you, and what mercy He has shown you."

Mark 5:19 · BSB

Then Mark makes a small move that is easy to miss. Jesus says to tell how much "the Lord has done" (Mark 5:19), and the man goes and proclaims "how much Jesus had done for him" (Mark 5:20). Mark sets the two sentences side by side and adds no comment. This study reads that as Mark's point about who Jesus is, and it says so as a reading.

Consider: List the five requests. Which does Mark answer? Why might the man's be the one refused? What does Jesus tell him to include when he tells it?
Reflect: Has Jesus sent you back to your own people when you wanted to go somewhere else with Him? What has He done for you that they have not yet heard?

## 03 They all ate and were satisfied

Slug: fed
Account: loaves, cana
Read: Mark 6:30–44; John 2:1–12
Ask: Where is the moment it happened?
Hook: Mark counts everything except the moment. John tells us who knew.

### Compassion first

Jesus meant to rest with His apostles. The crowd arrives before the boat does, and He "had compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd" (Mark 6:34). What He does first is teach. The bread comes later, in the same compassion. The disciples say to send them away, and He says, "You give them something to eat." Mark 6:37

After that come the counts: the cost they cannot pay, five loaves, two fish, groups by hundreds and fifties, twelve basketfuls left over, five thousand men. What he does not do is show the bread growing. Jesus blesses and breaks and gives. Then "they all ate and were satisfied" (Mark 6:42).

[read] The missing moment is not an invitation to doubt. Twelve baskets of pieces from five loaves is not a story of people sharing lunches they had hidden, and the text gives no hint of that. The writers show us the result and leave the reader looking at the one who did it.

### He thus revealed His glory

At Cana, the wine runs out at a wedding. Jesus' mother tells Him, and He answers that His hour has not yet come. She tells the servants to do what He says. Six stone jars are filled. No word is spoken over them. John then tells us only that the master of the banquet "tasted the water that had been turned into wine" (John 2:9).

> Jesus performed this, the first of His signs, at Cana in Galilee. He thus revealed His glory, and His disciples believed in Him.

John 2:11 · BSB

[read] John says what the sign is for, so we need not guess. This is one of the places where a writer stops and explains, and it is the one to hold on to. The six jars had been set for "the Jewish rites of purification" (John 2:6), and readers have made much of that. John does not comment on it, and this study does not make a code of the jars or the number.

Consider: Who knew what at Cana? What does each writer say beyond naming what was supplied?
Reflect: When you read of a provision like this, do you ask for the same, or do you ask who this is? What would asking both sound like?

## 04 Even the wind and the sea obey Him

Slug: sea
Account: storm, walking
Read: Mark 4:35–41; Mark 6:45–52
Ask: What had they failed to understand?
Hook: Two crossings, two nights of wind, and the same words at the end of both: the wind died down. After the second, Mark tells us what they had not understood.

### Who is this?

Jesus is asleep in the stern, and the boat is filling. They wake Him with a charge: "Teacher, don't You care that we are perishing?" (Mark 4:38). He speaks to the wind and the sea as you would speak to someone who could hear. Then He asks about their fear. And now they are more afraid than before:

> Overwhelmed with fear, they asked one another, "Who is this, that even the wind and the sea obey Him?"

Mark 4:41 · BSB

[read] Mark does not answer the question there. He leaves it with the reader. Psalm 107:29 had already said who does this: "He calmed the storm to a whisper". Mark does not cite that psalm, so this link is this study's, and it is set out with the others further down.

### They had not understood about the loaves

The second time, He is not in the boat. He sees them straining at the oars, comes to them on the sea, and says, "Take courage! It is I. Do not be afraid." Mark 6:50 The wind dies down in the same words as before. And Mark stops and explains:

> for they had not understood about the loaves, but their hearts had been hardened.

Mark 6:52 · BSB

[read] They had carried the baskets that afternoon. They had seen the deed, handled what was left, and not seen what it showed. Later Jesus Himself goes back to it, baskets and all, and asks, "Do you still not understand?" (Mark 8:21). Seeing a work and understanding it are two different things, and Mark says so of the men closest to Him.

[source:barnes] Barnes softens the verse. He writes that the hardening does not mean they were opposed to Jesus, "but simply, that they were slow to perceive his power". Mark's charge stands, both failures of it, and Jesus repeats it (Mark 8:17). The same disciples go on with Him.

[care] These crossings show who Jesus is. They do not promise that every danger you face will stop tonight. Paul was shipwrecked three times (2 Corinthians 11:25), and he did not lose his faith or his Lord in the water.

Consider: Set the two crossings side by side. What is the same, what is different, and why does Mark refer back to the loaves?
Reflect: What has Jesus done in your life that you have remembered without yet understanding what it shows about Him?

## 05 I am the resurrection and the life

Slug: raised
Account: lazarus
Read: John 11:1–44; Mark 5:35–43
Ask: To whom does He speak, and what does He say?
Hook: He stays away two days. He weeps at the tomb. He thanks the Father aloud. He speaks to many before and after one loud sentence.

### Before the stone is moved

John tells us that Jesus loved Martha, Mary and Lazarus, and in the next breath that "He stayed where He was for two days" (John 11:6). Both stand, and John does not soften either. [Encounters] takes up the delay and the sentence the two sisters share. Here we follow what Jesus says, because He says a great deal before the deed.

To Martha, who believes in the resurrection "at the last day" (John 11:24), He says this:

> Jesus said to her, "I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in Me will live, even though he dies.

John 11:25 · BSB

[read] He does not correct her about the last day. He moves her hope onto Himself. And she answers with the same confession John wrote his book to bring about: "You are the Christ, the Son of God" (John 11:27). That comes before any stone has moved.

### One loud sentence

He comes to the tomb "deeply moved" (John 11:38). Jesus knows what He is about to do, and He weeps anyway (John 11:35). He thanks the Father aloud "for the benefit of the people standing here, so they may believe that You sent Me" (John 11:42). So Jesus Himself says what the deed is for. Then He calls, the dead man comes out still bound, and Jesus tells others to unwrap him.

Mark's account of Jairus' daughter is smaller and has the same care in it. There is a hand, a word in her own language, and then an instruction that everyone might forget: "give her something to eat" (Mark 5:43).

[source:barnes] Barnes on that last line: "He had raised her by extraordinary power, but he willed that she should be sustained by ordinary means."

[read] Jesus told the freed man at Gerasa to tell, and He told Jairus' house that no one should know (Mark 5:43). Mark does not say why the instructions differ, and this study does not invent one rule to cover them.

[care] These are particular people raised at His command, and they would die again. They do not promise any grieving family the same reversal today. The promise that holds for every believer is the one He gave Martha, and the confession's plain word that "the dead will be raised" (Article 10) when He returns. Grief stays in this story right up to the tomb. It may stay in yours.

Consider: What does Jesus say about believing before He calls Lazarus? How do His tears and His authority stand in the same account?
Reflect: Where do you most want Him to have come sooner? Can you say that to Him, as Martha and Mary did, and then hear what He said to Martha?

## 06 So that you may believe

Slug: believe
Account: blind
Read: John 9:1–41; John 11:45–53; John 20:30–31
Ask: Who sees, and who only claims to?
Hook: A man who could not see comes to worship. Those who examine him end by throwing him out.

### One thing I do know

His disciples ask whose sin caused the blindness. Jesus rejects both answers they offer and says it happened "so that the works of God would be displayed in him" (John 9:3). He makes mud, sends him to wash, and in {{blindDeed}} verses the man can see.

Then Jesus leaves the scene for {{absent}} verses. The man faces his neighbours, then the Pharisees, then a second hearing. His parents will not speak for him, "because they were afraid" (John 9:22). He has no argument prepared. He has what happened: "I was blind, but now I see!" John 9:25 Step by step, what he says of Jesus grows, and what they say of Jesus hardens. The form above lays the two side by side.

[read] Jesus says neither this man's sin nor his parents' caused it. That answers this case, and it is a strong warning against reading anyone's illness as a verdict. It does not say that no act ever has a consequence in the body. And when the man says that "God does not listen to sinners" (John 9:31), that is his argument in a dispute, not a rule John gives us about prayer.

Jesus finds him after he is thrown out. "Lord, I believe," he said. And he worshiped Jesus. John 9:38 The chapter ends on those who are sure they can see: "your guilt remains" (John 9:41).

### Written for you

John 9 and John 11 both end in a fork, set out in section 03 below. John knows that his readers were not standing there, and he tells them what his book is.

> Jesus performed many other signs in the presence of His disciples, which are not written in this book.

John 20:30 · BSB

John chose what he wrote, and he tells us why, in the verse that opens this study. You were not there, and the writing was made for you. The aim is more than amazement. It is that you believe and have life in His name.

Consider: How does the man's word for Jesus change across John 9? How does John separate seeing a work from receiving what it shows?
Reflect: What have these accounts brought you to ask about Jesus Himself, beyond how a thing happened?

## Question faith: If her faith healed her, was my faith too small?

Text: Mark 5:34

Jesus says to her, "your faith has healed you" (Mark 5:34). He says it to her, of her. He gives no amount, and He does not make it a rule for everyone who is ill.

[read] Scripture will not let this verse become a measure. A father cries, "I do believe; help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24), and his son is healed. Paul pleads three times to have his thorn taken away, and the answer is, "My grace is sufficient for you" (2 Corinthians 12:9). Paul, who worked signs, left Trophimus "sick in Miletus" (2 Timothy 4:20), and he told Timothy to take something for his "frequent ailments" (1 Timothy 5:23). Nobody in those passages is told that illness remained because faith fell short.

[read] So this site does not teach that healing is owed to enough faith, or that continued illness shows too little. Prayer for healing is right and commanded (James 5:14–15). James says the prayer offered in faith "will restore the one who is sick" (James 5:15), and Trophimus shows this cannot mean that every illness ends now. We still wait, with Paul, for "the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:23).

[care] Being told that you are still sick because you did not believe enough is a cruelty, and the Gospels do not say it. If someone has said this to you, read 2 Corinthians 12:9 again. And see your doctor: nothing here stands against that.

## Question could: "He could not perform any miracles there." Could He not?

Text: Mark 6:5

In Nazareth, Mark says, "He could not perform any miracles there, except to lay His hands on a few of the sick and heal them" (Mark 6:5). The next verse says He "was amazed at their unbelief" (Mark 6:6). Matthew tells the same visit in other words: "He did not do many miracles there, because of their unbelief" (Matthew 13:58).

[source:barnes] Barnes reads it as what would serve: "We are not to suppose that his power was limited by the belief, or unbelief, of men." The people were so set against Him, he says, that a miracle would have convinced no one.

[source:maclaren] Maclaren refuses to soften Mark's word: "Startling as the expression is, it is not to be weakened down because it is startling". He takes it that unbelief truly hindered what Christ gave there.

[read] This study does not choose between those two ways of putting it. Both keep what the text keeps: He did heal a few, and unbelief there was no small thing. Neither makes Jesus weak, and the site's voice holds what the account of the storm shows. Nor does this verse turn every unhealed person into an unbeliever. It is about a hometown that took offense at Him (Mark 6:3).

## Question spirits: Was it a demon, or an illness? And what about the pigs?

Text: Mark 5:1–20

Mark names an unclean spirit in this man. Scripture can list them apart: people brought those "ill with various diseases", "the demon-possessed" and "those having seizures" (Matthew 4:24). It can also name a spirit behind a bodily trouble: a woman "disabled by a spirit" (Luke 13:11). Only the text can say which. Peter sums up Jesus' work as "healing all who were oppressed by the devil" (Acts 10:38).

[source:maclaren] Maclaren takes Mark at his word: "It paints for us not merely a maniac, but a demoniac."

[read] So this study holds both things. Spiritual powers are real in these accounts, and Jesus is never in doubt against them: "if I drive out demons by the finger of God, then the kingdom of God has come upon you" (Luke 11:20). And no passage gives a reader a way to look at someone in distress and name a spirit. The Baptist Faith and Message says little on this, and a study on angels and spiritual powers is still to be written.

[read] Why the pigs? Mark does not say. He tells us that the spirits asked, that Jesus allowed it, and that the people of the region, once they had heard about the man and the pigs, asked Him to go (Mark 5:16–17). It does not explain more.

[source:barnes] Matthew tells of two men where Mark and Luke tell of one (Matthew 8:28). Barnes observes that "neither Mark nor Luke say that there was no more than one". Mark tells of the man who went on to proclaim Him.

## Question today: Does Jesus still do this?

Text: Hebrews 2:3–4

The Baptist Faith and Message says that the Spirit "bestows the spiritual gifts by which they serve God through His church" (Article 2), and it does not say whether gifts of healing and miracles continue. Baptists differ, and this page does not choose. What they share is more than what divides them: God can heal, Christians should ask Him to, and every claim must be tested against Scripture and the facts.

[source:schreiner] Thomas Schreiner, a Southern Baptist seminary professor, argues that these gifts were chiefly given to accredit the gospel in the apostles' time, on a foundation laid once (Ephesians 2:20), and he doubts that they are a regular part of church life now. He still believes God heals, sometimes dramatically, and that we should pray for it. He adds that claims need checking, as the people checked the blind man's healing (John 9:18–19).

[source:piper] John Piper, for 33 years pastor of Bethlehem Baptist Church in Minneapolis, set out both cases in 1990. Stephen, Philip and the Galatian churches saw miracles (Acts 6:8; Acts 8:6; Galatians 3:5), and Paul lists gifts of healing apart from apostles (1 Corinthians 12:9). He says he is not fully persuaded by the case that they ceased, though he honours what was unique about Jesus and the apostles.

[care] Anyone who promises healing for money, or as what you are owed, is not teaching from these accounts. A page like this cannot tell you what God will do for you. Pray, ask your elders to pray, and see your doctor.

## Question happened: Did these things really happen?

Text: John 20:30–31

The writers report these as things that happened: "as you yourselves know", Peter tells a crowd in Jerusalem (Acts 2:22). The council in John 11 has every reason to deny the signs, and it does not (John 11:47).

[read] This site holds with the Baptist Faith and Message that Scripture has "truth, without any mixture of error" (Article 1), so it reads these as events. The site's apologetics answer takes up the larger question of whether a reasonable person can believe in miracles at all. Explanations that remove the deed, such as a crowd sharing hidden lunches or a storm that happened to die down, do not come from these accounts. With their counts, their tombs and their witnesses, these accounts leave no room for them.

[read] A writer's care for what a work means does not make the work less real. It is the reverse: the meaning stands on the event. The whole New Testament rests on Christ's own resurrection, as Paul says (1 Corinthians 15:14). What this study has asked is that you read on past the deed, not that you read around it.

## Close: Read past the deed

Close: You have seen the marks. A few bright ones, and then many verses of people speaking, asking, afraid, believing, leaving.
Way: Take this to the next account you read: What did He do? What did He say? What does the writer say it shows? Who believed, and who turned away? Then read on past the deed.
Invite: John wrote for someone who was not there. If you have never trusted Christ, these signs were written for you. Christ died for our sins and was raised (1 Corinthians 15:3–4), and His call is "Repent and believe in the gospel" (Mark 1:15). This site holds with the Baptist Faith and Message that "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord" (Article 4). The man at Siloam had only a little to say at first. You can come to where he came: "Lord, I believe" (John 9:38).
Question: Is there something you have asked Him to do? Have you also asked who He is?
Again: Read the passage again before you answer.
