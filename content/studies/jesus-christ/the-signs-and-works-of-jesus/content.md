# The signs and works of Jesus

**Study 18 · Jesus Christ · First content draft · 2026-10-13**

The Gospel accounts tell what Jesus does and what people make of it. A person is healed, a crowd is fed, a storm ceases, or someone dead comes forth. These events lead to questions, testimony, worship, fear, and sometimes a determination to oppose him.

Read the works with the words that surround them. Jesus explains what he is doing; the narrators identify connections that witnesses do not always understand. These selected readings follow healing, deliverance, provision, authority over creation, raising the dead, and the differing responses to his works.

## 01 Go in peace

**Main reading:** Mark 5:21–43, with particular attention to verses 25–34.

### Who touched my clothes?

Jesus is on his way to Jairus’s dying daughter when a woman approaches through the crowd. Mark tells of twelve years of bleeding, much suffering, money spent, and worsening illness. Her need is not a passing inconvenience. Having heard of Jesus, she comes behind him and touches his garment, believing that even touching his clothes will make her whole.

Mark reports the healing immediately. Jesus then turns and asks who touched his clothes. The disciples point to the crowd pressing around him, but the woman comes forward, fearful and trembling, and tells him the truth. The account continues beyond the bodily change to an encounter in which she is heard and addressed.

### Daughter

> And he said unto her, Daughter, thy faith hath made thee whole; go in peace, and be whole of thy plague.

Mark 5:34 · KJV

Jesus names her faith and sends her in peace. Her approach is personal, and his answer is personal. The story should not become a method in which a garment, a gesture, or a sufficient quantity of confidence automatically produces a result. Its subject is this woman’s coming to Jesus and what he does and says.

While he is still speaking, news arrives that Jairus’s daughter has died. Mark places the two needs together: the woman’s healing occurs during the journey to the child. Keep that sequence rather than treating the encounter as an isolated example with no cost of waiting felt elsewhere in the narrative.

The woman’s years of suffering are not explained as proof of deficient faith. The account records her history and Jesus’ response without making her experience a rule for judging everyone who remains ill.

**Consider:** Why does the account continue after the woman is healed? How does the surrounding story affect the way you read the interruption?

**Reflect:** Notice whether your attention stops at an outcome or also attends to the person, the history of suffering, and Jesus’ words.

## 02 Tell them how great things the Lord hath done

**Main reading:** Mark 5:1–20.

### Come out of the man

Across the sea, Jesus meets a man who lives among the tombs. Mark explicitly identifies an unclean spirit and describes repeated, unsuccessful attempts to restrain the man. The misery of his condition and the failure of human restraint prepare the reader to see what changes when Jesus commands the spirit to leave.

The account names Legion, the appeal concerning the swine, Jesus’ permission, and the herd’s destruction. These details belong to the event Mark tells. They should not be converted into a system for identifying spirits in other people or explaining every kind of distress. The narrator’s explicit identification is the basis for calling this deliverance from an unclean spirit.

### Go home to thy friends

Those who come out find the man sitting, clothed, and in his right mind. They are afraid and ask Jesus to depart. The changed man, by contrast, asks to be with him. These requests move in opposite directions even though they concern the same work.

> Howbeit Jesus suffered him not, but saith unto him, Go home to thy friends, and tell them how great things the Lord hath done for thee, and hath had compassion on thee.

Mark 5:19 · KJV

Jesus gives him a task among his own people. Mark then says he proclaims in Decapolis what Jesus has done for him, and people marvel. Compassion belongs within the account’s own explanation, alongside the authority displayed.

Do not assume the man is refused a meaningful part because he does not join the boat. His return becomes the setting of his testimony. Nor does Mark explain every motive of those who ask Jesus to leave. Their fear and request are stated; a complete account of their inner reasoning is not.

**Consider:** How do the requests of the people and the delivered man differ? What does Jesus tell him to include in his testimony?

**Reflect:** Consider how telling what has been done for you differs from speculating about what has not been disclosed.

## 03 They did all eat, and were filled

**Main readings:** Mark 6:30–44; John 2:1–12.

### Moved with compassion

The apostles return to Jesus, and he invites them to rest. The crowds arrive ahead of them. Mark says Jesus is moved with compassion because they are like sheep without a shepherd, and he begins teaching them. As the day passes, the disciples propose sending them away to buy food. Jesus tells the disciples to feed them.

The five loaves and two fishes are identified before the people sit down. Jesus blesses and breaks the bread, and the disciples distribute it. All eat and are filled, and twelve baskets of fragments and fish remain. Mark reports both the need and the sufficiency of the provision.

The teaching and the feeding belong to the same compassionate response. The disciples participate, but the narrative does not present them as discovering an adequate supply through their own calculations. Neither does it describe the event merely as a lesson that persuaded the crowd to share hidden lunches.

### Manifested forth his glory

At Cana, the need arises within a wedding. Jesus’ mother tells him there is no wine; his reply mentions his hour, and she directs the servants to do what he says. They fill the stone waterpots and take what is drawn to the ruler of the feast. John distinguishes what the ruler knows from what the servants know.

> This beginning of miracles did Jesus in Cana of Galilee, and manifested forth his glory; and his disciples believed on him.

John 2:11 · KJV

John supplies the significance explicitly: glory is manifested and the disciples believe. Read that conclusion before inventing a symbolic identity for every vessel, quantity, or participant. The provision is part of what happens; John also directs attention to the one whose glory it reveals.

**Consider:** What does each narrator say beyond identifying the supplied food or drink? Who sees, serves, understands, or believes?

**Reflect:** Read one of these accounts again with attention to its stated purpose, rather than only to the size or strangeness of the provision.

## 04 Even the wind and the sea obey him

**Main readings:** Mark 4:35–41; 6:45–52.

### Peace, be still

Jesus proposes crossing the sea. During the crossing, a storm fills the boat with water while he sleeps. The disciples wake him with a question about whether he cares that they perish. He rebukes the wind and commands the sea; the wind ceases and there is a great calm.

His question about their fear and faith is followed by their own question about him:

> And they feared exceedingly, and said one to another, What manner of man is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?

Mark 4:41 · KJV

The end is more than relief from danger. The disciples must reckon with the identity of someone whom wind and sea obey. Their fear changes its immediate object, and the narrator leaves their question before the reader.

### They considered not the miracle of the loaves

After the feeding, Jesus sends the disciples ahead by boat and goes to pray. He sees them struggling against a contrary wind and comes walking on the sea. They think they have seen a spirit and cry out. He speaks reassurance, enters the boat, and the wind ceases.

Mark explains their extraordinary amazement by saying they had not understood the miracle of the loaves and that their heart was hardened. This is an explicit connection between the two events. They have participated in a work without yet grasping what it should show them about Jesus.

Read these crossings as narrated encounters with his authority. An application about trusting him in difficulty may follow, but it should not replace the sea, boat, commands, and question of identity with a story entirely about inward feelings. Nor do the accounts promise that every danger a reader faces will cease immediately.

**Consider:** What question remains after the first storm ceases? Why does Mark refer back to the loaves after the second crossing?

**Reflect:** Consider the difference between remembering an event and understanding what the account says it reveals about Jesus.

## 05 I am the resurrection, and the life

**Main readings:** John 11:1–44; Mark 5:35–43.

### Believest thou this?

John names Jesus’ love for Martha, Mary, and Lazarus before describing his remaining where he is for two more days. His delay and his love must both remain in the reading. The account does not invite the reader to erase either because the other is difficult to understand.

Jesus tells the disciples plainly that Lazarus is dead. When he arrives, Lazarus has been in the grave four days. Martha speaks of resurrection at the last day, and Jesus directs her to himself as the resurrection and the life. Her answer confesses him as the Christ, the Son of God.

The conversation is not a denial that Lazarus has died. Jesus has already said so. It concerns the life found in him and the belief to which he calls Martha before the tomb is opened.

### Lazarus, come forth

Jesus encounters Mary’s tears and those of the mourners. He is troubled and weeps. The coming work does not make grief disappear from the story or make compassion unnecessary. At the tomb he commands the stone to be removed, and Martha names the consequence of four days of death.

Jesus thanks the Father and explains that he speaks for the people’s sake, that they may believe the Father sent him. He calls Lazarus forth. John reports the dead man coming out in graveclothes and Jesus directing others to loose him.

Mark’s account of Jairus’s daughter likewise includes a death report, Jesus’ word, astonishment, and practical care: the girl is to be given food. Keep the scenes distinct. Jairus’s household is charged to keep the event quiet; the man delivered earlier in Mark 5 is sent to tell what happened.

These accounts concern particular people raised at Jesus’ command. They disclose his authority and compassion without promising every bereaved family the same immediate reversal.

**Consider:** What does Jesus say about belief before calling Lazarus? How do his tears and his authority appear within the same account?

**Reflect:** Allow both grief and hope to remain present as you read, without requiring one to prove that the other is absent.

## 06 That believing ye might have life

**Main readings:** John 9:1–41; 11:45–53; 20:30–31.

### Whereas I was blind, now I see

John 9 opens with a question about whose sin caused a man’s blindness from birth. Jesus rejects the proposed explanation and speaks of God’s works being manifested in him. He gives the man sight. The chapter then follows the questioning that develops around the work.

Neighbours debate the man’s identity; the Pharisees divide over Jesus; the parents confirm their son’s former blindness but fear the consequences of confessing Jesus as Christ. The man repeatedly testifies to what happened and argues about what it means. Their different responses should not be flattened into a single reaction by an entire people.

After the man is cast out, Jesus finds him and speaks of the Son of God. He believes and worships. The conclusion brings physical sight into relation with recognition and the claim to see. The healed man’s testimony develops into a personal response to Jesus.

### Many believed; they took counsel

After Lazarus is raised, many who saw believe in Jesus. Some report what happened to the Pharisees. The resulting council acknowledges his many miracles and fears Roman intervention. From that day they plan his death. A work that gives life thus stands near the decision to put Jesus to death.

John also distinguishes Caiaphas’s argument from the further significance the narrator sees in his words: Jesus would die not only for the nation but gather God’s scattered children. Keep the speaker’s intention and the narrator’s explanation visible.

Near the Gospel’s end, John states why selected signs have been written:

> But these are written, that ye might believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God; and that believing ye might have life through his name.

John 20:31 · KJV

The written witness addresses readers who were not present. Its aim is not simply amazement at unusual events but belief in Jesus and life through his name. The variety of responses within the accounts makes that invitation searching rather than automatic.

**Consider:** How does John distinguish witnessing a work from receiving its significance? What does his stated purpose ask of the reader?

**Reflect:** Consider what these accounts have brought you to ask about Jesus himself, beyond asking how an event occurred.

The works belong with the words, the people helped, and the responses that follow. Read the whole account: what Jesus does, what he says, what the narrator explains, and what the witnesses come to believe or refuse.
