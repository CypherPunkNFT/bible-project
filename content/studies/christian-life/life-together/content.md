# Life together: belonging to a church

Concepts: One another · Burdens · Confession · Restoring · Gathering · Unity

Motto: Loved first. / Then one another.

Lead: The New Testament tells believers again and again what to do for "one another": love, carry burdens, confess, forgive, correct, encourage, agree. This study teaches one habit for reading those commands. Before you ask what you must do for the other person, find what God has already done for you in the same paragraph.

> A new commandment I give you: Love one another. As I have loved you, so you also must love one another. By this everyone will know that you are My disciples, if you love one another.”

John 13:34–35 · BSB

Frame: In this study the word *church* never means a building. It means people. Most often it means a local church: a group of believers who meet together, know one another and answer to one another. The confession this site writes under, the Baptist Faith and Message 2000, calls a local church an "autonomous local congregation of baptized believers". Autonomous means self-governing. A *believer* is someone who has trusted Jesus Christ. Belonging to a church means sharing your life with these people, and the New Testament has a great deal to say about how that is done.

Most of what it says comes in two parts. Look at Jesus' words above. The command is "Love one another." The ground under it is "As I have loved you." The command does not stand by itself. Paul writes "Accept one another, then, just as Christ accepted you" (Romans 15:7). Hebrews says "Let us hold resolutely to the hope we profess, for He who promised is faithful" (Hebrews 10:23), and only then turns to what believers owe each other. Read the commands without the ground, and the church becomes a club with rules. Read the ground first, and the commands become the way a family lives that was taken in by the same Father.

So here is the habit this study teaches. When you meet a "one another", stop and look for the "as" or the "because" beside it. Find the gift. Then read the command.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. The study walks eight passages, chosen because each gives a paragraph to one part of shared life: love, carrying burdens, confessing and forgiving, correcting and restoring, meeting together and unity. It does not walk the picture of the church as a body (1 Corinthians 12) or the household of God (Ephesians 2); those belong to *The church and its calling*. It quotes only a line or two on differences of conscience, which *Faith in daily life* walks, and on baptism and church membership, which *Baptism and the Lord's Supper: the two things Jesus told His church to do* explains. This study reads Scripture. It is not legal, medical or counselling advice.

[care] If someone in a church has harmed you, or is harming you now, part 04 begins with where to find help. You do not have to read the rest of this study first.

## 01 Love one another

**Main readings:** John 13:34–35; 1 Corinthians 13

Hook: On the night before He died, Jesus gave one command and set its measure beside it.

### The measure of the new command

John 13 happens at a supper on the last night before Jesus' death. He has just washed His disciples' feet, the work of a household servant (John 13:1–17). Judas has gone out into the night to betray Him (John 13:30). Then Jesus says, "A new commandment I give you" (John 13:34).

Why new? The command to love your neighbour was already old. It stands in the law God gave through Moses (Leviticus 19:18). What is new is the measure: "As I have loved you, so you also must love one another" (John 13:34). The disciples had just watched what that looked like, with the water and the towel, and within a day they would see it on the cross. Love is no longer measured by how much I love myself. It is measured by how Jesus has loved me.

Then comes a sentence about who is watching: "By this everyone will know that you are My disciples, if you love one another" (John 13:35). A *disciple* is a follower and learner of Jesus. The proof Jesus names is not a building, a programme or a set of opinions. It is visible love among people who could not have chosen one another. Notice also who the command is for: "one another". It is first about the love believers show to each other. Other passages widen it to everyone (Galatians 6:10, in part 02).

### Love in a quarrelling church

Most people hear 1 Corinthians 13 at weddings. Paul wrote it to a church that was fighting. The Corinthians were proud of spectacular gifts, especially speaking in tongues, and chapters 12 and 14 sit on either side of this one to correct how they used them. Chapter 13 is not a break from the argument. It is the centre of it.

The chapter has three movements. First, without love, everything else is empty:

> If I speak in the tongues of men and of angels, but have not love, I am only a ringing gong or a clanging cymbal.

1 Corinthians 13:1 · BSB

Prophecy, knowledge, faith that moves mountains, even giving away everything: "but have not love, I gain nothing" (1 Corinthians 13:3).

Second, Paul says what love does. Almost every line is a verb, something love does or will not do:

> Love is patient, love is kind. It does not envy, it does not boast, it is not proud. It is not rude, it is not self-seeking, it is not easily angered, it keeps no account of wrongs.

1 Corinthians 13:4–5 · BSB

This site reads these lines as a mirror held up to the Corinthians. Earlier Paul had rebuked them for being proud (1 Corinthians 4:18) and for taking each other to court (1 Corinthians 6:1–8). "Keeps no account of wrongs" is the opposite of keeping a file on your brother to use later.

Third, love outlasts everything: "Love never fails" (1 Corinthians 13:8). Prophecy and knowledge are partial; "Now we see but a dim reflection as in a mirror; then we shall see face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12). The chapter ends: "And now these three remain: faith, hope, and love; but the greatest of these is love" (1 Corinthians 13:13).

[care] "It bears all things, believes all things, hopes all things, endures all things" (1 Corinthians 13:7) does not ask anyone to believe lies or to stay in danger. The verse just before it says love "takes no pleasure in evil, but rejoices in the truth" (1 Corinthians 13:6). Love that rejoices in the truth will not cover up harm. If someone uses this chapter to tell you to put up with abuse quietly, they have cut verse 7 away from verse 6.

[open] Paul says that prophecies "will cease" and tongues "will be restrained" "when the perfect comes" (1 Corinthians 13:8–10). Whether gifts such as tongues and prophecy continue today is a question Baptists differ on, and the Baptist Faith and Message does not settle it. Many on both sides read "the perfect" as the return of Christ, when "we shall see face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12). Those who hold that these gifts ended with the apostles usually argue it from other passages, and a smaller number read "the perfect" as the finished New Testament. This study does not choose. *The Holy Spirit* takes the question up. Both sides agree on the chapter's own point: love lasts longer than any gift.

Consider: What does Jesus give as the measure of the new command? In 1 Corinthians 13:4–7, which line would have been hardest for a proud, quarrelling church to hear?

Reflect: Read 1 Corinthians 13:4–7 again and put the name of someone in your church in place of "love". Which line describes how you treat them, and which does not?

## 02 Carry one another's burdens

**Main readings:** Galatians 6:1–10

Hook: Carry each other's weight, and carry your own. Paul says both within four verses.

### Two weights, two words

Galatians is Paul's sharpest letter. Some teachers were telling the believers in Galatia that faith in Christ was not enough: to belong fully to God's people, they must also keep the law of Moses, beginning with circumcision. Paul answers that people are made right with God by trusting Christ, not by keeping the law. Just before this passage he says they were called to freedom, and that freedom is to be used to serve one another in love (Galatians 5:13). Chapter 6 shows what that service looks like in ordinary life.

It begins with someone who has done wrong. "Brothers, if someone is caught in a trespass, you who are spiritual should restore him with a spirit of gentleness" (Galatians 6:1). A *trespass* is a wrong, a stepping over a line God has drawn. *Brothers* here means fellow believers, men and women alike. Part 04 returns to this verse.

Then:

> Carry one another’s burdens, and in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ.

Galatians 6:2 · BSB

What is "the law of Christ"? Paul does not define it here. Most readers take it to be the command to love, which Paul had just called the whole law summed up (Galatians 5:14) and which Jesus gave as His new command (John 13:34, in part 01). This study reads it that way. You keep Christ's law by lifting someone else's weight.

Three verses later Paul seems to say the opposite: "For each one should carry his own load" (Galatians 6:5). The Berean uses two different English words, and so does Paul's Greek. A burden is a weight too heavy for one person: grief, sickness, a fall into sin, a crushing need. A load is the ordinary pack each person carries: their own work, their own choices, their own answer to God. The church carries the first together. It does not take away the second. Helping someone does not mean doing their repenting for them, or making their decisions.

Between those two verses comes a warning to the helper: "If anyone thinks he is something when he is nothing, he deceives himself" (Galatians 6:3). "Each one should test his own work" (Galatians 6:4). The person who helps is not above the person helped. Help is never a stage for showing how strong you are.

### Doing good when it is slow

Paul then names a practical duty: "the one who receives instruction in the word must share in all good things with his instructor" (Galatians 6:6). Those who teach the Bible in a church are to be supported by those they teach.

Then comes a picture from farming. "Whatever a man sows, he will reap in return" (Galatians 6:7). "The one who sows to please his flesh, from the flesh will reap destruction; but the one who sows to please the Spirit, from the Spirit will reap eternal life" (Galatians 6:8). *Flesh* here means human nature turned in on itself. *The Spirit* is the Holy Spirit, God Himself present with believers. *Eternal life* is life with God that begins now and does not end.

[care] Galatians 6:7 is sometimes taught as a promise that giving money will bring money back. The harvest this verse names is "eternal life", not wealth. The confession this site writes under teaches stewardship, the faithful use of what God has entrusted, and does not promise riches as a reward for faith. *Money, possessions and generosity* takes this up.

The paragraph ends in two sentences:

> Let us not grow weary in well-doing, for in due time we will reap a harvest if we do not give up. Therefore, as we have opportunity, let us do good to everyone, and especially to the family of faith.

Galatians 6:9–10 · BSB

"In due time" means the timing is not ours. "Everyone" means the church's care does not stop at its own door. "Especially to the family of faith" means the church does not neglect its own people either.

Consider: What is the difference between a burden (Galatians 6:2) and a load (Galatians 6:5)? Why does Paul put a warning to the helper in the middle of his call to help?

Reflect: Is there someone in your church carrying a burden too heavy for one person? Ask them what would actually help before you decide for them.

## 03 Confessing and forgiving

**Main readings:** 1 John 1:5–2:2; James 5:16 (quoted)

Hook: Two claims are lies. One sentence tells the truth.

### Walking in the light

To *confess* a sin means to say plainly what you did, agreeing with God about it instead of excusing it. To *forgive* means to release someone from a debt: to give up the claim to make them pay. This part reads both, because John and James put them side by side.

John's letter begins with a single picture: "God is light, and in Him there is no darkness at all" (1 John 1:5). Then come three claims people make about themselves, each beginning "If we say", and each shown to be false:

- "If we say we have fellowship with Him yet walk in the darkness, we lie" (1 John 1:6). *Fellowship* means a shared life. You cannot claim to share God's life while living in what He calls darkness.
- "If we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves" (1 John 1:8).
- "If we say we have not sinned, we make Him out to be a liar" (1 John 1:10).

Between them stand two sentences that tell the truth. The first is about life together:

> But if we walk in the light as He is in the light, we have fellowship with one another, and the blood of Jesus His Son cleanses us from all sin.

1 John 1:7 · BSB

Notice "with one another". You might expect John to say walking in the light gives fellowship with God, and it does. But he names fellowship with each other. People who have stopped hiding from God can stop hiding from each other.

The second is the promise:

> If we confess our sins, He is faithful and just to forgive us our sins and to cleanse us from all unrighteousness.

1 John 1:9 · BSB

Faithful, because God keeps His word. Just, because forgiving is right, not a blind eye. Why is it just? The next verses answer. "If anyone does sin, we have an advocate before the Father—Jesus Christ, the Righteous One" (1 John 2:1). An *advocate* is someone who speaks for you before a judge. "He Himself is the atoning sacrifice for our sins" (1 John 2:2). An *atoning sacrifice* is one that turns away God's just judgement on sin by bearing it. Forgiveness rests on what Christ has done, not on how well we word the confession. *The cross, the empty tomb and the throne: what Jesus' death and rising did* explains this fully.

Look for the habit again. John does say, "I am writing these things to you so that you will not sin" (1 John 2:1). The command is real. But it comes after the light, the blood and the promise.

[care] If you are afraid that what you have done is too much to be forgiven, read 1 John 1:9 once more. It says "all unrighteousness", not "some". If shame is so heavy that you are thinking of harming yourself, tell someone today. In the United States, call or text 988 [check before publishing]. In Spain, call 024 [check before publishing]; in Mexico, Línea de la Vida, 800 911 2000 [check before publishing]; in other Spanish-speaking countries, call your local emergency number. In Brazil, call CVV on 188 [check before publishing]. These feelings do pass, and people do recover.

[open] John says Christ's sacrifice is "not only for ours but also for the sins of the whole world" (1 John 2:2). Baptists differ on how to read "the whole world": some read it as every person; others read it as people of every nation, not Jewish believers alone. The confession says salvation is offered freely to all who trust Christ, and this study does not settle the wider question. *Chosen and kept: can I be sure?* shows both Baptist readings.

### Confessing to one another

John speaks of confessing to God. James adds a second direction:

> Therefore confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed. The prayer of a righteous man has great power to prevail.

James 5:16 · BSB

James's setting is sickness and prayer, and *What Scripture promises the sick* walks his paragraph. Read more there. For life together, the line is enough: believers are to tell one another the truth about their sins and pray for one another.

[other] Other Christians practise this in their own ways, and their teaching is theirs. The Roman Catholic Church teaches that confession of serious sins to a priest is a sacrament, which it calls Penance or Reconciliation. Luther's Small Catechism, a Lutheran teaching, has a section on confession in which the person confesses sins and receives absolution, a declaration of forgiveness, from the pastor as from God Himself [check: wording against the site's copy of the Small Catechism].

[read] Baptists read 1 John 2:1 as meaning that every believer comes to the Father directly, through Christ the advocate, with no other go-between needed. Baptists also read James 5:16 as a real command for believers to confess to each other: to a trusted friend, a pastor, or the person you wronged. Confession to a fellow believer does not create forgiveness. It brings a hidden sin into the light, where others can pray and help.

### Forgiving as you have been forgiven

The same order runs through Paul's commands to forgive. Believers are to forgive one another as God in Christ forgave them (Ephesians 4:32; Colossians 3:13). The ground comes first; *A new creation: what changes when you believe* walks those passages. Read more there. A person who knows they have been forgiven much has a reason, and a power, to forgive others.

[care] Forgiving is not the same as trusting again, and it is not the same as pretending nothing happened. You can release your claim to make someone pay, and still need time before you trust them. You can forgive, and still report a crime. Love "rejoices in the truth" (1 Corinthians 13:6). Forgiveness and accountability are different things, and Scripture asks for both.

Consider: Which three claims does John call false? What reasons does he give that God's forgiveness is both faithful and just?

Reflect: Is there a sin you have confessed to God but kept from everyone else? Is there one trusted person you could tell, so they can pray for you?

## 04 Correcting and restoring

**Main readings:** Matthew 18:15–20; 2 Corinthians 2:5–11; Galatians 6:1 (walked in part 02)

Hook: Every step Jesus gives has one aim: "you have won your brother over."

In the United States: the National Domestic Violence Hotline, 1-800-799-7233 [check before publishing]; for a child at risk, the Childhelp National Child Abuse Hotline, 1-800-422-4453 [check before publishing]. In Spain: 016 for gender violence [check before publishing]. In other Spanish-speaking countries, contact your local emergency number or a women's or family protection service [check: lines for the other Spanish-speaking countries the site serves]. In Brazil: Ligue 180 for violence against women, and Disque 100 for violence against children [check before publishing]. A crime belongs with the authorities, even when it happened in a church. Telling someone is not disloyalty to Christ or to your church.

### Four steps, one aim

The passages in this part are about *church discipline*. The phrase sounds harsh, but it simply means the way a church corrects a member who is doing wrong and refuses to stop, with the aim of bringing them back.

Read Matthew 18:15–20 inside its chapter. Just before it, Jesus tells of a shepherd who leaves ninety-nine sheep to find one that wandered (Matthew 18:12–14). Just after it, Peter asks how often he must forgive, and Jesus answers with a story about a servant forgiven a debt he could never pay (Matthew 18:21–35). Correction sits between a search for the lost and a command to forgive. That frame tells you what it is for.

> If your brother sins against you, go and confront him privately. If he listens to you, you have won your brother over.

Matthew 18:15 · BSB

Privately first. The matter goes no wider than it needs to. "If he listens to you, you have won your brother over" (Matthew 18:15). That is the goal, and it is reached most often at this first step.

"But if he will not listen, take one or two others along, so that ‘every matter may be established by the testimony of two or three witnesses’" (Matthew 18:16). Jesus is quoting the law of Moses (Deuteronomy 19:15). In Israel's courts that rule protected the accused: no one could be condemned on one person's word. The same care is kept here. The others come to make sure the matter is true and fairly heard, not to gang up.

"If he refuses to listen to them, tell it to the church" (Matthew 18:17). The whole congregation now hears. "And if he refuses to listen even to the church, regard him as you would a pagan or a tax collector" (Matthew 18:17). In Jesus' setting, a *pagan* was someone outside God's covenant people (a *covenant* is a binding agreement God makes with His people), and a *tax collector* was a Jew who collected taxes for Rome and was shut out of the community's life. So this last step treats the person as outside the church's fellowship. It does not mean hatred. Jesus Himself ate with tax collectors and called them to follow Him (Matthew 9:9–11). A person put outside is still someone the church longs to win back. Paul says the same: do not regard such a person as an enemy, but warn him as a brother (2 Thessalonians 3:15).

[read] Then Jesus speaks of binding and loosing: "whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven, and whatever you loose on earth will be loosed in heaven" (Matthew 18:18). In Jesus' day, binding and loosing were words for deciding what was forbidden and what was allowed. Baptists read this verse as saying that when a church faithfully follows these steps and acts by Christ's word, its decision agrees with heaven's. It is not a power to decide anything it likes.

[read] Verses 19–20 are often quoted at small prayer meetings: "where two or three gather together in My name, there am I with them" (Matthew 18:20). That is true; Christ is with His people. In this passage, though, "two or three" echoes the "two or three witnesses" of verse 16. This site reads verses 19–20 first as a promise to a church facing a hard decision of discipline: Christ is with them in it. That is a reading of the context, and it does not cancel the wider use.

[care] Matthew 18 is for a brother who "sins against you", between people who can speak as equals. It does not tell anyone who has been abused to go alone and confront the abuser. Where one person has power over another, or where a crime has been committed, private confrontation is not the first step. Getting safe and telling the authorities is. That is this site's application, and it is marked as one.

### How Baptists practise it

Baptist churches take Matthew 18 as instruction for the local congregation. The Baptist Faith and Message says each congregation "operates under the Lordship of Christ through democratic processes". The older Second London Baptist Confession of 1677, chapter 26, speaks of church members as "under the Censures and Government thereof, according to the Rule of Christ". *Censure* is an old word for formal correction. Who belongs to a church in the first place, and how, is explained in *Baptism and the Lord's Supper: the two things Jesus told His church to do*. Read more there.

### When correction has done its work

Second Corinthians shows the other end of discipline. Someone in the church at Corinth had caused deep grief, and the church had acted. Now Paul writes:

> The punishment imposed on him by the majority is sufficient for him. So instead, you ought to forgive and comfort him, so that he will not be overwhelmed by excessive sorrow. Therefore I urge you to reaffirm your love for him.

2 Corinthians 2:6–8 · BSB

Three things stand out. "By the majority": the congregation had acted together. "Sufficient": discipline has an end. Once the person has turned back, punishing longer is not faithfulness. And "forgive and comfort" and "reaffirm your love": the church's task is now active and warm. A person who has repented, turned from their sin back to God, is not to be left forever known by their worst moment.

Paul gives a reason that cuts both ways: "in order that Satan should not outwit us. For we are not unaware of his schemes" (2 Corinthians 2:11). *Satan* is the devil, the enemy of God and His people. This site reads the warning as covering both errors. A church can be outwitted by ignoring sin, and it can be outwitted by refusing to forgive, so that a repentant person is "overwhelmed by excessive sorrow" (2 Corinthians 2:7) and lost.

[open] Who was this man? Paul does not say. Many readers, especially older ones, have taken him to be the man disciplined in 1 Corinthians 5. Many others think he was someone who had opposed or insulted Paul himself, because Paul writes "he has not grieved me but all of you" (2 Corinthians 2:5) and speaks later of "the one who did wrong" (2 Corinthians 7:12) [check: name representative commentators for each view]. This study does not choose; the passage's teaching is the same either way.

### Restore him gently

Go back to Galatians 6:1. Its verb is "restore", and the word was used of setting a broken bone or mending a net [check: confirm this usage of the Greek verb against a lexicon]. Its manner is "a spirit of gentleness". And it adds a warning to whoever does the correcting: "But watch yourself, or you also may be tempted" (Galatians 6:1). No one corrects from above.

[care] Forgiving a person does not mean giving back at once every responsibility they held. A church can forgive and comfort someone, as 2 Corinthians 2 commands, and still decide carefully, with those who were harmed in view, whether that person should teach, lead or work with children again. These passages command forgiveness and love. They do not say that trust must be restored on the same day.

Consider: What goal does Jesus name at the first step in Matthew 18:15? In 2 Corinthians 2:5–11, what does Paul say the church should do once discipline has been "sufficient"?

Reflect: Is there someone you have talked about but never talked to? What would the first, private step look like, and what would it mean to go "with a spirit of gentleness"?

## 05 Meeting together

**Main readings:** Hebrews 10:23–25

Hook: Some had stopped coming. Hebrews does not scold them. It says: think about each other.

### Three "let us"

Hebrews was written to believers under pressure. They had once lost property and been publicly insulted for their faith (Hebrews 10:32–34), and some were tempted to drift back. The long chapters before this passage show Christ as the great priest who has opened the way to God. A *priest* is one who goes to God on behalf of others. Then the letter turns to what follows from that, in three sentences that each begin "Let us". Notice it is "us", not "you". The writer stands inside the group.

The first is the ground:

> Let us hold resolutely to the hope we profess, for He who promised is faithful.

Hebrews 10:23 · BSB

Here is the habit again. Before any instruction about each other, there is the faithfulness of God. Believers hold on because He holds on.

The second:

> And let us consider how to spur one another on to love and good deeds.

Hebrews 10:24 · BSB

"Consider" means to think carefully about. The verse does not say "encourage everyone in the same way". It asks believers to think about a particular person: what would help this one toward love and good deeds? "Spur" is a strong word. Encouragement can push as well as comfort.

The third:

> Let us not neglect meeting together, as some have made a habit, but let us encourage one another, and all the more as you see the Day approaching.

Hebrews 10:25 · BSB

"As some have made a habit": staying away is not new. The reason given for meeting is not attendance for its own sake, but "encourage one another". You cannot encourage people you never see. And there is a horizon: "the Day", the day Christ returns. *The hope to come* walks what Scripture says of it. Read more there.

### Belonging to a particular church

Hebrews speaks of meeting together; it does not use the word membership. Baptists read the New Testament as showing believers belonging to particular churches: churches that could count their members (Acts 2:41), act together "by the majority" (2 Corinthians 2:6), and tell a matter "to the church" (Matthew 18:17). The Second London Baptist Confession, chapter 26, says "all Believers are bound to joyn themselves to particular Churches, when and where they have opportunity so to do". The Baptist Faith and Message says baptism "is prerequisite to the privileges of church membership and to the Lord's Supper". An *ordinance* is a practice Christ commanded His church to keep, and Baptists name two: baptism and the Lord's Supper. *Baptism and the Lord's Supper: the two things Jesus told His church to do* explains them and how a person joins a church. *The church and its calling* asks whether a person can follow Christ without a church. Read more in both.

[care] Some people cannot come: the sick, the very old, those in prison, those caring for someone at home. Hebrews 10:25 speaks of neglect, not of what a person cannot do. Others stay away because a church hurt them. That is a wound, not indifference, and it deserves care rather than a rebuke.

### Does watching online count?

The Baptist Faith and Message does not address this, and the New Testament was written before it was possible. What can be said from the passage is this. Hebrews asks for "meeting together" so that believers can "encourage one another" and "consider" each other (Hebrews 10:24–25). Watching a service can bring teaching to someone who cannot come, and that is a real gift. But for someone who can come, the passage pictures more than hearing a sermon: being known, noticed, spurred on, and doing the same for others. This is this site's application, not a ruling, and churches answer the question in more than one way.

Consider: What does Hebrews 10:23 give as the ground for the commands that follow? What reason does verse 25 give for meeting, beyond simply being there?

Reflect: Think of one person in your church. What would spur them on to love and good deeds this week, and what is the first step you could take toward it?

## 06 One in spirit and purpose

**Main readings:** Philippians 2:1–4; Ephesians 4:2–3, Romans 14:1–15:7 and 1 Corinthians 12:26 (quoted)

Hook: Four "ifs", and every one of them is already true.

### What unity stands on

*Unity* here means believers held together in one shared life, without everyone becoming the same. Paul's letter to the Philippians was written to a church he loved, and it had a quarrel in it: he begs two women, Euodia and Syntyche, "to agree in the Lord" (Philippians 4:2). Chapter 2 opens with the ground for that agreement:

> Therefore if you have any encouragement in Christ, if any comfort from His love, if any fellowship with the Spirit, if any affection and compassion,

Philippians 2:1 · BSB

The "ifs" are not doubts. Paul means: since you have these. Encouragement from Christ, comfort from His love, a shared life in the Spirit. This is the gift, and it comes first.

Then the command: "make my joy complete by being like-minded, having the same love, being united in spirit and purpose" (Philippians 2:2). And how:

> Do nothing out of selfish ambition or empty pride, but in humility consider others more important than yourselves. Each of you should look not only to your own interests, but also to the interests of others.

Philippians 2:3–4 · BSB

*Humility* is not thinking badly of yourself. It is thinking of yourself less, so that others come into view. Look at the small words in verse 4: "not only to your own interests, but also". Paul assumes you have interests, and does not tell you to despise them. He tells you not to stop there.

[care] "Consider others more important than yourselves" does not mean that your safety, your health or your conscience count for nothing. It does not require anyone to give way to someone who is harming them. The verse asks for humility among people who all belong to Christ, and it is followed by the example of Christ, who gave Himself freely (Philippians 2:5–8). No one can be forced into that kind of humility, and it is never a reason to let harm go on.

The next verses turn to Christ Himself, who "humbled Himself" all the way to the cross (Philippians 2:5–8). *Who do you say that I am? Who Jesus really is* walks that passage. Read more there.

### Keeping what you have been given

Paul's letter to the Ephesians says the same thing in other words. Believers are to live

> with all humility and gentleness, with patience, bearing with one another in love, and with diligence to preserve the unity of the Spirit through the bond of peace.

Ephesians 4:2–3 · BSB

"Preserve": unity is not something believers build from nothing. The Spirit has given it. Their part is to keep it, and that takes "diligence". *The church and its calling* walks this passage. Read more there.

That same study walks Paul's picture of the church as a body, in which every member's life touches every other's: "If one part suffers, every part suffers with it; if one part is honored, every part rejoices with it" (1 Corinthians 12:26). Unity means you cannot be untouched by a fellow believer's sorrow or joy.

### Freedom limited by love

Many disagreements in a church are not about plain sin at all. They are about matters of conscience, things the Bible leaves free, where believers who love Christ come to different convictions. In Rome the dispute was over food and special days. Paul's answer begins:

> Accept him whose faith is weak, without passing judgment on his opinions.

Romans 14:1 · BSB

He tells both sides to stop despising and judging one another, and to use their freedom with love for the other person: "So then, let us pursue what leads to peace and to mutual edification" (Romans 14:19). *Edification* means building someone up. Paul says the same about food offered to idols in 1 Corinthians 8. And he ends with the gift under the command, as this study's habit would expect: "Accept one another, then, just as Christ accepted you, in order to bring glory to God" (Romans 15:7). *Faith in daily life* walks this passage. Read more there.

### Telling disagreements apart

Not every disagreement is the same kind, and the passages in this study do not all fit every case. This site's summary, drawn from them:

- **A burden or a weakness** calls for help and gentleness (Galatians 6:1–2).
- **A sin against you** calls for going privately first, with the aim of winning your brother back (Matthew 18:15).
- **A matter of conscience**, something Scripture leaves free, calls for acceptance without contempt and freedom limited by love (Romans 14).
- **Harm or a crime** calls for safety and the authorities first (part 04).
- **The good news itself** — the *gospel*, the message that people are made right with God by trusting Christ — is not open to bargaining for the sake of peace. Paul would not give way on it even for a moment (Galatians 2:5).

Unity is not uniformity, and it is not silence about harm. It is people who have all been received by Christ, choosing to keep the peace He made, and telling the truth inside it.

Consider: What do the four "ifs" of Philippians 2:1 give as the ground for unity? What does the word "preserve" in Ephesians 4:3 say about where unity comes from?

Reflect: Think of a disagreement you are in with another believer. Which kind is it? Could you describe their view fairly, in words they would accept, before you answer it?

## Loved first, then one another

Way: Find the "one another". Look in the same paragraph for the "as" or the "because": what God has already done. Read the gift. Then read the command. Then ask what kind of situation you are in, and which passage speaks to it.

Close: You have read eight passages: a new command at a supper, a chapter on love written to a quarrelling church, burdens and loads, two lies and a promise, four steps aimed at winning a brother, a punishment that was enough, three "let us", and four "ifs" that were already true. In every one, the command stood on a gift. "As I have loved you" (John 13:34). "He who promised is faithful" (Hebrews 10:23). "He is faithful and just to forgive" (1 John 1:9). A church is not first a group of people who are good at loving each other. It is a group of people who have each been loved by Christ, and who are learning, slowly and together, to pass it on.

Invite: If you have not yet trusted Christ, none of this begins with joining a church or trying harder to love. It begins with Him. The Baptist Faith and Message says "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord". To *repent* means to turn from your sin to God, and *grace* is His kindness given to people who have not earned it. Turn from your sin and trust Christ, who is "the atoning sacrifice for our sins" (1 John 2:2). *The good news: how a person is made right with God* explains how. Then find a church, and let them love you.
