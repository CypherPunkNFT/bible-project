# Life together

Receiving one another. Caring for one another. Growing together in Christ.

> Wherefore receive ye one another, as Christ also received us to the glory of God.

Romans 15:7 · KJV

The letters describe Christian life in relationships: members of a body, people who belong to Christ, neighbours who can strengthen or wound one another. Their instructions reach beyond being pleasant. Believers are called to share burdens, use their gifts for others, encourage the discouraged, address wrongdoing and make room for differences without contempt.

Those responsibilities do not all require the same response. Someone discouraged may need comfort; someone doing harm may need correction. A disagreement about conscience calls for careful attention to a different passage from one about persistent disobedience. This study follows those distinctions while asking what holds the community together.

## 01 Love and welcome

**Read:** Romans 12:9–16 and 15:1–7.

### Making room for another person

Romans 12 gives love practical expression. It speaks of affection, honouring others, contributing to needs and offering hospitality. It also tells believers to share another person's joy and sorrow. Welcome involves paying attention to a life beyond one's own interests. — Romans 12:9–16

The paragraph begins by joining sincere love with rejecting evil and holding fast to good. Kindness and moral seriousness belong in the same account of community. Receiving a person does not require pretending that everything a person does is good. — Romans 12:9

In Romans 15, the strong are told to bear with the weaknesses of others and seek their neighbour's good rather than simply please themselves. This continues the discussion of food, days and conscience in chapter 14. The person whose practice differs from mine is not merely an inconvenience to be removed. — Romans 15:1–2

### Christ as the reason for welcome

Paul grounds this instruction in Christ, who did not please himself, and prays for a shared life that glorifies God. The appeal to receive one another follows: Christ's reception of believers is the pattern for their reception of one another. — Romans 15:3–7

Hospitality is one concrete expression of welcome. First Peter calls for it without grumbling. That qualification directs attention to the spirit in which care is offered, as well as the outward act. — 1 Peter 4:9

**Consider:** What costs something in these passages—time, attention, resources, convenience or status? How does Romans 15 connect welcome with the good of the other person?

**Reflect:** Notice someone whose presence is easy to overlook. Consider a specific act of welcome: learning their name, listening without rushing, sharing a meal or helping them participate. These are possible applications, not a checklist supplied by the passage.

## 02 Bear burdens

**Read:** Galatians 6:1–10.

> Bear ye one another’s burdens, and so fulfil the law of Christ.

Galatians 6:2 · KJV

### Help that begins with humility

The paragraph begins with a person overtaken in a fault. Those who restore that person are to act gently and watch themselves, recognising their own susceptibility to temptation. Bearing burdens appears alongside this instruction, followed by a warning against thinking too highly of oneself. — Galatians 6:1–3

The setting matters. Help is not presented as an opportunity for one person to demonstrate superiority. The person helping must also examine their own work. Another person's difficulty does not remove the helper's need for humility and self-examination. — Galatians 6:3–4

### Shared care and personal responsibility

Within the same passage, Paul says both to bear one another's burdens and that each person will bear their own burden. Read together, the instructions make room for mutual help and personal responsibility. They do not offer a simple rule that every difficulty belongs entirely to the individual or entirely to the community. — Galatians 6:2–5

The paragraph widens into sharing, perseverance in doing good and taking opportunities to help. Its horizon includes everyone, with particular attention to the household of faith. Care within the church is not given as a reason to withhold good from everyone outside it. — Galatians 6:6–10

Practical care therefore begins by understanding the need rather than assuming it. Some help requires sharing resources; some requires patient accompaniment; some requires the gentleness described in the opening verse. Those are applications to discern in light of the passage, not categories it defines exhaustively.

**Consider:** Why might the command to examine oneself belong beside the command to help someone else? How can help support another person's responsibility rather than erase it?

**Reflect:** Ask what assistance would actually help before deciding for someone. Consider both what you can share and what you should not promise beyond your capacity.

## 03 Encourage and build up

**Read:** 1 Thessalonians 5:9–15 and Hebrews 10:19–25.

### Encouragement has a foundation

First Thessalonians connects mutual encouragement with salvation through Jesus Christ, who died so that believers might live with him. The instruction to build one another up follows this shared hope. Encouragement can remind another person of what the passage says is true, as well as acknowledge a present difficulty. — 1 Thessalonians 5:9–11

The following instructions recognise different needs. The unruly are to be warned, the fainthearted comforted and the weak supported, with patience toward everyone. The KJV uses “feebleminded” here; this is an older expression for the fainthearted, not a clinical description of intellectual disability. — 1 Thessalonians 5:14

| The need or situation | The response named in the passage |
| --- | --- |
| Unruly conduct | Warning |
| Faintheartedness | Comfort |
| Weakness | Support |
| Every person addressed by these instructions | Patience |

These are summaries of 1 Thessalonians 5:14. The verse is not a method for diagnosing every person or problem.

### Paying attention to one another

Hebrews grounds its appeal in access to God through Jesus and in God's faithfulness. It calls believers to draw near, hold fast and consider one another. — Hebrews 10:19–24

> And let us consider one another to provoke unto love and to good works:

Hebrews 10:24 · KJV

The next verse connects this attention with gathering and encouragement. Meeting together has a purpose in the argument: believers are to strengthen one another in love, good works and perseverance. The passage gives a reason to gather, beyond merely recording that a meeting took place. — Hebrews 10:25

**Consider:** How do the different instructions in Thessalonians challenge a habit of giving everyone the same advice? What would it mean to consider a particular person before offering encouragement?

**Reflect:** Think of someone who may need a specific word of encouragement or practical support. Let listening guide your response; avoid assuming that a brief exchange tells you everything about their situation.

## 04 Correct and restore

**Read:** Galatians 6:1, 2 Thessalonians 3:6–15 and 2 Corinthians 2:5–11.

### Correction with a purpose

Galatians names restoration as the task when someone is overtaken in a fault. Gentleness and self-watchfulness shape the way it is done. The wrong matters, and so does the way the person is approached. — Galatians 6:1

Second Thessalonians addresses a more particular situation: people living disorderly lives, refusing to work and becoming busybodies. Paul's instruction concerns unwillingness to work, not a declaration that anyone unable to work should be denied food. Read the warning in verse 10 with the conduct described in verses 11–12. — 2 Thessalonians 3:6–12

The letter calls for a response to someone who refuses its instruction, including a restriction of association. It immediately qualifies how that person is to be regarded:

> Yet count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother.

2 Thessalonians 3:15 · KJV

The passage holds a serious response and a continuing concern for the person together. It does not make contempt the mark of faithfulness.

### Knowing when to comfort

In 2 Corinthians, Paul says a punishment imposed by the community has been sufficient. He now urges forgiveness, comfort and a reaffirmation of love, lest the person be overwhelmed by sorrow. The community's responsibility changes with the situation; continuing to punish is not automatically the faithful response. — 2 Corinthians 2:5–11

These passages describe different circumstances. They offer important purposes and responsibilities, but they should not be assembled into a single disciplinary procedure that each letter is assumed to prescribe.

**Consider:** What does each passage seek for the person being corrected? What responsibilities does it place on those who correct?

**Reflect:** Where correction is appropriate, distinguish the conduct at issue from assumptions about someone's motives. Restoration must also attend to anyone harmed; forgiveness is not a reason to conceal harm or presume that trust and responsibility are instantly restored.

## 05 Gifts and service

**Read:** 1 Corinthians 12:4–27 and 1 Peter 4:8–11.

### Different members of one body

First Corinthians speaks of different gifts, forms of service and activities, while naming the same Spirit, Lord and God. The gifts are given for shared benefit. Their variety belongs within the argument for mutual dependence. — 1 Corinthians 12:4–11

Paul then uses the body to address two ways people can misunderstand belonging. A foot or ear cannot conclude that it does not belong because it differs from another member. Equally, an eye or head cannot dismiss another member as unnecessary. Difference supplies no reason for either self-exclusion or superiority. — 1 Corinthians 12:12–21

The argument gives special attention to members that seem weaker or receive less honour. They are necessary. God's arrangement of the body is directed toward shared care rather than division. — 1 Corinthians 12:22–25

> And whether one member suffer, all the members suffer with it; or one member be honoured, all the members rejoice with it.

1 Corinthians 12:26 · KJV

### What a gift is for

First Peter describes gifts as something received and entrusted to believers for service to one another. Speaking and serving are to depend on what God supplies, with God's glory through Jesus Christ as their aim. The emphasis falls on stewardship and service rather than personal distinction. — 1 Peter 4:10–11

This suggests a practical starting question: who might benefit from what has been entrusted to me? A formal title or public role is not the only way to ask that question. At the same time, a claim to possess a gift does not by itself establish competence or entitlement to a particular responsibility.

**Consider:** Which part of the body illustration challenges feeling unnecessary? Which challenges treating others as unnecessary? Where does your community tend to give or withhold honour?

**Reflect:** Identify a need and a way you could contribute faithfully. Also notice a contribution you depend on but seldom acknowledge.

## 06 Unity through disagreement

**Read:** Romans 14:1–15:7 and Ephesians 4:1–6.

### Welcome without contempt

Romans 14 addresses disputes about eating and the observance of days. Paul instructs those who eat not to despise those who abstain, and those who abstain not to judge those who eat. God's reception of the other person matters. Each belongs to the Lord and will answer to God. — Romans 14:1–12

This does not make conduct irrelevant. The argument turns attention toward the effect of one's actions on another person. A freedom can be exercised in a way that wounds someone for whom Christ died. Paul therefore calls for restraint that seeks the other's good. — Romans 14:13–23

> Let us therefore follow after the things which make for peace, and things wherewith one may edify another.

Romans 14:19 · KJV

The passage asks those with different convictions to consider more than winning the immediate dispute. Romans 15 continues by directing the strong toward their neighbour's good and Christ's example. — Romans 15:1–7

### What unity requires

Ephesians names humility, gentleness, patience and bearing with one another in love as the conduct appropriate to the calling. The appeal to keep the unity of the Spirit is grounded in one body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism and one God and Father. — Ephesians 4:1–6

These instructions give unity a foundation and a manner of life. They do not identify every disagreement as the food-and-days dispute of Romans 14. Other passages in this study explicitly call for correction. The question is therefore not simply whether people disagree, but what kind of matter is at issue and which instructions address it.

**Consider:** Is a particular disagreement about a practice comparable to those in Romans 14, a preference, a claim about the faith, or harmful conduct? What would you need to understand before deciding how to respond?

**Reflect:** Describe another person's position fairly before answering it. Where the matter genuinely permits different convictions, consider what restraint and welcome might serve their good. Where conduct requires correction, pursue it without turning the person into an enemy.

## A community that cares

Return to the instruction to receive one another as Christ received us. Across these passages, that reception becomes concrete in shared burdens, attentive encouragement, restoration, service and patient restraint.

Choose one relationship and one responsibility named in the study. Begin by listening and understanding the situation, then consider the response the relevant passage calls for. The aim is the good of another person within a life shared in Christ.
