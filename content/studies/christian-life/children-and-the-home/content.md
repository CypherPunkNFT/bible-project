# Children and the home

Concepts: Doorposts · Generations · Favourites · Waiting

Motto: A doorpost. A table. A road home. / Words carried from one generation to the next.

Lead: The Bible says a great deal about children and the people who bring them up. It says it in very different kinds of sentence: commands, songs, stories and short sayings. This study walks the passages it owns and teaches one habit for every line about family: before you hold a verse as a promise, ask what kind of sentence it is.

> These words I am commanding you today are to be upon your hearts. And you shall teach them diligently to your children and speak of them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up.

Deuteronomy 6:6–7 · BSB

Frame: Scripture speaks about families in at least four kinds of sentence. A **command** tells you what to do: "teach them diligently to your children". A **song** praises God and blesses the people who trust him: "Children are indeed a heritage from the LORD". A **story** tells what happened, often without saying whether it was good: "Isaac … loved Esau; but Rebekah loved Jacob". A **saying**, or proverb, tells you how life usually goes: "Train up a child in the way he should go". Each kind asks something different of you. A command is to be obeyed, a song sung and trusted, a story learned from, and a proverb applied with wisdom. Reading a story as a model, or a proverb as a guarantee, has left many parents proud when they should have been thankful, and crushed when they should have been comforted.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. These passages were chosen because each one shows a different side of the home: teaching, telling, gift, favouritism, failure and waiting. The Bible's sayings on children and discipline in Proverbs belong to *The wisdom books*. The instructions to children and fathers in Ephesians and Colossians are walked in *Faith in daily life*, and the commandment to honour father and mother in *The Ten Commandments*. Marriage itself, adoption, single parenting, and caring for ageing parents have no reading of their own here. This study reads Scripture. It is not medical, legal or professional guidance.

## 01 Words on the doorposts

**Main readings:** Deuteronomy 6:4–9

Hook: Faith is taught at the kitchen table and on the walk to school.

Deuteronomy is Moses' last address to Israel before they enter the land God promised them. He is speaking to a whole people about to settle into houses, fields and towns. The passage begins with a call that Jewish people have prayed for thousands of years.

> Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is One. And you shall love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your strength.

Deuteronomy 6:4–5 · BSB

When an English Bible prints LORD in small capitals, it stands for God's own personal name, given to Moses at the burning bush.

**In Jewish practice first.** These verses open the *Shema*, named from its first Hebrew word, "Hear". Observant Jews recite it in the evening and the morning, taking up the words "when you lie down and when you get up" (Deuteronomy 6:7). The command to "write them on the doorposts of your houses" (6:9) is kept with the *mezuzah*, a small handwritten scroll of this passage and Deuteronomy 11:13–21, fixed in a case on the doorpost. The command to "tie them as reminders on your hands and bind them on your foreheads" (6:8) is kept with *tefillin*, small leather boxes holding these passages, worn on the arm and head at weekday morning prayer. For a Jewish family the passage is a daily practice in the home, as well as a teaching to be studied.

**The Christian reading.** Jesus called Deuteronomy 6:5 the first and greatest commandment (Mark 12:29–30). Christians confess the oneness of God in verse 4 as the one God who is eternally Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Read more in *the site's articles on the Trinity* [check: exact article title and link].

Look at the order of the passage. First God, then love for God, then "these words … upon your hearts" (6:6), and only then the children (6:7). The parent is not handed a curriculum to deliver from the outside. The words are to be in the parent's own heart first. Then come four ordinary places: sitting at home, walking along the road, lying down, getting up. These are not religious occasions. They are a day.

> And you shall teach them diligently to your children and speak of them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up.

Deuteronomy 6:7 · BSB

**Its limits.** This is a command, and it is given to a people. Israel's children heard these words in their homes, and they also heard the law read at the festivals and saw the worship of the whole nation. The passage does not make parents a family's only teachers, and it does not promise that a child taught this way will believe. It tells parents what is theirs to do. The Baptist Faith and Message 2000, the confession of faith this site writes under, says the same in its article XVIII (the family): parents are "to teach their children spiritual and moral values" [check: wording against bfm.sbc.net].

Some Christian households hang verses by the door or read the Bible at meals. These are good ways to take the passage seriously, though the passage does not require any particular one of them. That is this site's application.

**Consider:** What has to be true of the parent in verse 6 before anything is said about the children in verse 7? Which four parts of an ordinary day does Moses name?

**Reflect:** Which one of those four ordinary moments in your day could carry a word about God, without turning it into a lecture?

## 02 The child's question and the old story

**Main readings:** Deuteronomy 6:20–25; Psalm 78:1–8

Hook: A child asks why. The answer begins with a story.

### "What is the meaning?"

Moses expects children to ask questions.

> In the future, when your son asks, "What is the meaning of the decrees and statutes and ordinances that the LORD our God has commanded you?"

Deuteronomy 6:20 · BSB

At the Jewish Passover meal, the *seder*, the Haggadah (the book read at the table) tells of four sons who ask about the night in four different ways. The question of the "wise son" is drawn from this verse. Generations of Jewish children have learned the story of the exodus by asking it.

Notice what the parent is told to answer. The parent does not begin with a list of rules or a warning. The answer begins with what God did: "We were slaves of Pharaoh in Egypt, but the LORD brought us out of Egypt with a mighty hand" (6:21). The rescue comes first. The commands follow as the way a rescued people lives (6:24).

Two phrases need care. "That we may always be prosperous and preserved" (6:24) speaks of the life of the whole people in the land under God's covenant, the binding promise God made with Israel. It is not a promise of private wealth to anyone who obeys. Verse 25, "that will be our righteousness", describes a people living rightly in response to God's rescue. It does not describe earning that rescue, which verse 21 says they already had. Christians read the whole law in the light of Christ, in whom God declares sinners righteous through faith, as a gift. Read more in *Saved by grace: how God forgives and changes us*.

### "We will not hide them from their children"

Psalm 78 is a long poem about Israel's history, and its opening verses say why it was written.

> We will not hide them from their children, but will declare to the next generation the praises of the LORD and His might, and the wonders He has performed.

Psalm 78:4 · BSB

Count the generations in verses 5 and 6: "our fathers", "their children", "the coming generation", "children yet to be born", and "their own children". Five links in one chain. The aim is named in verse 7: "that they should put their confidence in God, not forgetting His works, but keeping His commandments."

Then verse 8 says something a family-values speech would leave out. The children are to be "not … like their fathers, a stubborn and rebellious generation". The psalm hands down a faith, and it also hands down a warning about the people who handed it down. Faithful teaching does not mean pretending the older generation got it right.

Scripture remembers this kind of handing on. Paul writes to Timothy:

> I am reminded of your sincere faith, which first dwelt in your grandmother Lois and your mother Eunice, and I am convinced is in you as well.

2 Timothy 1:5 · BSB

Acts 16:1 says Timothy's father was a Greek and does not say he believed. So the faith Paul praises came through a grandmother and a mother. A home does not need every member to believe for faith to be passed on in it.

**Its limits.** The psalm states a purpose. It does not promise a result, and the rest of the psalm shows the chain breaking again and again. Psalm 78 as a whole is read in *The book of Psalms*.

**Consider:** In Deuteronomy 6:21, what comes before any explanation of the commands? In Psalm 78:8, what does the older generation hand down besides praise?

**Reflect:** If a child asked you why you trust God, what is the story you would tell, and does it begin with what God has done or with what you do?

## 03 Unless the LORD builds the house

**Main readings:** Psalms 127–128

Hook: Two songs for pilgrims. One about sleep, one about a table.

The headings of these psalms call them "songs of ascents", among the songs traditionally linked with going up to Jerusalem for the feasts. Psalm 127's heading also names Solomon. They were sung on the road, and they are about home.

> Unless the LORD builds the house, its builders labor in vain; unless the LORD protects the city, its watchmen stand guard in vain. In vain you rise early and stay up late, toiling for bread to eat— for He gives sleep to His beloved.

Psalm 127:1–2 · BSB

In Hebrew, "house" can mean a building or a household, a family line. The psalm does not tell anyone to stop working. Builders still build and watchmen still watch. It says their work rests on God. To an anxious parent lying awake, verse 2 is a gentle word: you are not holding the whole house up yourself.

Then the psalm turns to children: "a heritage from the LORD" and "His reward" (127:3). A heritage is something given, an inheritance, not something earned or produced. The confession this site writes under says the same in its article XVIII (the family), that children "from the moment of conception, are a blessing and heritage from the Lord" [check: wording against bfm.sbc.net]. The picture of arrows in a warrior's hand and of not being put to shame "at the gate" (127:4–5) belongs to a world where the town gate was the place of courts and disputes, and grown sons stood beside their father there.

Psalm 128 widens the picture to a table:

> Your wife will be like a fruitful vine flourishing within your house, your sons like olive shoots sitting around your table.

Psalm 128:3 · BSB

Notice where the blessing begins: "Blessed are all who fear the LORD, who walk in His ways!" (128:1). To "fear the LORD" in the Bible means to hold him in awe and trust him enough to obey him. "Blessed" means living under God's favour. The psalm closes looking beyond one household, to Jerusalem and to "Peace be upon Israel!" (128:5–6).

**Its limits.** These are songs, and the blessings in them are what God's goodness usually looks like. They are not a guarantee that every God-fearing person will have children, or that a large family proves God's favour. Hannah, whom we meet in the next readings, prayed for years for a child (1 Samuel 1). Paul called his own unmarried life a gift (1 Corinthians 7:7). Read more in *Sexuality, singleness and divorce*.

[care] If you long for children and have none, or have lost a child, these psalms may be hard to read. Their blessing does not measure your worth, and they do not say your sorrow is a sign of God's displeasure. Read more in *Grief and dying*.

**Consider:** What is the difference between Psalm 127's builder who works in vain and one who does not? Where does Psalm 128 say the blessing begins?

**Reflect:** What would it change in your evenings to believe that the LORD, and not your worry, builds your house?

## 04 Families Scripture does not tidy up

**Main readings:** Genesis 25:27–28; Genesis 37:3–4

Hook: A favoured son grows up and has a favourite son.

Genesis is full of families, and almost none of them is a model. Here are two sentences, a generation apart.

> When the boys grew up, Esau became a skillful hunter, a man of the field, while Jacob was a quiet man who stayed at home. Because Isaac had a taste for wild game, he loved Esau; but Rebekah loved Jacob.

Genesis 25:27–28 · BSB

Look at the reason Genesis gives for Isaac's love: "a taste for wild game". The narrator says no more, and does not need to. Two chapters later the divided love splits the family, as Rebekah helps Jacob deceive his blind father and Esau plans to kill his brother (Genesis 27).

Years later Jacob, the son his mother favoured, has twelve sons of his own. God has renamed him Israel.

> Now Israel loved Joseph more than his other sons, because Joseph had been born to him in his old age; so he made him a robe of many colors. When Joseph's brothers saw that their father loved him more than any of them, they hated him and could not speak a kind word to him.

Genesis 37:3–4 · BSB

The robe made the favouritism visible every day. The brothers' hatred does not excuse what they went on to do, selling Joseph into slavery (Genesis 37:28), but Genesis lets us see where it grew. It also lets us see that a pattern in one generation can turn up in the next.

**How to read stories like these.** This is the habit of the whole study. A story in Scripture tells what happened. It does not always say whether what happened was good, and silence is not approval. These are the families of God's promise, and Genesis tells their failures plainly. God kept his promise through them, not because of them. At the end of the book Joseph tells his brothers that God meant for good what they meant for evil (Genesis 50:20). Read more in *God: who He is and what He does*.

**Its limits.** These verses do not say that a parent must feel exactly the same about every child, or that every child must be treated the same way. Children differ, as Esau and Jacob did. The story shows what happens when love is given for what a child does for the parent, and when one child is lifted up where the others can see it. That is this study's reading of the story, not a command from Genesis.

**Consider:** What reason does Genesis 25:28 give for Isaac's love? What did the robe do to the brothers, according to 37:4?

**Reflect:** Is there a child, or a person you care for, who could reasonably believe they are loved less? What would they need to see from you?

## 05 A father who would not restrain his sons

**Main readings:** 1 Samuel 2:12–25; 1 Samuel 3:13

Hook: Two boys grow up at the same place of worship. One serves God. Two others rob him.

Eli was the priest at Shiloh, where Israel's place of worship, the "Tent of Meeting", stood before there was a temple. A priest offered sacrifices for the people and served at God's house. Eli's two sons, Hophni and Phinehas, were priests too, grown men in office.

The story begins bluntly: "the sons of Eli were wicked men; they had no regard for the LORD" (2:12). They took the meat people had brought to offer to God, with a three-pronged fork, before the fat was burned for God as the law required, and by force if anyone objected (2:13–16). "They were treating the LORD's offering with contempt" (2:17). Later Eli hears that they "were sleeping with the women who served at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting" (2:22). These were men using a holy office to take what they wanted.

In the middle of this, the story keeps showing us someone else. "Samuel was ministering before the LORD—a boy wearing a linen ephod", the simple linen garment of one who served at the sanctuary (2:18). Every year his mother Hannah brought him "a little robe" (2:19). Read beside Joseph's robe, this one is not a sign of favour over brothers. It is a mother's love for a son she had given to God's service. Samuel "grew up in the presence of the LORD" (2:21), in the same courtyard as Eli's sons.

Eli does speak to his sons:

> "Why are you doing these things?" Eli said to his sons. "I hear about your wicked deeds from all these people. No, my sons; it is not a good report I hear circulating among the LORD's people."

1 Samuel 2:23–24 · BSB

"But they would not listen to their father" (2:25). Then God's judgement is given to the boy Samuel:

> I told him that I would judge his house forever for the iniquity of which he knows, because his sons blasphemed God and he did not restrain them.

1 Samuel 3:13 · BSB

What was Eli's failure? He knew, and he spoke, but he "did not restrain them". Eli was the chief priest and a judge of Israel (1 Samuel 4:18). He had the authority to remove them from serving, and he left them in office while they robbed worshippers and abused women. His failure was a leader's failure as well as a father's.

[open] The end of 2:25, "since the LORD intended to put them to death", is a hard line. It says both that they would not listen and that God had already purposed judgement. Scripture often holds God's rule and human responsibility in one sentence without explaining how they fit. This study does not try to settle it. It notes that the verse does not excuse the sons, whom the story holds fully responsible.

**Its limits.** This story is not a rule that every grown child's sin is the parent's fault. Samuel himself, raised in God's house and faithful all his life, later had sons who "did not walk in his ways" (1 Samuel 8:3), and Scripture does not blame him as it blames Eli. The difference is not that Samuel's sons turned out well. It is that Eli had power to stop the harm and chose not to use it.

[care] If you were harmed by someone with religious authority, and the people who could have stopped it did not, this passage takes your side. God saw what was done at the entrance to the Tent of Meeting. Telling someone you trust is right, and where a crime was committed, it belongs with the police.

**Consider:** What did Eli do, and what did he not do? How does the story keep Samuel in view while it tells of Eli's sons?

**Reflect:** Is there harm going on near you that you have the power to stop and have only talked about?

## 06 Sayings, commands and the long road home

**Main readings:** Proverbs 22:6; Ephesians 6:1–4; Colossians 3:20–21; Luke 15:20 (each quoted here and walked in its own study)

Hook: A saying is not a contract. A father keeps watching the road.

### A proverb, not a guarantee

Here is the verse parents quote most:

> Train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not depart from it.

Proverbs 22:6 · BSB

It is a proverb, a short saying about how life usually goes, and Proverbs shows how its sayings work. Proverbs 26:4 tells you not to answer a fool according to his folly, and 26:5 tells you to answer him. Both are true, in different situations, and wisdom knows which one fits. So Proverbs 22:6 describes the usual fruit of careful training. It is not God's promise that every child so trained will believe.

[open] The Hebrew words behind "in the way he should go" are, word for word, "according to his way". Readers take them in more than one way: the right way a child ought to go; a way suited to this particular child's age and nature; or, a few hold, the child's own chosen way, as a warning [check: named commentators who hold each reading, read directly]. This study does not choose. All three readings leave the verse a saying about how things tend to go. Read more in *The wisdom books*.

### Commands to children, and to fathers

> Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right. "Honor your father and mother" (which is the first commandment with a promise), "that it may go well with you and that you may have a long life on the earth." Fathers, do not provoke your children to wrath; instead, bring them up in the discipline and instruction of the Lord.

Ephesians 6:1–4 · BSB

> Fathers, do not provoke your children, so they will not become discouraged.

Colossians 3:21 · BSB

The children are spoken to directly, as people who can hear and answer. In the same breath the fathers are told not to provoke them, and Colossians gives a reason a parent can feel: "so they will not become discouraged". Authority in these verses is given for the child's good. It is never permission to frighten or crush. The confession this site writes under speaks of leading children "through consistent lifestyle example and loving discipline" (article XVIII, the family) [check: wording against bfm.sbc.net]. Read more in *Faith in daily life*, and on the commandment itself in *The Ten Commandments*.

[care] A study on parents' authority will be read by children whose homes are not safe. God never commands a child to stay in danger. Ephesians says "in the Lord": no parent's word can require what the Lord forbids, and harming a child is forbidden. Getting to safety and telling someone does not disobey these verses. That last sentence is this site's application, carried from *Faith in daily life*, and it is not a quotation from Paul. The help lines are at the top of this page.

### The father who watched the road

Jesus told a story, a parable, about a younger son who demanded his inheritance, wasted it in a far country, and came home with nothing.

> So he got up and went to his father. But while he was still in the distance, his father saw him and was filled with compassion. He ran to his son, embraced him, and kissed him.

Luke 15:20 · BSB

"While he was still in the distance, his father saw him." He was looking. The parable is first about God and the sinner who comes home to him, and that is its main point. Read more in *The good news: how a person is made right with God*. But a parent who waits for a wandering child may notice what the father did and did not do. He did not drag his son back from the far country. He did not stop watching the road. When the son came, he ran. These observations are this site's application, not a parenting rule Jesus gave.

**Its limits.** The parable does not promise that every wandering child comes home, and this study will not promise it either.

**Consider:** What kind of sentence is Proverbs 22:6, and how does Proverbs 26:4–5 show that kind of sentence working? What do the commands to fathers add to the commands to children?

**Reflect:** If a child of yours is far away, in distance or in faith, what would it look like this week to keep watching the road?

## Questions readers bring

### Does "honour your father and mother" mean I must obey a parent who hurts me?

No. To honour means to give real weight and respect, and Scripture asks it of children all their lives. But honour does not mean pretending, and it never means staying where you are being harmed. Ephesians 6:1 says to obey "in the Lord", and Ephesians 6:4 forbids fathers to provoke their children to wrath. A parent who abuses a child is doing what God forbids. Telling a trusted adult, leaving to be safe, and seeking help are not dishonour. Grown children of harmful parents may honour them by speaking truthfully and refusing revenge, while keeping a safe distance. The help lines at the top of this page are for you.

### My grown child has walked away from God. Did I fail?

Scripture does not let us answer that with a formula. Proverbs 22:6 is a proverb, not a contract. Samuel was faithful, and his sons did not walk in his ways (1 Samuel 8:3). Psalm 78:8 assumes children may need to be unlike their parents, and the reverse is true too. Some parents do fail, as Eli did, and where you have wronged a child, it is right to say so to God and to them. But your child's choices are their own, and their heart is not yours to control. Psalm 127:2 says God "gives sleep to His beloved." You can keep praying, keep loving, and keep the door open, like the father who saw his son "while he was still in the distance" (Luke 15:20). Scripture does not promise when or whether a child will return, and this page will not promise it for you.

### We have no children. Do these passages leave us out?

They do not. Psalm 127 calls children a gift. It does not call the childless less blessed, and Psalm 128 places the blessing on "all who fear the LORD" (128:1). Deuteronomy 6 and Psalm 78 speak of "the next generation" (78:4), and every believer has a part in that through the church: teaching, praying for, and befriending other people's children. Timothy's faith came through a grandmother as well as a mother (2 Timothy 1:5). Read more in *Life together: belonging to a church*.

## Learning the sentences of the home

Way: Before you hold a line about family as a promise, ask what kind of sentence it is. A command, such as Deuteronomy 6:7, is yours to obey. A song, such as Psalm 127, is yours to sing and trust. A story, such as Isaac's, Jacob's and Eli's, is yours to learn from, and it does not always tell you what to copy. A saying, such as Proverbs 22:6, is wisdom about how things usually go, to be used with wisdom. Then ask what the passage asks of you, and what it does not promise.

Close: The home in Scripture is not a place of perfect families. It is a place where God's words are spoken at the table and on the road, where the old story is told when a child asks, and where a father keeps watching the road. You do not hold the house up yourself. "Unless the LORD builds the house, its builders labor in vain."

Invite: If you have never trusted Christ yourself, start there before anything else in this study. The love of the father in Luke 15 is the love of God for anyone who comes home to him. Turn from your sin and trust Jesus Christ as Lord. Read *The good news: how a person is made right with God*.
