## Direction

Teach one habit: before you hold a verse as a promise, ask what kind of sentence it is. That is the study's own habit, in its lead, its frame and its close. The shape is already in the passages. Scripture speaks about families in four kinds of sentence: a command, a song, a story and a saying. Each asks something different of the reader: a command is to be obeyed, a song sung and trusted, a story learned from, a saying applied with wisdom. The harm the study names comes from mixing them up, reading a story as a model or a proverb as a guarantee. So the page marks the kind beside every quotation, and lets the reader light up one kind at a time. One direction was built, because the text already existed.

## The page

- **Opening.** The standard heading: the study trail (Study / Living the Christian Life / Children and the home), the four concepts in gold capitals (Doorposts · Generations · Favourites · Waiting), the title "Children" in ink with "and the home" in gold italic, the lead, and Deuteronomy 6:6–7 in full, already marked as a command. Beside it, in a faint circle and dotted orbit: a doorpost with its small case (Deuteronomy 6:9), a road running from it to the horizon (6:7), five young olive saplings along the road, smaller as it goes, for the five generations of Psalm 78:5–6, and an olive beside a low table with shoots around it (Psalm 128:3). No one is drawn. The leaves move slowly. Motto: "A doorpost. A table. A road home. / Words carried from one generation to the next." Under it, four counts, each computed by the build, each with what it means: four kinds of sentence, six "Its limits" notes, five generations in one chain, 31 verses printed in full. The study's care note for a child who is being hurt comes straight after, with the help lines, before anything else, as the study places it.
- **00 What kind of sentence is it?** The study's frame, then the four kinds side by side. Each has its mark (a doorpost, rising steps, a road, scales), its example from the frame, what it asks, and how many quotations of that kind the page holds. Choose a kind to mark every quotation of that kind down the page and list them; the choice is kept in the address. Then the study's note on what it does and does not cover.
- **01–06, one part per reading.** Each part opens with its number, its title, its hook, its main readings and the kinds of sentence it contains. Then comes its figure, then the text. Every block quotation carries its kind in the left margin, as the mark and the word, with what that kind asks. The one quotation that is none of the four, 2 Timothy 1:5, is marked "Letter" and says why. Notes keep their own labels in the same margin: Its limits (one in every part), With care, and An open question. Each part has one figure, made from its own verses:
  - **01 Words on the doorposts.** Where does Moses put the words first, and how far out do they go? Five nested rooms with the heart innermost: your heart, your children, the day, hand and forehead, doorpost and gate. Above them, the two verses everything rests on (6:4–5). Choose a room to read its words. The sentence that reads it: the passage moves outward and never steps back. On a phone the rooms give way to the same five as a numbered list.
  - **02 The child's question and the old story.** When a child asks what the commands mean, where does the answer begin? Three steps from Deuteronomy 6: the child asks, the story comes first, then the commands. Then the five generations of Psalm 78:5–6 as linked rings, the warning of 78:8 as a broken ring, and Timothy's three (grandmother Lois, mother Eunice, Timothy) as a smaller chain, with the Greek father of Acts 16:1 beside it.
  - **03 Unless the LORD builds the house.** How often does Psalm 127 say "in vain" before it turns? Its lines with each "in vain" lit, the count, and the turn: "for He gives sleep to His beloved". Then Psalm 128's blessing from the one who fears the LORD to all Israel.
  - **04 Families Scripture does not tidy up.** Why does each parent love the child they love? Isaac and Esau, with the reason Genesis gives; Rebekah and Jacob, where Genesis gives none and the page says so; then, a generation on, Jacob renamed Israel and his love for Joseph, the robe, and the brothers' hatred. Jacob is marked in both rows, because the son his mother favoured grows up to favour a son of his own.
  - **05 A father who would not restrain his sons.** How does the story keep Samuel in view while it tells of Eli's sons? 1 Samuel 2:12–26 as a strip of four stretches, sons and Samuel in turn; choose one to read it. Under it, what Eli did (he knew, he spoke) and did not do (he did not restrain them). Then two robes: Joseph's and the little robe Hannah made.
  - **06 Sayings, commands and the long road home.** What kind of sentence is each one, and what does it ask? Proverbs 26:4 beside 26:5, a saying beside its opposite. The commands to children beside the commands to fathers. Then the road of Luke 15:20: "while he was still in the distance", and the father's five acts in order. The sentence under it says that what a waiting parent takes from them is this site's application, not a rule Jesus gave.
- **Questions readers bring.** Three questions about readers' own homes, all three answers in view side by side, not hidden behind switches.
- **Close.** The way of reading, which names each kind once more with an example, then the four kinds as a short list with their marks, then the close, the invitation and the question for the reader. Beside them, Deuteronomy 6:4–9 is printed in full as "the passage again". Under them, a thin drawing of the doorpost and the road going on to the horizon. After that come the sources and the link back to Living the Christian Life.
- **Every reference** opens its verses in a sheet, in the Berean, and takes its book section's colour. Escape closes the sheet.

## Colour

History orange, because most of the passages the study walks are in the Law and the History books: Deuteronomy 6, Genesis 25 and 37, 1 Samuel 2–3. The study's other passages keep their own sections' colours on their reference links (Poetry teal for the Psalms and Proverbs, Epistles gold for Ephesians, Colossians and Timothy, Gospels violet for Luke), so the colour of a link also says where in the Bible it sits. The kind of sentence is never shown by colour alone: it is always a mark and a word. The site's gold marks the care notes and the heading's turn. Grief and dying and Marriage, sexuality and singleness also use History orange in this collection, so this page is told apart from them by its shape, not its colour.

## Doctrine and care

The site's voice follows the Baptist Faith and Message 2000. Its article XVIII (the family) is quoted three times, each phrase checked by script against the copy held on this PC, and each still marked for a check against bfm.sbc.net. Children are "a blessing and heritage from the Lord", and parents are to teach their children spiritual and moral values. Deuteronomy 6:4 is read with Christians as the one God who is Father, Son and Holy Spirit, after the Jewish practice of the Shema, mezuzah and tefillin is described first in its own terms. No story is presented as a model to copy, no proverb as a guarantee, and no song as a promise to every household: every part carries an "Its limits" note saying what its passage does not promise. Where readers differ, on what "in the way he should go" means in Proverbs 22:6, the page shows the three readings and does not choose. The study is written for children in unsafe homes as well as for parents. Its care note and help lines stand at the top of the page, before anything else, and again in the first question. God never commands a child to stay in danger, and telling a trusted adult does not dishonour a parent. Every crisis number still carries the study's "check before publishing" mark. The page does not say that a child who wanders proves a parent failed, or that every wandering child comes home. See [passage-notes.md](passage-notes.md) for the rest.
