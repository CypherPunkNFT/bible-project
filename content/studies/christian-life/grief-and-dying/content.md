# Grief and dying

Concepts: Mourning · Lament · Dying · Hope

Motto: Tears first. / Then the song.

Lead: The Bible is full of people standing beside graves. They weep and they bargain over burial plots. They write songs for the fallen and call out a son's name at the city gate. They also tell God plainly that it hurts. This study walks six of those passages and teaches one habit: before you ask what a grief passage explains, ask who is weeping, and to whom.

> Even though I walk through the valley of the shadow of death, I will fear no evil, for You are with me; Your rod and Your staff, they comfort me.

Psalm 23:4 · BSB

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. These six passages were chosen because each one shows a person grieving or dying, and they are read here in that light. Many great passages on death are walked in other studies and only quoted here. Jesus at the tomb of Lazarus belongs to *The signs and works of Jesus*, Paul on those who have died belongs to *The hope to come*, and the Psalms of mourning belong to *The book of Psalms* and *Fear, anxiety and despair*. It is not medical advice, and it is not a substitute for a pastor, a doctor or a grief counsellor.

## 01 Weeping at the graveside

**Main readings:** Genesis 23; Genesis 50:1–14

Hook: The first piece of the promised land Abraham ever owned was a grave.

Line: Scripture's first great mourners weep openly, take their time, and still do the work grief requires.

### Abraham buys a grave

Sarah dies at 127, and the text gives Abraham's grief before anything else: "Abraham went out to mourn and to weep for her" (Genesis 23:2). The next verse is easy to pass over: "Then Abraham got up from beside his dead wife" (Genesis 23:3). He sat beside her, and then he stood up, because there was a burial to arrange.

What follows is a long, polite negotiation at the city gate. Abraham calls himself "a foreigner and an outsider among you" (Genesis 23:4). The Hittites offer him "the finest of our tombs" for nothing (Genesis 23:6). Ephron offers the field as a gift. Abraham insists on paying "four hundred shekels of silver, according to the standard of the merchants" (Genesis 23:16), and the field is "deeded over" in front of witnesses (Genesis 23:17–18).

[read] God had promised this land to Abraham's descendants (Genesis 12:7). Abraham dies owning only a field and a cave, and he buys them to bury his dead. This study reads the purchase as a quiet act of hope: he plants his family's dead in the land God promised and pays for it with silver. The letter to the Hebrews later says the patriarchs died in faith without receiving what was promised (Hebrews 11:13). Read more in *Covenants and promises*.

### Seventy days, then seven

Jacob, also called Israel, dies in Egypt. "Then Joseph fell upon his father’s face, wept over him, and kissed him" (Genesis 50:1). The Egyptians embalm Jacob over forty days and mourn him for seventy (Genesis 50:2–3). Then Joseph keeps the promise his father made him swear: "You must bury me in the tomb that I dug for myself in the land of Canaan" (Genesis 50:5).

A huge procession goes up from Egypt. At the threshing floor of Atad "they lamented and wailed loudly, and Joseph mourned for his father seven days" (Genesis 50:10). The Canaanites name the place after the mourning they saw (Genesis 50:11). Jacob is laid in the same cave Abraham bought (Genesis 50:13).

Jewish mourners still keep seven days of mourning after a burial, called *shiva* (Hebrew for "seven"). The family stays at home and receives visitors who come to comfort them. Jewish tradition has long connected the seven days with Joseph's mourning [check: the rabbinic source that links shiva to Genesis 50:10]. Christians reading the same chapter see that Scripture gives mourning time and ceremony, and that it does not hurry the grieving.

Jesus did not hurry it either. Standing near his friend's tomb, knowing he was about to raise him:

> Jesus wept.

John 11:35 · BSB

Read more in *The signs and works of Jesus*. Paul tells believers to weep with those who weep (Romans 12:15).

[care] These passages describe mourning. They do not set how long it should last, and the Egyptian embalming is reported, not commanded. If your grief is taking longer than people around you expect, nothing in these chapters says you are doing it wrong.

**Consider:** What does Abraham do between weeping for Sarah and burying her? What two lengths of mourning does Genesis 50 record?

**Reflect:** Is there a practical task your grief is asking of you, or a task you need to let someone else do for you?

## 02 Lament: grief made into a song

**Main readings:** 2 Samuel 1:17–27; 2 Samuel 18:24–33

Hook: David wept for the king who hunted him, and for the son who drove him out.

Line: A lament is grief spoken aloud in a form others can learn, and Scripture gives it even for losses that are tangled and unresolved.

### The Song of the Bow

A *lament* is a poem or song of mourning, spoken or sung aloud. When Saul and Jonathan fall in battle, David "took up this lament" and "ordered that the sons of Judah be taught the Song of the Bow" (2 Samuel 1:17–18). He wanted his grief to be learned by a whole people, so that they could grieve together.

The song has a refrain, three times over: "How the mighty have fallen!" (2 Samuel 1:19, 25, 27). It begs that the news be kept from Israel's enemies: "Tell it not in Gath" (2 Samuel 1:20). It curses the mountains where the men died (2 Samuel 1:21).

Saul had spent years trying to kill David. Yet David's song names what was good in him: "O daughters of Israel, weep for Saul, who clothed you in scarlet and luxury" (2 Samuel 1:24). Then it turns to his friend: "I grieve for you, Jonathan, my brother. You were delightful to me; your love to me was extraordinary, surpassing the love of women" (2 Samuel 1:26).

[read] Jonathan had made a covenant with David, a binding promise of friendship sworn before God (1 Samuel 18:3), and had protected him from his own father. This study reads David's line in that setting, as the love of sworn friends who risked their lives for each other. Some modern readers take it another way. The passage itself places it in covenant friendship and in war.

### "Is the young man Absalom all right?"

Absalom, David's son, had led a rebellion and driven his father out of Jerusalem. Now the battle is over. David sits between the gates while a watchman calls down that runners are coming (2 Samuel 18:24–27). Ahimaaz arrives shouting "All is well!" and praises God for the victory (2 Samuel 18:28). David asks one question: "Is the young man Absalom all right?" (2 Samuel 18:29). Ahimaaz avoids it. The second runner answers in a roundabout way that leaves no doubt (2 Samuel 18:32).

> The king was shaken and went up to the gate chamber and wept. And as he walked, he cried out, “O my son Absalom! My son, my son Absalom! If only I had died instead of you, O Absalom, my son, my son!”

2 Samuel 18:33 · BSB

This is grief for someone who had done great wrong, and who had wronged the mourner himself. Scripture does not tidy it up. In the next chapter Joab tells David that his grief is shaming the men who fought for him, and David goes out to them (2 Samuel 19:1–8). Grief has to make room, in time, for the living. But the chapter first lets the father weep.

[care] "If only I had died instead of you" is a cry many parents and partners know. It is grief speaking, and Scripture records it honestly. If you find that wish growing into thoughts of ending your own life, tell someone today, and use the crisis lines at the top of this page. That weight does lift, and you matter to the people still with you.

**Consider:** What does David choose to remember about Saul? What question does he keep asking the runners?

**Reflect:** Is there a loss you have not grieved because the person was difficult, or because the relationship was broken? What would it be like to say it aloud to God?

## 03 Lamentations: grief under God's hand

**Main readings:** Lamentations 1; Lamentations 3

Hook: In the darkest book of the Bible, the most famous line about hope sits in the middle.

Line: Lamentations mourns a city destroyed for its sin, keeps telling God that no one is there to comfort, and still finds words for hope before the darkness lifts.

### How Jewish readers keep this book

The book's Hebrew name is *Eikhah*, "How", its first word: "How lonely lies the city, once so full of people!" (Lamentations 1:1). It mourns Jerusalem after the Babylonians destroyed it and its Temple in 586 BC. Jews read it in synagogue on *Tisha B'Av*, the ninth day of the month of Av. That is a day of fasting and mourning for the destruction of the first Temple and of the second, in AD 70. In many synagogues it is read with the lights lowered and the people sitting low, as mourners do [check: these customs as currently practised]. The book names no author. Jewish tradition, recorded in the Talmud, ascribes it to Jeremiah [check: Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 15a].

The poems are built on the Hebrew alphabet. Chapter 1 has twenty-two verses, each beginning with the next of the twenty-two letters. Chapter 3 gives each letter three verses, sixty-six in all. Many readers suggest the form says that this grief is being spoken from A to Z, held inside an order even when it feels like chaos. That is a reading of the shape, not something the book states.

### "No one to comfort her"

Chapter 1 pictures Jerusalem as a widow weeping at night (Lamentations 1:1–2). One phrase returns five times: there is "no one to comfort" (Lamentations 1:2, 9, 16, 17, 21). The city calls out to anyone passing: "Is this nothing to you, all you who pass by? Look around and see! Is there any sorrow like mine" (Lamentations 1:12).

This is also grief over sin. "The LORD has brought her grief because of her many transgressions" (Lamentations 1:5). "Jerusalem has sinned greatly" (Lamentations 1:8). The city itself says, "The LORD is righteous, for I have rebelled against His command" (Lamentations 1:18). It names God as the one who struck: "He sent fire from on high" (Lamentations 1:13). And it keeps praying through it all: "See, O LORD, how distressed I am!" (Lamentations 1:20).

[care] Lamentations is about a city judged for its sin. It does not teach that a person's death is punishment for their sin, or for yours. Jesus refused that reasoning (John 9:2–3; Luke 13:1–5). Some grief does carry regret: words not said, things done wrong. Lamentations gives such regret words to bring to God, instead of letting it fester alone.

### "Yet I call this to mind"

Chapter 3 begins in the darkest place in the book: "I am the man who has seen affliction under the rod of God’s wrath" (Lamentations 3:1). God has walled him in. "Even when I cry out and plead for help, He shuts out my prayer" (Lamentations 3:8). By verse 18 he says, "My strength has perished, along with my hope from the LORD." Then he turns.

> Yet I call this to mind, and therefore I have hope: Because of the loving devotion of the LORD we are not consumed, for His mercies never fail. They are new every morning; great is Your faithfulness! “The LORD is my portion,” says my soul, “therefore I will hope in Him.”

Lamentations 3:21–24 · BSB

Nothing outside has changed. The city still lies in ruins. What changes is what he chooses to remember. "Loving devotion" translates the Hebrew *hesed*, God's loyal, promise-keeping love for his people. The poem goes on:

> For the Lord will not cast us off forever. Even if He causes grief, He will show compassion according to His abundant loving devotion. For He does not willingly afflict or grieve the sons of men.

Lamentations 3:31–33 · BSB

[read] Look where this hope stands. It comes in the middle of the book, not at the end. A few verses later the poet prays, "We have sinned and rebelled; You have not forgiven" (Lamentations 3:42), and the book closes in chapter 5 with a question still open. Hope in Lamentations is spoken in the dark. It does not wait for the dark to lift.

[read] The chapter also asks, "Do not both adversity and good come from the mouth of the Most High?" (Lamentations 3:38). This is what Christians call *providence*: God's rule over all that happens. Lamentations holds that rule beside "He does not willingly afflict" and does not explain how the two fit. Neither should a reader standing at someone else's graveside.

**Consider:** Which phrase returns five times in chapter 1? What has changed, and what has not, between Lamentations 3:18 and 3:21?

**Reflect:** What is one true thing about God you could "call to mind" today, without pretending the loss is smaller than it is?

## 04 Dying well

**Main readings:** Luke 23:39–43

Hook: A dying criminal with nothing to offer asks to be remembered, and is answered with "today".

Line: Scripture's picture of dying well is not dying impressively. It is turning, at the end, to the one who can save.

### "Jesus, remember me"

Two criminals are crucified beside Jesus. One mocks him: "Save Yourself and us!" (Luke 23:39). The other rebukes him: "Do you not even fear God, since you are under the same judgment?" (Luke 23:40). Then he says something no one else at the cross says: "We are punished justly, for we are receiving what our actions deserve. But this man has done nothing wrong" (Luke 23:41).

> Then he said, “Jesus, remember me when You come into Your kingdom!” And Jesus said to him, “Truly I tell you, today you will be with Me in Paradise.”

Luke 23:42–43 · BSB

This man cannot be baptized, join a church, make amends or do one good deed. He admits his guilt, he believes Jesus is a King with a kingdom beyond death, and he asks. That is what Christians mean by the *gospel*, the good news: forgiveness given by *grace*, God's undeserved kindness, to those who *repent*, which means turning from sin to God, and trust Christ. "Paradise" is an old word for a garden or park. Here Jesus uses it for the place where the forgiven are with him after death (compare 2 Corinthians 12:4). Read more in *The good news: how a person is made right with God*.

[open] A few translations move the comma: "Truly I tell you today, you will be with Me in Paradise". That reading is used, for example, by Jehovah's Witnesses, who teach that the dead are unconscious until the resurrection [check: the exact wording of Luke 23:43 in the New World Translation]. The Berean, like nearly all English versions, reads "today" with the promise. The phrase "Truly I tell you" appears again and again in Luke without "today" attached, which supports the usual reading.

### Getting ready

Scripture shows other ways of preparing to die. Jacob told his son where he wanted to be buried and made him promise (Genesis 50:5). Abraham bought a grave before he needed his own (Genesis 23). The Psalm many people ask to hear at the end speaks of the Lord's company, not of a technique:

> Surely goodness and mercy will follow me all the days of my life, and I will dwell in the house of the LORD forever.

Psalm 23:6 · BSB

Read more in *The book of Psalms*. Christians have written whole books on dying well. They include the fifteenth-century Latin handbooks called *Ars moriendi* ("the art of dying") and Jeremy Taylor's *The Rule and Exercises of Holy Dying* (1651), written by an Anglican bishop. What they share with these passages is plain: make your peace with God, say what needs saying to the people you love, and let others care for you.

Paul, facing possible death in prison, wrote:

> For to me, to live is Christ, and to die is gain. But if I go on living in the body, this will mean fruitful labor for me. So what shall I choose? I do not know. I am torn between the two. I desire to depart and be with Christ, which is far better indeed.

Philippians 1:21–23 · BSB

Read more in *Witness and the nations: telling others about Jesus*.

[care] Paul's "gain" is Christ, not escape. In the next verses he says it is more necessary for his friends that he stay, and he expects to remain with them (Philippians 1:24–25). His longing is no reason to hasten death. The confession this site writes under asks Christians to "contend for the sanctity of all human life from conception to natural death" (article XV, the Christian and the social order). Decisions about treatment belong to you and your doctors, and this page does not make them.

**Consider:** What three things does the second criminal say before he asks Jesus to remember him? What does Jesus promise, and when?

**Reflect:** If you knew you had little time left, who would you need to speak to, and what would you say to God?

## 05 Where the believing dead are now

**Main readings:** Luke 23:43; 2 Corinthians 5:8; Philippians 1:23; 1 Thessalonians 4:13–14

Hook: "Asleep" is the gentle word. "At home with the Lord" is the plain one.

Line: Those who die trusting Christ are with him at once, consciously and far better off, while they wait for the resurrection of their bodies.

### With Christ, now

Christians call the time between a person's death and the final resurrection the *intermediate state*. Scripture says less about it than people wish, but it says enough. Jesus promised the dying man "today... with Me in Paradise" (Luke 23:43). Paul wrote:

> We are confident, then, and would prefer to be away from the body and at home with the Lord.

2 Corinthians 5:8 · BSB

Read more in *What Scripture promises the sick*. And to depart is "to be with Christ, which is far better indeed" (Philippians 1:23).

Paul also calls the believing dead "asleep":

> Brothers, we do not want you to be uninformed about those who sleep in death, so that you will not grieve like the rest, who are without hope. For since we believe that Jesus died and rose again, we also believe that God will bring with Jesus those who have fallen asleep in Him.

1 Thessalonians 4:13–14 · BSB

Read more in *The hope to come*. "Sleep" describes how death looks from this side: the body at rest, waiting to be woken. It does not mean the person is unconscious, for Paul speaks of being "at home with the Lord". And this is not the end of the story. Jesus said:

> I am the resurrection and the life. Whoever believes in Me will live, even though he dies. And everyone who lives and believes in Me will never die. Do you believe this?

John 11:25–26 · BSB

The confession this site writes under looks forward to the day when "the righteous in their resurrected and glorified bodies" will dwell forever in heaven with the Lord (article X, last things). The believing dead are with Christ now, and they will be raised whole.

[source:lbc] Older Baptists said the same. The Second London Baptist Confession (1677), chapter 31, says the souls of the dead "neither die nor sleep", and that the souls of the righteous are "received into paradise where they are with Christ" [check: wording and spelling against the site's copy of chapter 31, paragraph 1].

[other] Others teach differently. Seventh-day Adventists and Jehovah's Witnesses teach that the dead are unconscious until the resurrection [check: the wording of the Seventh-day Adventist Fundamental Beliefs, no. 26, before quoting it]. The Catechism of the Catholic Church teaches that those who die "in God's grace and friendship, but still imperfectly purified" are assured of salvation but "undergo purification" after death, which Catholics call purgatory [check: wording of paragraph 1030]. Baptists answer from "far better" and "today" that the believer goes straight to Christ. They also answer from Hebrews: Christ's one offering has made his people perfect for ever (Hebrews 10:14), and so nothing is left to be purged.

[care] Scripture does not tell us what the believing dead do now, or whether they see us. This page will not say more than it does. It does say where they are, and with whom.

**Consider:** What words do Jesus and Paul use for where the believing dead go? Why does Paul say "asleep" and still say "at home with the Lord"?

**Reflect:** If someone you love died trusting Christ, what changes if you picture them "at home with the Lord", rather than simply gone?

## Question: Where is my baby who died?

I will go to him, but he will not return to me.

[read] This answer is a confident inference, and it is labelled as one: Scripture gives no verse that says it in so many words. When David's infant son died, he stopped fasting and said:

> But now that he is dead, why should I fast? Can I bring him back again? I will go to him, but he will not return to me.

2 Samuel 12:23 · BSB

Read more in *Rulers and their choices*. Many Baptists read "I will go to him" as David's confidence that he would see his child again, though some take it to mean only the grave.

The confession says every person inherits a nature inclined toward sin. It adds that people become transgressors "as soon as they are capable of moral action" (article III, man). A baby who dies, before birth or after, never reached that point.

Baptists give three grounds for hope. The first is God's mercy: he spoke of little children who do not yet know good from evil (Deuteronomy 1:39), and he cared for the many in Nineveh who did not know their right hand from their left (Jonah 4:11). The second is the *age of accountability*. The phrase is not in the Bible, but it names the line article III draws. The third is *election*, God's gracious choosing. The Second London Baptist Confession, chapter 10, says "Elect infants dying in infancy are regenerated and saved by Christ through the Spirit" [check: wording against the site's copy]. Some Baptists add that all who die in infancy are elect.

[source:holders] Charles Spurgeon, the nineteenth-century London Baptist preacher, held this hope in his sermon "Infant Salvation" [check: sermon number and date]. So did R. Albert Mohler and Daniel Akin, presidents of Southern Baptist seminaries, in "The Salvation of the 'Little Ones'" [check: title, date and place of publication].

Your child is held by the One who knew them first. Grieve as long as you need to.

## Question: Is my loved one who took their own life lost?

[care] If you are thinking of ending your own life, this answer is not written as permission. It is written for those left behind. Please call or text 988 in the United States, 024 in Spain, 800 911 2000 in Mexico, or 188 in Brazil [check before publishing: all numbers, and lines for other Spanish-speaking countries]. These feelings pass, and people recover.

If you are grieving someone who died this way, you may be carrying shock, guilt, anger and questions all at once. Scripture has room for every one of them.

[read] For a loved one who trusted Christ, the answer rests on what Christ holds, not on how their life ended. The confession teaches that those God has accepted in Christ "will never fall away from the state of grace" (article V, God's purpose of grace). Jesus said no one will snatch his sheep out of his hand (John 10:28), and Paul names death itself among the things that cannot separate us from God's love in Christ (Romans 8:38–39). A believer's salvation does not depend on their last moment being a good one. Illness, despair and pain can cloud a mind that belongs to Christ. Read more in *Chosen and kept: can I be sure?*

This is not a word that suicide is right or harmless. It is a grief, and it is not God's will for anyone. But it is not the unforgivable sin, and the person was not "selfish". They were in pain.

[source:ccc] If you were taught as a Catholic that suicide damns, your Church no longer teaches that. The Catechism of the Catholic Church says: "We should not despair of the eternal salvation of persons who have taken their own lives" (paragraph 2283). On this, Catholics and Baptists stand on common ground.

If you do not know whether your loved one believed, read the next question.

## Question: Is my loved one who never believed lost?

Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?

[read] This is the hardest question in this study. You deserve an honest answer, not a soft one. Scripture teaches that there is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ (article IV, salvation). This page cannot change that, and it will not tell you your loved one was saved without faith.

But it also cannot tell you what it does not know, and neither can anyone else. You saw your loved one's life. You did not see their last hours, or their last minutes, or what passed between them and God in silence. A dying criminal turned to Jesus with only breaths left (Luke 23:42–43). No one at the foot of that cross could have guessed it an hour before.

Abraham, pleading for a city, asked God:

> Far be it from You to do such a thing—to kill the righteous with the wicked, so that the righteous and the wicked are treated alike. Far be it from You! Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?

Genesis 18:25 · BSB

You can leave your loved one with that Judge. He knows everything you do not, and he will do what is right. He is not careless with anyone, and he takes no pleasure in the death of the wicked (Ezekiel 33:11).

Your grief for them is real and right. Jesus wept at a grave. You do not have to resolve this question before you can mourn. If the question has stirred your own heart about God, a pastor would be glad to talk with you, and so would we: read *The good news: how a person is made right with God*.

## Close: Learning to grieve out loud

Way: Here is the way of reading to carry to the next passage about death. Ask who is weeping, and to whom. Abraham wept beside Sarah and then spoke to the Hittites. Joseph wept over his father and kept his promise. David sang to a nation and cried out at a city gate. Lamentations speaks straight to God, even when he seems silent. Notice the grief first. Then notice where it is aimed. Only then ask what the passage teaches.

Close: Almost no one in these passages grieves silently, and almost no one grieves alone. Scripture does not hurry the mourner, and it does not pretend death is small. It does keep pointing the grief somewhere: to the Lord whose mercies are new every morning, to Jesus who wept, and to the promise "today you will be with Me in Paradise".

Invite: If you have not yet trusted Christ, the dying man's prayer is short enough for anyone: Jesus, remember me. Turn from your sin and trust him as Lord, because "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord" (article IV). Then tell a pastor or a Christian friend.
