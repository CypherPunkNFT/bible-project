# Freedom that serves

Stand fast in liberty. By love serve one another.

> For, brethren, ye have been called unto liberty; only use not liberty for an occasion to the flesh, but by love serve one another.

Galatians 5:13 · KJV

The letters speak of freedom given in Christ and ask how that freedom is used. Paul defends liberty while also calling believers to serve, restrain themselves and consider another person's good. The question is more than whether an action is permitted. It includes what the action expresses, whom it affects and whether it builds up.

Galatians addresses a dispute about circumcision and justification. First Corinthians follows questions about food offered to idols, rights and participation in worship. Romans addresses differences over food and days. Reading these settings carefully helps us understand the responsibilities each passage names before applying them to our own choices.

## 01 Called to freedom

**Read:** Galatians 5:1–12.

### Stand fast

> Stand fast therefore in the liberty wherewith Christ hath made us free, and be not entangled again with the yoke of bondage.

Galatians 5:1 · KJV

The following verses explain the immediate concern. Paul warns the Galatians about receiving circumcision in connection with seeking justification by the law. He describes the obligation to keep the whole law and the incompatibility of that course with reliance on Christ and grace. The warning concerns where they seek their standing before God. — Galatians 5:2–4

Paul then speaks of waiting through the Spirit for the hope of righteousness by faith. In Christ, neither circumcision nor uncircumcision is what avails, but faith working through love. Freedom is connected to Christ, the Spirit, faith and love within the same argument. — Galatians 5:5–6

### What is being defended?

The passage continues with a rebuke of those troubling the community and hindering obedience to the truth. Paul is not merely defending the right to hold a preferred custom. He considers the gospel at stake in what is being required of these readers. — Galatians 5:7–12

This setting prevents us from turning the opening verse into a claim that all obligations are bondage. Paul himself will immediately command love and service. It also prevents treating his warning as a rejection of Jewish people or an assertion that cultural difference itself makes someone unacceptable. The issue named here is justification by the law and the demand being pressed upon the Galatians.

**Consider:** What explains the warning about bondage in verses 2–4? What does Paul place at the centre of life in Christ in verses 5–6?

**Reflect:** Examine something you treat as necessary for acceptance before God. Is your confidence being placed in Christ, or in an achievement or distinction that you expect to establish your standing?

## 02 By love serve one another

**Read:** Galatians 5:13–26.

### Liberty and the flesh

Paul does not leave freedom without a direction. He tells believers not to use it as an opportunity for the flesh, but to serve one another through love. He recalls the command to love one's neighbour and warns that mutual biting and devouring can destroy the community. — Galatians 5:13–15

The instruction turns attention from what I can claim for myself to how I treat another person. Liberty cannot justify cruelty, rivalry or disregard for a neighbour. The freedom Paul defends is to be lived in love.

### Walking in the Spirit

The next paragraph calls believers to walk in the Spirit. It describes the conflict between flesh and Spirit, names works of the flesh, and gives a serious warning concerning those who practise them. The account includes sexual wrongdoing, idolatry, hatred, envy, strife and other forms of destructive conduct. — Galatians 5:16–21

Paul then names the Spirit's fruit: love, joy, peace, patience, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness and self-control. He speaks of those belonging to Christ as having crucified the flesh with its passions and desires, and calls those who live in the Spirit to walk in the Spirit. The section ends by warning against provoking and envying one another. — Galatians 5:22–26

The whole paragraph matters. Freedom from the course rebuked earlier does not erase moral responsibility. The life of the Spirit has a recognisable character, including how people behave toward those beside them.

**Consider:** How does Paul connect the command to love with his warning about the community's conduct? Which qualities of the Spirit's fruit bear directly on disagreement?

**Reflect:** Consider a liberty you have defended strongly. Ask whether the manner of defending it has shown love, patience and self-control, and what serving another person would require now.

## 03 Conscience

**Read:** Romans 14:1–23.

### Before the Lord

Romans describes believers with different convictions about eating and observing days. Paul tells the one who eats not to despise the one who abstains, and the one who abstains not to judge the one who eats. God has received the believer; each is accountable to the Lord. — Romans 14:1–12

The chapter does not say that every conviction is equally accurate. Paul states his own persuasion that nothing is unclean of itself in the discussion of food. Yet he also takes seriously the person who regards something as unclean. The person's action cannot be separated from what they believe they are doing before God. — Romans 14:13–14

### Acting with conviction and care

The argument proceeds toward love, peace and building up. Paul warns against destroying God's work for the sake of food and against using a permitted action in a way that causes someone else to stumble. He also warns the person who eats while doubting, because the action does not proceed from faith. — Romans 14:15–23

Conscience must therefore be treated with care. Pressuring someone to copy a practice while they believe it wrong does not help them grow. Patient explanation and further understanding are different from demanding that they act against their conviction.

Nor does the chapter allow every action to be placed beyond examination by calling it a matter of conscience. Its examples are food and days, and its concern includes conduct toward others. Ask what the actual question is before applying the passage to a new situation.

**Consider:** How does Paul state his own conviction while still taking another person's conviction seriously? What does he ask both sides to stop doing?

**Reflect:** Identify a practice about which you and another believer differ. Can you explain your reasons without pressuring them to act before they understand, or treating them with contempt?

## 04 The weaker brother

**Read:** 1 Corinthians 8:1–13, then 10:1–22; Romans 15:1–7.

### Knowledge and its effect on another

First Corinthians discusses food offered to idols. Paul affirms one God and one Lord Jesus Christ, but observes that not everyone approaches the food with the same understanding. For some, eating remains connected in conscience with the idol. Knowledge about food must be considered alongside its effect on that person. — 1 Corinthians 8:1–8

> But take heed lest by any means this liberty of yours become a stumblingblock to them that are weak.

1 Corinthians 8:9 · KJV

Paul imagines someone being encouraged by another believer's example to eat in an idol's temple, thereby wounding a weak conscience. His language is severe: sinning against the brother for whom Christ died is sinning against Christ. The concern is not merely that someone dislikes a choice. Someone is being drawn into conduct that wounds their conscience. — 1 Corinthians 8:10–13

### A warning that continues

Do not stop the argument at chapter 8. Chapter 10 recalls Israel's wilderness failures and warns confident readers to take heed. It then commands them to flee idolatry. Paul distinguishes the Lord's table from participation in sacrifices to demons; such participation is not made permissible by someone claiming superior knowledge. — 1 Corinthians 10:1–22

Romans gives the strong a positive responsibility: bear with the weak and seek a neighbour's good, following Christ rather than pleasing themselves. The goal is shared praise and receiving one another as Christ received them. The description of weakness should not become an insult or a permanent rank assigned to another person. — Romans 15:1–7

**Consider:** What harm does Paul describe in First Corinthians 8? How does chapter 10 prevent a careless reading of the freedom discussed earlier?

**Reflect:** Consider whether your example could draw someone into an action they understand as unfaithful. Seek their good through patience and, where appropriate, restraint rather than demonstrating your freedom at their expense.

## 05 Choosing restraint

**Read:** 1 Corinthians 9:1–27; 10:23–11:1.

### A right freely forgone

Paul defends the right of those who proclaim the gospel to receive material support. He then explains his own decision not to use that right among the Corinthians, so that he would not hinder the gospel. His choice does not erase the right he has just defended. — 1 Corinthians 9:1–18

> For though I be free from all men, yet have I made myself servant unto all, that I might gain the more.

1 Corinthians 9:19 · KJV

Paul describes adapting to different people for the gospel's sake. He explicitly says that this does not mean being without obligation to God: he remains under the law of Christ. The chapter ends with purposeful discipline rather than carelessness about his own conduct. — 1 Corinthians 9:20–27

His example invites voluntary restraint for another's good. It should not become a device for requiring someone else to surrender every legitimate need or right. Paul speaks about what he chooses to forgo while defending the provision itself.

### Different settings, different responses

Chapter 10 asks what is beneficial and what builds up, then gives concrete distinctions. Food sold in the market may be eaten without investigating its history. At an unbeliever's invitation, the believer may eat what is served. But when someone identifies the food as sacrificed to idols, Paul instructs abstention for the sake of the person who disclosed it and conscience. — 1 Corinthians 10:23–30

This is different from the participation in idol worship forbidden earlier. The food's availability in a market, a meal in someone's home and participation at an idol's table are not treated as one identical situation. Understanding the setting is part of understanding the instruction.

> Whether therefore ye eat, or drink, or whatsoever ye do, do all to the glory of God.

1 Corinthians 10:31 · KJV

Paul concludes by directing attention to others' good and salvation, then calls for imitation of him as he follows Christ. Restraint serves a purpose; it is not simply the multiplication of prohibitions. — 1 Corinthians 10:32–11:1

**Consider:** How does Paul defend a right while declining to use it? What changes between the meal situations in chapter 10?

**Reflect:** Identify a permitted choice you could freely forgo for a particular person's good. Be clear about the reason, without turning your choice into a new universal rule for everyone.

## 06 Doing good

**Read:** Galatians 6:1–10.

### Freedom expressed in responsibility

After the call to walk in the Spirit, Galatians turns to restoring someone caught in wrongdoing, bearing burdens and examining one's own work. Restoration is to be gentle, with awareness of one's own vulnerability to temptation. Concern for others and personal responsibility stand beside one another. — Galatians 6:1–5

This gives service practical substance. Another person's failure is an occasion for careful restoration; another person's burden calls for help. At the same time, the reader is directed to examine their own conduct instead of establishing worth through comparison.

### Continuing when good is costly

Paul speaks of sharing with those who teach, warns that a person reaps what they sow, and contrasts sowing to the flesh with sowing to the Spirit. He then encourages perseverance in doing good. — Galatians 6:6–9

> As we have therefore opportunity, let us do good unto all men, especially unto them who are of the household of faith.

Galatians 6:10 · KJV

The instruction includes particular care for fellow believers without closing concern to everyone else. It directs attention to opportunities that arise and to the good that can be done within them. The sowing language should be kept within this account of life in the Spirit, rather than turned into a promise of financial return or immediate visible success.

The study's opening command to serve through love now has ordinary expressions: restore gently, help carry a burden, share and persevere. Freedom is used for a life of active care.

**Consider:** How does Paul hold mutual help and personal responsibility together? How wide is the instruction to do good?

**Reflect:** Name one opportunity for good that is already before you. Choose a response within your responsibility and means, and consider how to continue faithfully if appreciation or results are slow.

## Seek another's good

The letters defend freedom in Christ and give it a direction in love. Ask what the passage permits, what it forbids and what another person's good may call for. Some occasions require standing firm; others require restraint. In both, the aim is faithfulness to Christ and the building up of people for whom he died.
