If someone is hurting you or threatening you, getting to a safe place is the right thing to do, and it is not a failure of faith. Elijah ran from a woman who had sworn to kill him (1 Kings 19:2–3), and God met him on the road.

Feelings like these are real, and they are heavy. They also pass. People who have felt exactly what you feel now have recovered and gone on to live full lives. This site says plainly, in its own voice: God wants you alive.

Lead: Some of the darkest words ever spoken to God are in the Bible. Moses asks God to kill him. Elijah asks God to take his life. Job curses the day he was born, and one psalm ends in the dark. This study teaches one habit for reading them: when a passage records someone's worst words, keep reading to what God does next.

> When I am afraid, I put my trust in You.

Psalm 56:3 · BSB

Words: Three plain words name what this study is about. *Fear* is alarm at a danger, near or felt. *Anxiety* is worry that runs ahead of you, usually about what might happen. *Despair* is losing hope, the feeling that nothing will get better. Scripture has a name for the prayers people pray from inside these feelings: a *lament*, which is a complaint or cry of grief spoken to God, not about Him. The Bible is full of them.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. These passages were chosen because each one puts fear or despair into words and shows something of God's answer. Many others could stand beside them. Job's first chapters, where his losses come, belong to *What Scripture promises the sick*; God's answer to Job from the storm belongs to *The wisdom books*; Jesus' words on worry belong to *Following Jesus and the Sermon on the Mount*. This study reads Scripture. It is not medical or professional guidance, and it does not replace a doctor, a counsellor or a crisis line.

## 01 "Kill me now": Moses and a load too heavy

**Main readings:** Numbers 11:10–17

Hook: Moses asks to die. God sends him seventy helpers.

Line: The prayer under the prayer is "I cannot carry this by myself", and God answers that one.

Setting: Israel has left slavery in Egypt and is crossing the wilderness. God feeds them each morning with manna, a bread-like food from heaven, but the people are weeping for meat, "family after family" at the doors of their tents (Numbers 11:10). Moses is their leader, and the whole complaint lands on him.

### What Moses says

Moses does not hide what he feels. He tells God he has been made to carry a nation like a nurse carrying a baby, and he cannot feed them. Then he says the hardest thing:

> I cannot carry all these people by myself; it is too burdensome for me. If this is how You are going to treat me, please kill me right now—if I have found favor in Your eyes—and let me not see my own wretchedness.

Numbers 11:14–15 · BSB

Notice where Moses says it. He says it to God, out loud. In the Bible's own pages, the man God calls His servant prays a prayer to die.

### What God does next

God does not answer with a lecture. He answers the need inside the prayer.

> They will help you bear the burden of the people, so that you do not have to bear it by yourself.

Numbers 11:17 · BSB

Moses is to gather seventy of Israel's elders, the older leaders of the tribes, at the Tent of Meeting, the tent where God met with Moses (Numbers 11:16). God will "take some of the Spirit" that is on Moses and put it on them (Numbers 11:17). The Spirit here is God's own Spirit, the Holy Spirit, God present with His people and equipping them. The weight is not removed. It is shared.

[read] The anger in this chapter is aimed at the people's grumbling (Numbers 11:10), and the chapter goes on to deal with it. Toward Moses' prayer, God's first word is help.

Limit: The passage does not promise that the hard situation disappears. Moses still leads a difficult people. It does show that "I cannot do this alone" is something God takes seriously, and that His answer often comes through other people.

[care] If you have thought "I can't do this anymore", say it out loud, as Moses did: to God, and to a person, today. Telling someone is not weakness. In this passage it is the first step toward the help God sent.

**Consider:** What exactly does Moses say he cannot do? How does God's answer match it, word for word?

**Reflect:** What weight are you carrying alone that someone else could help you carry? Who could you tell about it today?

## 02 Under the broom tree: bread, sleep, company and work

**Main readings:** 1 Kings 19:1–18

Hook: The prophet who stood against hundreds runs from one letter.

Line: God answers Elijah's prayer to die with sleep and food first, then a gentle question, then people, then something to do.

Setting: Elijah is a prophet, someone God sends to speak His words. In the chapter before, he has faced the prophets of Baal, the Canaanite god whom King Ahab and Queen Jezebel promoted, and God has answered with fire (1 Kings 18). Now Jezebel sends him a message: by this time tomorrow, you will be dead (1 Kings 19:2).

### From victory to the wilderness

> And Elijah was afraid and ran for his life.

1 Kings 19:3 · BSB

He leaves his servant behind and walks alone a day into the wilderness. Fear has become exhaustion, and exhaustion has become despair:

> He sat down under a broom tree and prayed that he might die. "I have had enough, LORD," he said. "Take my life, for I am no better than my fathers."

1 Kings 19:4 · BSB

Where English Bibles print LORD in capitals, it stands for God's personal name, the name He gave to Israel.

### Sleep and bread

Then Elijah sleeps. The first thing God allows is rest. Next, an angel, a messenger sent from God, touches him and says "Get up and eat" (1 Kings 19:5). By his head is bread baked on hot coals and a jar of water. He eats, drinks and lies down again. Then it happens a second time:

> A second time the angel of the LORD returned and touched him, saying, "Get up and eat, or the journey will be too much for you."

1 Kings 19:7 · BSB

Look at what that sentence assumes. Elijah has asked to die, and God answers by preparing him for a journey. God has a road ahead for him.

### A question, asked twice

Strengthened, Elijah walks to Horeb, the mountain where God gave Israel His law (it is also called Sinai). In a cave, God asks him, "What are you doing here, Elijah?" (1 Kings 19:9). Elijah pours out his answer: he has been zealous, the Israelites have broken God's covenant (the binding agreement God made with His people), they have killed the prophets, "I am the only one left, and they are seeking my life as well" (1 Kings 19:10).

Then comes wind that breaks rocks, an earthquake and fire, and the LORD is not in any of them. "And after the fire came a still, small voice" (1 Kings 19:12). The Hebrew phrase is unusual, and translations render it in more than one way, but every rendering points to something quiet after the noise.

God asks the same question again, and Elijah gives the same answer again, word for word (1 Kings 19:13–14). God lets him say it twice.

### People and work

God's reply is practical. Go back. Anoint two kings, and anoint Elisha "to succeed you as prophet" (1 Kings 19:15–16). Elijah will have a companion; the next verses tell how Elisha left his oxen and followed him (1 Kings 19:19–21). And God corrects the lie despair has told him:

> Nevertheless, I have reserved seven thousand in Israel—all whose knees have not bowed to Baal and whose mouths have not kissed him.

1 Kings 19:18 · BSB

[read] Bread, sleep, company and work. God cares for Elijah's body before He talks with him about anything else. Food, rest, people and a next small task are not unspiritual. In this chapter they are the way God answers a prayer to die. That is this site's application, not a command from the passage.

[care] "I am the only one left" felt true to Elijah. It was not. Despair narrows what you can see and tells you that you are alone. If that is what your mind is telling you now, it is worth checking it with someone you trust.

[source:spurgeon-lectures] Charles Spurgeon, the nineteenth-century Baptist preacher in London, spoke openly to the students he trained about the depression that came on him and on faithful ministers, in the lecture "The Minister's Fainting Fits" in his *Lectures to My Students* [check: which series and lecture number]. Despair is not a sign that someone was never faithful.

Limit: God does not promise bread from an angel to everyone who despairs. The passage shows what God is like toward a worn-out servant: patient, practical and not finished with him.

**Consider:** In what order does God care for Elijah? Which of Elijah's words does God correct, and how?

**Reflect:** Which of these do you most lack right now: sleep, food, company or a next small step? What is one thing you could do about it today?

## 03 Cursing the day: Job, Jeremiah and an angry Jonah

**Main readings:** Job 3; Jeremiah 20:7–18

Hook: Two faithful men wish they had never been born. Scripture keeps every word.

Line: Raw complaint spoken to God is in the Bible, and God does not throw it out.

### Job: the night it was said

Job has lost his children, his wealth and his health in the space of a few days, and his friends have sat with him in silence for seven days (Job 2:13). Then he speaks.

> After this, Job opened his mouth and cursed the day of his birth.

Job 3:1 · BSB

> Why did I not perish at birth; why did I not die as I came from the womb?

Job 3:11 · BSB

> Why is light given to the miserable, and life to the bitter of soul,

Job 3:20 · BSB

> I sigh when food is put before me, and my groans pour out like water. For the thing I feared has overtaken me, and what I dreaded has befallen me. I am not at ease or quiet; I have no rest, for trouble has come.

Job 3:24–26 · BSB

Job's "why" is spoken toward God. It is a question, not a plan.

[care] In the middle of this chapter Job pictures the grave as a quiet place where the weary rest (Job 3:13–19). That is how unbearable pain talks. It is Job's cry from inside his suffering, not the book's verdict on death, and it is not true. Scripture calls death an enemy that Christ will destroy (1 Corinthians 15:26). The book of Job does not end in chapter 3: Job lives, God speaks to him, and the book closes with Job living many more years (Job 42:16–17). If thoughts like Job's are in your mind, they are a reason to reach out today, not a door to walk through.

### What God does next in Job

God's answer does not come quickly. It comes in chapters 38 to 42, when God speaks to Job out of a storm. He does not explain why Job suffered. He shows Job Himself. Read more in *The wisdom books*.

At the end, God tells Job's friends that they have not spoken of Him rightly, as Job had (Job 42:7–8). The friends had defended God with tidy explanations. Job had argued with God, honestly, to His face. God is angry with the friends.

### Jeremiah: fire in the bones

Jeremiah was a prophet in Jerusalem in the years before the city fell. Just before this passage, a priest had him beaten and put in the stocks (Jeremiah 20:1–2). His complaint begins with an accusation that may startle a reader:

> You have deceived me, O LORD, and I was deceived. You have overcome me and prevailed. I am a laughingstock all day long; everyone mocks me.

Jeremiah 20:7 · BSB

He tries to stop speaking for God, and he cannot:

> If I say, "I will not mention Him or speak any more in His name," His message becomes a fire burning in my heart, shut up in my bones, and I become weary of holding it in, and I cannot prevail.

Jeremiah 20:9 · BSB

Then, in a few verses, he swings from trust to praise to the darkest curse:

> But the LORD is with me like a fearsome warrior.

Jeremiah 20:11 · BSB

> Sing to the LORD! Praise the LORD! For He rescues the life of the needy from the hands of evildoers.

Jeremiah 20:13 · BSB

> Cursed be the day I was born! May the day my mother bore me never be blessed.

Jeremiah 20:14 · BSB

> Why did I come out of the womb to see only trouble and sorrow, and to end my days in shame?

Jeremiah 20:18 · BSB

[read] Scripture does not tidy this up. Praise in verse 13 and a curse in verse 14 sit side by side. Feelings in a dark season can lurch like this, and the Bible lets a faithful prophet's do the same. The chapter ends on his question, but his book does not: Jeremiah goes on speaking God's word for many years.

### A different "let me die": Jonah

Not every wish to die in Scripture comes from the same place. Jonah's comes from anger. God has spared Jonah's enemies in the city of Nineveh, and Jonah is furious:

> And now, O LORD, please take my life from me, for it is better for me to die than to live.

Jonah 4:3 · BSB

God answers with questions and a lesson about a plant, and the book ends on God's own question about His care for a city:

> So should I not care about the great city of Nineveh, which has more than 120,000 people who cannot tell their right hand from their left, and many cattle as well?

Jonah 4:11 · BSB

Read more in *Reading the Bible well: where to start and how to keep going*.

Limit: These passages do not say that every dark thought is from God, or that every complaint is right. They do show that God would rather hear the honest cry than the polite silence, and that He keeps speaking to the one who cries.

**Consider:** To whom does Job speak his "why"? What happens between Jeremiah 20:13 and 20:14, and why might Scripture leave it there?

**Reflect:** Is there something you have been afraid to say to God because it sounds too angry or too dark? What would it be like to say it to Him, honestly, today?

## 04 "Why are you downcast, O my soul?": Psalms 42–43 and 88

**Main readings:** Psalms 42–43; Psalm 88

Hook: One psalmist argues with himself. Another prays in the dark and stays there.

Line: Hope can be spoken to your own soul, and a prayer that ends in darkness is still a prayer.

Setting: A psalm is a song or prayer from the book of Psalms, Israel's prayer book. Jewish worshippers prayed these psalms, in the Temple, in the synagogue and at home, long before there were Christians, and they still do. Christians pray them too.

### A thirsty soul talks back

Psalms 42 and 43 share one refrain, and many readers take them as one poem. The superscription, the heading above the psalm, gives it to the sons of Korah, a family of Temple singers. The singer is far from the house of God, and people keep asking him where his God is.

> My tears have been my food both day and night, while men ask me all day long, "Where is your God?"

Psalm 42:3 · BSB

Then he turns and speaks to himself:

> Why are you downcast, O my soul? Why the unease within me? Put your hope in God, for I will yet praise Him for the salvation of His presence.

Psalm 42:5 · BSB

He says it three times (Psalm 42:5, 42:11, 43:5). The sadness does not lift after the first time; it comes back, and so does the refrain. And notice the next line: "O my God, my soul despairs within me. Therefore I remember You" (Psalm 42:6). Despair is the reason he reaches for memory.

> Send out Your light and Your truth; let them lead me.

Psalm 43:3 · BSB

[read] This is a way of praying you can borrow. You do not have to feel hope in order to say it. You can speak it to yourself, as a choice, and say it again tomorrow.

### The psalm that ends in the dark

Psalm 88 is given to Heman the Ezrahite. It begins with a cry and never climbs out:

> O LORD, the God of my salvation, day and night I cry out before You.

Psalm 88:1 · BSB

> For my soul is full of troubles, and my life draws near to Sheol.

Psalm 88:3 · BSB

Sheol is the Hebrew word for the grave, the place of the dead; the Pit and Abaddon (destruction) in this psalm are other names for it. Selah, a word that appears in several psalms, is probably a direction for music or a pause; its exact meaning is uncertain.

> But to You, O LORD, I cry for help; in the morning my prayer comes before You. Why, O LORD, do You reject me? Why do You hide Your face from me?

Psalm 88:13–14 · BSB

> You have removed my beloved and my friend; darkness is my closest companion.

Psalm 88:18 · BSB

That is the last line. No turn, no praise. Yet look again at the first line: he calls God "the God of my salvation", and he keeps praying "day and night" and "in the morning". The man who feels most abandoned is still talking to God.

[care] Heman says he feels God's anger on him (Psalm 88:7, 88:16). Scripture lets him say it. A feeling of being rejected by God is not the same as being rejected by God. For everyone who has trusted Christ, the New Testament says there is no condemnation (Romans 8:1). If you feel far from God, you are in the company of a psalm-writer, and the feeling is not the final word.

[care] Psalm 88 is in the Bible for people who cannot yet see any light. It does not mean the dark will last. If your days feel like this psalm, tell someone today and look at the help at the top of this page.

*What Scripture promises the sick* also turns briefly to Psalm 88; read more there.

Limit: Psalm 42 promises "I will yet praise Him", not a date when the sadness ends. Psalm 88 promises nothing at all, and this page will not make it promise more. What both show is that every feeling can be brought to God.

**Consider:** How many times does the refrain come in Psalms 42–43, and what does that tell you about the singer's sadness? What words in Psalm 88 show the psalmist still trusting?

**Reflect:** What would it sound like for you to speak the refrain of Psalm 42 to your own soul this week, even before you feel it?

## 05 "When I am afraid": Psalms 56 and 46

**Main readings:** Psalm 56; Psalm 46

Hook: Fear and trust in the same sentence.

Line: One psalm faces people who want to harm; the other faces a world coming apart. Both stand on God's presence.

### Afraid of people

The superscription of Psalm 56 sets it when the Philistines had seized David in Gath, an enemy city. The fear is of people who are watching him, twisting his words and waiting to take his life (Psalm 56:5–6).

> When I am afraid, I put my trust in You. In God, whose word I praise— in God I trust. I will not be afraid. What can man do to me?

Psalm 56:3–4 · BSB

Look at the first word: *when*. David does not say "I am never afraid". He says what he does with the fear.

> You have taken account of my wanderings. Put my tears in Your bottle— are they not in Your book?

Psalm 56:8 · BSB

God keeps count of the nights you could not sleep and the tears no one else saw. None of them is lost on Him. The psalm ends looking ahead: "that I may walk before God in the light of life" (Psalm 56:13).

### When the earth gives way

Psalm 46 imagines the worst: mountains falling into the sea, nations raging, kingdoms crumbling.

> God is our refuge and strength, an ever-present help in times of trouble. Therefore we will not fear, though the earth is transformed and the mountains are toppled into the depths of the seas, though their waters roar and foam and the mountains quake in the surge.

Psalm 46:1–3 · BSB

A refuge is a place to shelter. "The LORD of Hosts", in the refrain, means the LORD who commands the armies of heaven:

> "Be still and know that I am God; I will be exalted among the nations, I will be exalted over the earth." The LORD of Hosts is with us; the God of Jacob is our fortress.

Psalm 46:10–11 · BSB

[read] "Be still" comes straight after God makes wars cease and burns the shields (Psalm 46:9). In its place, it sounds like a command to stop: to warring nations to put down their weapons, and to God's frightened people to stop striving and remember who God is. Hearing it as a call to quiet trust is not wrong, provided you hear the noise of war it is spoken into.

[source:luther-hymn] Martin Luther's hymn known in English as "A Mighty Fortress Is Our God" draws on this psalm, and Christians have sung it in fearful times for centuries.

Limit: "We will not fear" is a decision of trust, made in the middle of the storm, not a promise that the feeling of fear will never come. Psalm 56 admits the fear in its first breath.

**Consider:** What does David do *when* he is afraid? What in Psalm 46 makes it possible to say "we will not fear"?

**Reflect:** Name one fear you are carrying. What would it mean to say Psalm 56:3 about that one fear, today?

## 06 "Do not fear, I am with you": God's word through Isaiah

**Main readings:** Isaiah 41:8–13; Isaiah 43:1–7

Hook: Not "if you pass through the waters". When.

Line: God's "do not fear" rests on who He is and what He has done, and it promises His presence in trouble, not a life without it.

Setting: Isaiah was a prophet of Judah. These chapters speak to the people of Israel facing exile, when the people of Judah were carried off to Babylon in the 500s BC, far from home. Jewish readers hear these words as God's promise to Israel, His people, and they are. Christians read them as showing what God is like toward those He calls His own, and many hear in them a promise that reaches believers in Christ too. How Israel and the church relate is a question on which Baptists themselves differ, and this page does not choose. Read more in *Covenants and promises*.

### Held by the hand

> Do not fear, for I am with you; do not be afraid, for I am your God. I will strengthen you; I will surely help you; I will uphold you with My right hand of righteousness.

Isaiah 41:10 · BSB

> For I am the LORD your God, who takes hold of your right hand and tells you: Do not fear, I will help you.

Isaiah 41:13 · BSB

Every "do not fear" here is followed by a reason, and every reason is about God: I am with you, I am your God, I will help you. The command does not rest on how brave you feel.

### Called by name

> Now this is what the LORD says— He who created you, O Jacob, and He who formed you, O Israel: "Do not fear, for I have redeemed you; I have called you by your name; you are Mine! When you pass through the waters, I will be with you; and when you go through the rivers, they will not overwhelm you. When you walk through the fire, you will not be scorched; the flames will not set you ablaze.

Isaiah 43:1–2 · BSB

To *redeem* is to buy back, to set someone free by paying the price. God says He has done this for His people, and calls them by name.

> Because you are precious and honored in My sight, and because I love you, I will give men in exchange for you and nations in place of your life.

Isaiah 43:4 · BSB

[read] The waters and the fire are not cancelled. "When you pass through" takes for granted that you will. The promise is that you will not go through them alone, and that they will not have the last word. That is far more than nothing, and it is not a promise of an easy life.

### Everyday worry

Not all anxiety is despair. Much of it is the ordinary weight of tomorrow: money, food, health, the people we love. Jesus speaks to that kind of worry and points to the Father's care:

> Look at the birds of the air: They do not sow or reap or gather into barns—and yet your heavenly Father feeds them. Are you not much more valuable than they?

Matthew 6:26 · BSB

> Therefore do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will worry about itself. Today has enough trouble of its own.

Matthew 6:34 · BSB

Read more in *Following Jesus and the Sermon on the Mount*.

The letters say the same thing in prayer: "in everything, by prayer and petition, with thanksgiving, present your requests to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:6–7). And: "Cast all your anxiety on Him, because He cares for you" (1 Peter 5:7). Read more in *Faith under pressure*.

[care] Jesus' words on worry are an invitation to trust the Father with daily needs. They are not a scolding for people whose anxiety has become an illness. Anxiety and depression can be medical conditions, and a person who has one has not failed a test of faith. That is this site's application, and it is not medical advice.

**Consider:** What reason follows each "do not fear" in Isaiah 41 and 43? What do the waters and the fire tell you about what the promise does and does not say?

**Reflect:** Which "I will" of God in these passages do you most need to hear today? Write it down where you will see it.

## Questions readers bring

### Is it a sin to feel like this? Does depression mean my faith is weak?

Feeling fear, sadness or despair is not in itself sin. Moses, Elijah, Job, Jeremiah and the psalm-writers all felt them, and God kept them as His servants. Paul, the apostle, writes about a time when "we despaired even of life" (2 Corinthians 1:8). Jesus Himself was "deeply grieved" in the garden the night before He died (Mark 14:34). What Scripture shows again and again is people bringing these feelings to God. Doing that is faith, not its failure.

### Is it a lack of faith to see a doctor or take medicine?

No. Faith under pressure (03) already says this: seeing a doctor or a counsellor does not contradict trusting God. God answered Elijah's despair with food and sleep, ordinary things for the body. Medicine, counselling and the care of a doctor can be ways God helps, in the same way. This site does not give medical advice; a doctor is the right person to ask about treatment. Read more in *Faith under pressure*.

### Does God want me to live?

Yes. God sent bread to Elijah "or the journey will be too much for you" (1 Kings 19:7); He had a road ahead for him. He tells His people "you are precious and honored in My sight, and because I love you" (Isaiah 43:4). The confession this site writes under, the Baptist Faith and Message 2000, calls Christians to "contend for the sanctity of all human life from conception to natural death" (article XV, the Christian and the social order). That includes your life. If you are struggling to believe that today, tell someone today, and use the help at the top of this page.

### What about someone I loved who died by suicide?

That grief is real and heavy, and the questions it raises deserve care. They are taken up gently in *Grief and dying*, not here. If you are in that grief now, you do not have to carry it alone; a pastor, a counsellor or one of the lines at the top of this page can walk with you.

## Keep reading to what God does next

Way: When a passage records someone's darkest words, keep reading. With Moses, God sends helpers. With Elijah, God sends sleep, bread, a question, a friend and a next task. With Job, God speaks at last, and calls Job's honest speech right. With Jeremiah, the curse is not the end of his story. With the psalms, the refrain comes back, and even the darkest psalm is still a prayer. With Isaiah, God says "do not fear" and gives His reason: I am with you.

Close: Paul, writing to the church in Corinth, names his own despair and then what God did:

> We were under a burden far beyond our ability to endure, so that we despaired even of life. Indeed, we felt we were under the sentence of death, in order that we would not trust in ourselves, but in God, who raises the dead.

2 Corinthians 1:8–9 · BSB

"We despaired... but God." That is the shape of almost every passage on this page. The despair is real. It is not the end.

Invite: If you do not yet know God, His help does not begin with getting yourself together. It begins with Jesus, who died and rose again so that anyone who trusts Him can be forgiven and brought to God. That is the gospel, which means good news. Read more in *The good news: how a person is made right with God*.

Help: If you are in danger, go back to the help at the top of this page. Tell someone today. These feelings pass, and people recover.

Question: What is one thing God did next for Elijah, or for Moses, that you need right now, and who could you ask to help you with it today?
