# Passage notes — The book of Psalms

Study 48 · Living the Christian Life · 2026-11-06 · First content draft. Written as step 11 of the
[next-20 sequence](../../../../../Pages/Topics/NEXT_20_DRAFTS.md). The folder keeps its planned name,
`praying-the-psalms`, as the brief instructs; the title and scope were reshaped on 2026-11-05 after the
[redundancy review](../../../../../Pages/Topics/REDUNDANCY_REVIEW.md) from "Praying the Psalms" into a study of the
book of Psalms itself. Prayer as a subject belongs to Study 04 (being reshaped into prayer across Scripture) and the
Letters study's prayer topics, so this draft is deliberately not a prayer study.

**Scripture source.** Every Bible quotation in [content.md](content.md) is from the Berean Standard Bible, copied from
the site's own text in [data/text/bsb/](../../../../data/text/bsb/) (psalm headings are held there under each
chapter's `t` key) and checked by script: 122 quoted pieces (a quotation broken by "..." counts as several) were
matched word for word, ignoring case, punctuation and quote marks. 96 match Bible text or psalm headings; 5 match the
Berean's own footnotes and are labelled as notes in the draft ("probably a musical or liturgical term", "another name
for Achish", the Psalm 119 acrostic note, the Psalm 145:13 note, "save, we pray"); 9 are single words ("Hallelujah",
"Blessed", "Hallel", "hymn", "Abimelech", "for") checked by hand; the other 12 are not Bible text and are not expected
to match: five from Spurgeon, two from Edersheim, one from Watts, two from Calvin, one phrase from the Baptist Faith
and Message, and one bracketed slip ("song[s] of ascents") that was then reworded to the heading's own "A song of
ascents". The check proves the words are the Berean's; it does not prove that each reference is the right verse
(checked by hand when each was copied) or that the reading of it is right.

**Counts, and how they were made.** All counted by script from the site's Berean text on 2026-11-06.
- *Headings:* 116 of 150 psalms have one; 34 have none (1, 2, 10, 33, 43, 71, 91, 93–97, 99, 104–107, 111–119,
  135–137, 146–150). A heading "names" a writer if the name occurs in it: David 73, Asaph 12 (50, 73–83), sons of
  Korah 11 (42, 44–49, 84, 85, 87, 88), Solomon 2 (72, 127), Moses 1 (90), Heman 1 (88, also a Korah psalm), Ethan 1
  (89); "song of ascents" 15 (120–134); "For the choirmaster" 55; Jeduthun 3 (39, 62, 77, not named in the draft).
  These match the counts usually given for the Hebrew text.
- *Headings tied to an event in David's life:* 13 (3, 7, 18, 34, 51, 52, 54, 56, 57, 59, 60, 63, 142). Psalm 102's
  heading names a situation, not a person.
- *New Testament citations:* the Berean's footnotes that say a verse is "cited in" a New Testament passage, each
  New Testament passage counted once per psalm, "see also" references excluded. 40 psalms are cited, in 82
  psalm-and-passage pairs. Psalm 118: 13; Psalm 110: 8 (110:1 in Matthew 22:44, Mark 12:36, Luke 20:42–43, Acts
  2:34–35, Hebrews 1:13; 110:4 in Hebrews 5:6, 7:17, 7:21); Psalms 2, 22, 69 and 95: 5 each; Psalm 8: 3; Psalm 16: 2
  (Acts 2:25–28; 13:35). Allusions the Berean does not footnote (for example Mark 1:11 and Psalm 2:7) are not
  counted, so this is a count of the Berean's marked quotations, not of every use.
- *Acrostics:* the psalms whose Berean notes say "acrostic": 9 (with 10), 25, 34, 37, 111, 112, 119, 145.

**Scope.** The book itself: the five books and their doxologies (01); Psalms 1 and 2 as the gate (02); the headings
and named writers, with what headings can and cannot tell us (03); the kinds (04); parallelism and the acrostics (05);
the psalms the New Testament quotes most and how it reads them of Christ (06); the cursing psalms (07); a closing that
links the Hymns study. The way of reading taught is three questions: where am I in the book; what kind of psalm is
this; how does the rest of Scripture read it.

**Boundaries kept.** Study 04 and the Letters study own prayer. Study 45, What Scripture promises the sick, owns the
psalms of sickness (6, 38, 41, 102, 88): part 04 links there. Study 41, Christ in the Old Testament, owns Christ as
king and priest through Psalm 110 and 2 Samuel 7: part 06 names the psalm and links, without repeating Hebrews 7.
Study 37, Long Memory, walks Peter's Pentecost sermon (Acts 2) in full: part 06 quotes only Acts 2:29–31. The Hymns
study ([/study/hymns](https://bibleguide.io/study/hymns), record [README.md](../../../../../Pages/Study/hymns/README.md))
owns the psalms as sung by Watts and others; its section "Through the Psalms" is the natural link from the closing.

## Context, readings and limits

1. **The doxologies (01).** That each of Books I–IV closes with a blessing is plain in the text. The Berean marks the
   books with headings ("BOOK I", "Psalms 1—41" and so on). Whether the doxology at 106:48 was part of Psalm 106 or
   added to close Book IV is debated; 1 Chronicles 16:35–36 has it with Psalm 106:47, which shows it was sung with the
   psalm by the time of the Chronicler. The draft says only what the two texts show.
2. **The shape of the five books (01).** The reading that Books I–III trace the Davidic kingship to its fall (Psalm 89)
   and Books IV–V answer "The LORD reigns" is associated above all with Gerald H. Wilson, *The Editing of the Hebrew
   Psalter* (Society of Biblical Literature Dissertation Series 76; Chico, California: Scholars Press, 1985).
   **Not read for this draft**: the attribution is from general knowledge and must be checked before the name is put
   on the page. The draft therefore names no scholar and labels the reading as "held by some and doubted by others".
3. **Duplicate psalms (01).** Psalm 14 and 53 differ in small words ("acts"/"ways" in verse 1) and in the name of God:
   "The LORD looks down" (14:2) and "God looks down" (53:2). Scholars connect this with Psalms 42–83, where "God" is
   used far more often than "the LORD" (often called the "Elohistic Psalter"). Left out of the reader's text for
   length; a candidate for the design pass.
4. **Psalms 1 and 2 as the gate (02).** That they are untitled, and that "Blessed" frames them (1:1; 2:12), is in the
   text. Reading them as an introduction to the whole book is widely held; Spurgeon's comment (on 119:1, not on Psalm
   1) is the named source used. Some ancient witnesses joined Psalms 1 and 2 as one psalm; a Western text of Acts 13:33
   reads "the first Psalm". **Not checked against an apparatus for this draft**, so not stated on the page.
5. **"Of David" (03).** The Hebrew preposition *le* can mean "to, for, belonging to, about" as well as authorship. The
   draft states the range without choosing for any psalm. Jesus and the apostles treat Davidic headings as authorship
   in the cases they argue from (Psalm 110 in Matthew 22:43–45; Psalm 16 in Acts 2:25–31).
6. **Are the headings inspired? (03).** Interpreters who hold to inerrancy differ: some treat the headings as original
   to the psalms; others as very early editorial notes, faithfully transmitted. The Baptist Faith and Message does not
   address it. **Named holders on each side are still needed** before publication (see open question 3); the draft
   describes both without naming anyone and does not choose.
7. **Abimelech and Achish (03).** The Berean's own note (Abimelech "another name for Achish; see 1 Samuel 21–29 and 1
   Kings 2:39") is the explanation the draft gives. Another common explanation, that Abimelech was a royal title among
   the Philistines (compare Genesis 20 and 26), is not given; it could be added with a named source.
8. **The kinds (04).** Sorting psalms by shared form goes back to the German scholar Hermann Gunkel in the early
   twentieth century; the draft says "worked out by scholars in the last century" and names no one because no work by
   Gunkel was read. That laments are the largest group is the usual count, not checked here by script; the draft says
   "the largest group" and could soften to "a large group".
9. **The Hallel (04).** The Hallel at Passover rests on later Jewish sources (the Mishnah, tractate Pesachim 10), here
   through Edersheim. Edersheim himself gives the "great Hallel" two ranges (Psalms 120–136 in chapter 11; Psalms
   120–137 in chapter 12); others restrict the name to Psalm 136. The draft says the sources differ. The link between
   Matthew 26:30 and the Hallel is called "likely, not certain".
10. **Reading psalms of Christ (06).** The draft follows the New Testament's own reasons: David "was a prophet" (Acts
    2:30), the royal promise to David's son (2 Samuel 7; Psalm 89), and Jesus praying Psalms 22 and 31. Psalm 69:5 is
    the limit case: a psalm quoted of Christ can include lines that are the singer's own. This is the site's reading;
    it agrees with the rule in the [study standard](../../../../../Pages/Study/STUDY-STANDARD.md) that a type is
    claimed only where the New Testament draws it.
11. **Psalm 22:16 (06).** "They have pierced my hands and feet" is the Berean's text, with its note that "most MT"
    read "like a lion at my hands and feet". The draft does not quote the verse, to keep part 06 to what the New
    Testament itself cites; if the design pass adds it, the note must go with it.
12. **The cursing psalms (07).** Three readings with named holders: prophecy (Spurgeon, and Acts 1:20's own use);
    turned against spiritual enemies (Watts, 1719); honest prayer left with God (argued from Psalm 109:4, Romans 12:19
    and Revelation 6:10; a named modern holder should be added, see open question 3). The draft's weighting ("the first
    follows the New Testament's own use; the third follows the psalms' own words") is the site's own; it does not
    choose one reading to the exclusion of the others. Spurgeon also says Psalm 137:9 was "literally fulfilled" in
    Babylon's fall; that historical claim is **not** repeated, because its source is not given there.
13. **Psalm 137:9 and Isaiah 13:16 / Jeremiah 51.** The prophets announce Babylon's judgment in similar terms; a
    sentence linking them would support the "prophecy" reading. Left out for length.

## Doctrine

- **Article I (the Scriptures):** the psalms, their headings included, are treated as God's word; the arrangement of the
  five books, the date of the headings and the meaning of musical terms are presented as reconstructions, never as
  corrections of the text. The cursing psalms are kept as Scripture, not explained away.
- **Article II (God the Son):** part 06 reads the royal psalms and Psalms 16, 22 and 118 of Christ where the New
  Testament does, and marks the limit (69:5) where it does not.
- **Where Baptists differ:** whether the headings are original to the psalmists (part 03), shown without choosing.
  How to read the cursing psalms is a question of interpretation, not of confession: the draft gives the readings with
  holders and the textual reason for each (shared rule 9).
- **Other traditions:** no tradition's distinctive teaching is involved. The Hallel is described from Jewish practice
  through Edersheim and labelled as later Jewish sources.

   Hallel paragraph; move the duplicate psalms (01) and the Psalm 145 note (05) to the notes; trim part 07's "How
   Christians have read them" to two readings.
2. **Category.** A study of one book sits naturally in *Explore the Bible*; the folder and the brief keep it in Living

3. **Named holders still needed** before publishing: the shape-of-the-Psalter reading (note 2, after reading Wilson);
   each side of whether the headings are original (note 6); a modern holder of the "honest prayer left with God"
   reading of the cursing psalms (note 12).
