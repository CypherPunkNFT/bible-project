## Direction

Teach one habit: before you read a psalm, find where it stands in the book. The study's three questions (where am I in the book, what kind of psalm is this, how does the rest of Scripture read it) become three ways of looking at one picture of the whole book, and every part of the study points back into it. One direction was built, because the text already existed.

The shape comes from the book itself (stage 2 of the study prompt, "Scripture's own structure"): the Psalter has a gate (Psalms 1 and 2, with no heading), five books, four closing blessings (41:13, 72:19, 89:52, 106:48) and an ending of pure praise (146–150). None of that is imposed; it is printed in the Berean.

## The page

- **Opening.** The standard heading: study trail (Study / Living the Christian Life / The book of Psalms), the concepts in gold capitals (The five books · The kinds · The reading of Christ), the title *The book of* in ink with *Psalms* in gold italic, the lead (the paragraph on 150 poems with a gate, five books and an ending of praise). Beside it, in a faint circle and dotted orbit, a line drawing of an open gateway with a tree planted by streams of water beyond it (Psalm 1:3), and the motto "A gate. Five books. / A last word of praise." Under the lead, Matthew 26:30 and Luke 24:44, the night Jesus sang and the day He named the Psalms. No figure of God or of Christ is drawn.
- **Figures.** Four counts computed from the Berean, each with what it means: 150 psalms (the most chapters of any book), 5 books (four close with a blessing, the fifth with Hallelujah), 116 headings (34 psalms have none), 40 psalms quoted in the New Testament (by the Berean's own notes).
- **Three questions to carry.** The study's frame and its three questions, set as three numbered lines joined by a fine rule. Each question opens the matching view of the book below.
- **The whole book.** All 150 psalms in five rows, one row per book, each psalm a stroke whose height is its number of verses (Psalm 117's two verses, Psalm 119's 176). Each row ends with its closing blessing in its own words; the fifth ends with the five Hallelujah psalms. Four views, chosen by large chips with counts: *Five books* (the gate, the books, the closings), *Who the headings name* (David, Asaph, the sons of Korah, Solomon, Moses, Heman, Ethan, no heading), *What the headings and notes mark* (songs of ascents, the events in David's life, the alphabet psalms), and *Quoted in the New Testament* (each stroke as dark as the number of passages that quote it). Point at a psalm and its heading, book and citations show on the line beneath; choose it and its sheet opens. The chosen view and psalm live in the address. On a phone the rows wrap and the strokes stay their true size.
- **01–07 The parts.** Each part opens with its number, title, hook and main readings, and a thin strip of the 150 psalms with the ones this part cites lit, so the reader always sees where in the book the part is reading. Then the text, with the passages printed in full and "What it means" kept apart from "Where the reading stops" as the text already keeps them, and Consider and Reflect side by side at the end. One figure per part, only where the text has the shape:
  - 01 The five closings side by side, the four blessings and Psalm 150:6, each under its book (the part's own Consider asks for exactly this).
  - 02 The gate: "Blessed" at the start of Psalm 1 and the end of Psalm 2, joined by one rule, and the walk, set foot, sit of 1:1 drawn as three steps.
  - 03 The headings: a bar of the writers the headings name, sized by count, and the thirteen headings tied to a day in David's life, as words joined by lines.
  - 04 Psalm 13's shape: complaint, request, trust and praise across its six verses.
  - 05 Psalm 119's alphabet: twenty-two stanzas of eight verses as columns of dots, each under its Hebrew letter as the Berean names it; the stanza for *nun* (119:105) lit.
  - 06 The psalms the New Testament quotes most, as ranked bars from the Berean's notes, each opening its psalm.
  - 07 No drawing. The cursing psalms are given words only: the four "look closely" points, then the three readings side by side with who holds each.
- **Close.** "Read the Psalms again": the closing paragraph, Calvin's line, the question for the reader and "Read the passage again before you answer.", then the gateway drawing again, links to the studies the text names that exist as pages (Hymns, Long Memory, How Hebrew works, The Dead Sea Scrolls, Prayer in the letters as a mock-up), the sources and a back link to Living the Christian Life.
- **Every reference** opens its verses in a sheet, in the Berean. A whole psalm opens its heading, its first verse and the New Testament passages that quote it, with a link to read it in the Bible reader.

## Colour

Poetry & Wisdom teal, the site's fixed colour for the Psalms. The site's gold marks the four closing blessings and the heading's turn. Shape and words always carry the meaning too: each view's chips name their groups, and the strokes of a group share a mark as well as a colour.

## Doctrine and care

The site's voice follows the Baptist Faith and Message 2000. The psalms, headings included, are God's word, "truth, without any mixture of error" (article I); the arrangement of the five books, the date of the headings and the meaning of the musical terms are shown as readings, never as corrections. Whether the headings are original to the psalmists is shown both ways, without choosing, because Baptists differ. A psalm is read of Christ where the New Testament reads it so (article II), and the page keeps the text's own limit (Psalm 69:5). The cursing psalms are kept as Scripture and given the three readings with who holds each; nothing on the page lets a reader curse a person. The page draws no figure of God or of Christ and no musical score. See [passage-notes.md](passage-notes.md) for the rest.
