# The book of Psalms

**Study 48 · Living the Christian Life · First content draft · 2026-11-06**

On the night He was betrayed, Jesus sang with His friends: "And when they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives" (Matthew 26:30 · BSB). The next afternoon He cried out from the cross in the words of a psalm. After He rose, He named the Psalms beside the Law and the Prophets as Scripture written about Him:

> Jesus said to them, "These are the words I spoke to you while I was still with you: Everything must be fulfilled that is written about Me in the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms."

Luke 24:44 · BSB

The Psalms have more chapters than any other book of the Bible: 150 poems, written over centuries by several named writers and many unnamed ones. They have no story line, but they are not a loose pile. They have a gate, five books, an ending of pure praise, and headings, kinds and patterns you can learn to see.

This study is about the book itself. (Prayer as a subject has its own studies, beginning with Study 04, Prayer in the letters; the Hymns study shows how the church has sung the psalms.) Carry three questions into every psalm you read:

1. **Where am I in the book?** Which of the five books, and what stands before and after?
2. **What kind of psalm is this?** A cry for help is not read like a hymn of praise.
3. **How does the rest of Scripture read it?** The way the apostles read the psalms teaches us how to read the rest.

## 01 Five books and their blessings

**Main readings:** Psalms 41:13; 72:18–20; 89:52; 106:48; 150:1–6; 1 Chronicles 16:36.

Four times the book stops to say "Blessed be the LORD". The fifth time it says nothing but "Hallelujah".

### What the text shows

The Berean, like most Bibles, marks five books inside the Psalms: Book I (Psalms 1–41), Book II (42–72), Book III (73–89), Book IV (90–106) and Book V (107–150). Each of the first four ends with a blessing of God, a doxology, that stands apart from the psalm before it:

- "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting. Amen and Amen." (41:13 · BSB)
- "And blessed be His glorious name forever; may all the earth be filled with His glory. Amen and amen." (72:19 · BSB)
- "Blessed be the LORD forever! Amen and amen." (89:52 · BSB)
- "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting. Let all the people say, 'Amen!' Hallelujah!" (106:48 · BSB)

Book V has no single closing line. Its last five psalms each begin and end with "Hallelujah" ("Praise the LORD"), and the last is nothing else:

> Let everything that has breath praise the LORD! Hallelujah!

Psalm 150:6 · BSB

Book II adds a second note: "Thus conclude the prayers of David son of Jesse" (72:20 · BSB). Yet David is named in later headings too (Psalms 86, 101, 103 and others). The note seems to close an earlier collection, kept when the collections were joined. That also explains why a few psalms appear twice: Psalm 53 is almost the same as Psalm 14, and Psalm 70 repeats the end of Psalm 40.

### What it means, and where the reading stops

The blessings were sung by a people. The song David gave Asaph when the ark came to Jerusalem ends with the words that close Book IV: "Blessed be the LORD, the God of Israel, from everlasting to everlasting.' Then all the people said, 'Amen!'" (1 Chronicles 16:36 · BSB).

Many readers see a movement across the books. Book III ends with a psalm that mourns the fallen throne of David: "Where, O Lord, is Your loving devotion of old, which You faithfully swore to David?" (89:49 · BSB). Book IV opens with Moses, "Lord, You have been our dwelling place through all generations" (90:1 · BSB), and soon cries "The LORD reigns!" (93:1). Some scholars read this as the book's designed shape: the king's throne has fallen, but the LORD still reigns. That is a reading of the arrangement, held by some and doubted by others; Scripture does not say who arranged the five books, or when. What anyone can see is where the book ends: in praise.

**Consider:** Read the four doxologies side by side. What do they share? What does Psalm 150 add?

**Reflect:** The book does not reach "Hallelujah" by skipping the hard psalms; it reaches it through them. Where are you on that road now?

## 02 The gate: Psalms 1 and 2

**Main readings:** Psalms 1:1–6; 2:1–12; Acts 4:24–26; 13:33.

The book opens with a blessing and a war. Which way will you walk, and whom will you serve?

### What the text shows

Psalms 1 and 2 are two of only four psalms in Book I with no heading (10 and 33 are the others). They stand at the door before any named writer speaks. Psalm 1 sets out two ways:

> Blessed is the man who does not walk in the counsel of the wicked, or set foot on the path of sinners, or sit in the seat of mockers. But his delight is in the Law of the LORD, and on His law he meditates day and night.

Psalm 1:1–2 · BSB

The righteous man "is like a tree planted by streams of water" (1:3 · BSB); the wicked "are like chaff driven off by the wind" (1:4 · BSB). Psalm 2 turns from one man to the world: "Why do the nations rage and the peoples plot in vain?" (2:1 · BSB). The kings plot "against the LORD and against His Anointed One" (2:2 · BSB). God answers, "I have installed My King on Zion" (2:6 · BSB), and the King reports the decree: "You are My Son; today I have become Your Father" (2:7 · BSB). The psalm ends where Psalm 1 began: "Blessed are all who take refuge in Him" (2:12 · BSB).

### What it means

"Blessed" opens Psalm 1 and closes Psalm 2, framing them as one doorway. They give the reader two things to hold through all 150 psalms: God's word, to be loved, and God's King, to be trusted. Charles Spurgeon, the Baptist preacher, called Psalm 1's blessing the one "set in the forefront of the entire book", and saw "foreshadowings of the Son of David, who began his great sermon as David began his great psalm" (*The Treasury of David*, on Psalm 119:1). The church in Acts prayed Psalm 2 under threat and read it of Jesus: "You spoke by the Holy Spirit through the mouth of Your servant, our father David" (Acts 4:25 · BSB).

### Where the reading stops

Psalm 1 does not promise that the righteous never suffer; the very next psalms are full of their cries. The tree yields its fruit "in season" (1:3). Read the gate with the whole book, not against it.

**Consider:** Psalm 1:1 has a man who walks, then sets foot, then sits. What is happening to him?

**Reflect:** "On His law he meditates day and night." What does your mind turn to when nothing else demands it?

## 03 The headings: who wrote, and what we cannot know

**Main readings:** the headings of Psalms 18, 51, 72 and 90; 2 Samuel 22:1; 23:1–2; 1 Chronicles 25:1; Acts 4:25; Hebrews 4:7.

"A prayer of Moses the man of God." One line above Psalm 90 changes how you hear it.

### What the text shows

In the site's Berean text, 116 of the 150 psalms carry a heading and 34 do not. (Hebrew Bibles count the heading as part of the psalm.) The headings name these writers:

- **David**, in 73 headings, "the sweet psalmist of Israel", who said, "The Spirit of the LORD spoke through me; His word was on my tongue" (2 Samuel 23:1–2 · BSB).
- **Asaph**, in 12 (Psalms 50 and 73–83), head of one of the families of singers David set apart (1 Chronicles 25:1). Hezekiah's Levites sang "in the words of David and of Asaph the seer" (2 Chronicles 29:30 · BSB).
- **The sons of Korah**, a family of Levite singers, in 11 (Psalms 42, 44–49, 84–85, 87–88).
- **Solomon** in 2 (72, 127); **Moses** in 1 (90); **Heman** and **Ethan** in 1 each (88, 89).

Thirteen headings tie a psalm to a day in David's life, among them Psalm 3 (fleeing from Absalom) and Psalm 51:

> For the choirmaster. A Psalm of David. When Nathan the prophet came to him after his adultery with Bathsheba.

Heading of Psalm 51 · BSB

Psalm 18's heading matches the history almost word for word, and the song that follows in 2 Samuel 22 is Psalm 18 (2 Samuel 22:1). Other headings speak to singers: "For the choirmaster" (55 headings), tune names such as "The Doe of the Dawn" (Psalm 22), and words like *Maskil* and *Miktam*, which the Berean's notes call "probably a musical or liturgical term".

### What the headings tell us, and what they cannot

They tell us the psalms came from real people on real days. Read "Against You, You only, have I sinned" (51:4 · BSB) beside 2 Samuel 11–12 and the prayer has a face. They tell us the psalms were sung, with choirmasters and tunes. But three things in the text keep a reader humble:

1. **"Of David" is brief.** The Hebrew phrase is usually read "by David", but its little preposition can also mean "for" or "belonging to".
2. **No heading does not mean no author.** Psalms 2 and 95 have no heading, yet the New Testament names David as the voice of both (Acts 4:25; "He spoke through David", Hebrews 4:7 · BSB).
3. **Someone gathered them.** Psalm 72 is headed "Of Solomon" yet closes "the prayers of David" (72:20). Psalm 34's heading names "Abimelech" where 1 Samuel 21 names Achish; the Berean's note explains that Abimelech is "another name for Achish".

Christians who hold Scripture to be "truth, without any mixture of error", with the Baptist Faith and Message, treat the headings as very old witnesses that came down in the Hebrew text. Some hold that the psalmists wrote them; others, sharing the same trust in Scripture, think they were added early by those who gathered the psalms for worship. The confession does not address it, and this study does not choose.

**Consider:** Read 2 Samuel 22:1, then Psalm 18:2, "The LORD is my rock". What does knowing the day add?

**Reflect:** A king wrote Psalm 51 after his worst sin and gave it to the choirmaster for everyone to sing. Where does that say confession belongs?

## 04 The kinds of psalm

**Main readings:** Psalms 13; 23; 30; 73; 72; 113–118; 120–134.

"How long, O LORD?" and "Hallelujah!" are in the same book. Knowing which you are reading is half of reading it.

### Where the names come from

A few kinds are named by the headings: fifteen are headed "A song of ascents" (120–134), "A Psalm of praise" (145), "A Psalm of thanksgiving" (100). Most other names are a reader's tool, worked out by scholars in the last century from psalms that share a shape. They overlap, and readers place some psalms differently. These are the kinds most often named:

- **Praise.** A call to praise and a reason: "Praise the LORD, all you nations!... For great is His loving devotion toward us" (117:1–2 · BSB).
- **Lament.** A cry out of trouble, the largest group. Psalm 13 shows the shape in six verses: complaint, "How long, O LORD? Will You forget me forever?" (13:1 · BSB); request, "Consider me and respond" (13:3 · BSB); then trust and praise, "But I have trusted in Your loving devotion; my heart will rejoice in Your salvation" (13:5 · BSB). (Study 45 reads the psalms of sickness.)
- **Thanksgiving.** A lament answered and told to others: "You turned my mourning into dancing" (30:11 · BSB).
- **Trust.** Quiet confidence: "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want" (23:1 · BSB).
- **Wisdom.** The two ways, and why the wicked prosper (1, 37, 49, 73). Psalm 73 begins in envy and turns "until I entered God's sanctuary; then I discerned their end" (73:17 · BSB).
- **Royal.** The king of David's line crowned (2, 110), married (45) and reigning (72): "May he rule from sea to sea" (72:8 · BSB).
- **Imprecatory.** Psalms, or parts of them, that ask God to judge enemies (35, 69, 109, 137). Part 07 is theirs.
- **The songs of ascents.** Usually taken as songs for going up to Jerusalem for the feasts: "I was glad when they said to me, 'Let us go to the house of the LORD'" (122:1 · BSB). The contents fit; the heading does not explain itself.
- **The Hallel.** "Hallel" is Hebrew for praise. Psalms 113–118, opening "Hallelujah! Give praise, O servants of the LORD" (113:1 · BSB), were sung at Passover. Alfred Edersheim, working from the Jewish sources, says that at the meal's close "the second portion of the 'Hallel' was sung, consisting of Psalms 115, 116, 117, and 118" (*The Temple: Its Ministry and Services*, chapter 12). Many think this was the "hymn" of Matthew 26:30. That is likely, not certain: the Gospels do not say, and Edersheim warns that later Passover liturgy keeps "comparatively very few relics from New Testament times". Psalm 136, with its refrain "His loving devotion endures forever", is often called the great Hallel, though the old sources differ on that name's range.

### Where the reading stops

The kinds are a way to listen, not a box. Psalm 22 is a lament that ends in thanksgiving; Psalm 89 is a royal psalm that ends in lament. Ask the question, then let the psalm answer.

**Consider:** In Psalm 13, did the singer's circumstances change between verse 1 and verse 6? What did change?

**Reflect:** Which kind of psalm do you find easiest to pray? Which do you skip?

## 05 How Hebrew poetry works

**Main readings:** Psalms 19:1; 1:6; 103:12; 119:1–16, 105; 145:13.

English poems rhyme sounds. Hebrew poems rhyme thoughts, and a rhyme of thought survives translation.

### Parallel lines

Most verses in the Psalms say something twice. The second line echoes the first, or answers it, or carries it further. This is **parallelism**. The second line can **echo**:

> The heavens declare the glory of God; the skies proclaim the work of His hands.

Psalm 19:1 · BSB

It can **contrast**: "For the LORD guards the path of the righteous, but the way of the wicked will perish" (1:6 · BSB). Or it can **build** with a picture: "As far as the east is from the west, so far has He removed our transgressions from us" (103:12 · BSB). Paired lines interpret each other: when a word is hard, look at its partner.

### The alphabet psalms

Some psalms are **acrostics**: their lines or stanzas begin with the letters of the Hebrew alphabet in order. The Berean's notes mark Psalms 25, 34, 37, 111, 112, 119 and 145, and 9 and 10 together. Psalm 119, the longest chapter in the Bible, is "an acrostic poem of twenty-two stanzas, following the letters of the Hebrew alphabet; within a stanza, each verse begins with the same Hebrew letter" (the Berean's note): twenty-two letters, eight verses each, 176 verses on God's word. "Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path" (119:105 · BSB) opens the stanza for *nun*.

An acrostic is not a code. It is a form, like a sonnet: a way to say everything from A to Z and to make a poem easy to learn by heart.

### Where the reading stops

Translation keeps parallelism but loses the alphabet; you need the notes to see it. In Psalm 145 the line for *nun* is missing from most Hebrew manuscripts. The Berean prints it at the end of verse 13, "The LORD is faithful in all His words and kind in all His actions" (145:13 · BSB), and notes that it is "supplied by one MT manuscript, LXX, Syriac, and DSS": one manuscript of the traditional Hebrew text, the ancient Greek and Syriac translations, and the Dead Sea Scrolls. (The Academic studies *How Hebrew works* and *The Dead Sea Scrolls* go further.)

**Consider:** In Psalm 103:1, "Bless the LORD, O my soul; all that is within me, bless His holy name", what does the second line add?

**Reflect:** What would it take to learn one stanza of Psalm 119, eight verses, by heart?

## 06 The psalms the New Testament quotes most

**Main readings:** Psalms 2:7; 16:10; 22:1, 18, 22–24; 110:1, 4; 118:22–26; Acts 2:29–36.

Peter stood in Jerusalem and pointed to a grave: David's tomb "is with us to this day" (Acts 2:29 · BSB). So whom was the psalm about?

### What the text shows

The Berean's notes mark where a psalm is cited in the New Testament (quotations, not every echo). Counted, 40 psalms are cited. Psalm 118 leads, in 13 New Testament passages; Psalm 110 follows with 8; Psalms 2 and 22 have 5 each (as do 69 and 95). Psalm 16 is cited only twice, but both times at the heart of the first sermons in Acts. All five are read of Christ.

- **Psalm 2, the Son.** "You are My Son; today I have become Your Father" (2:7). Paul reads it of the resurrection: "As it is written in the second Psalm" (Acts 13:33 · BSB); Hebrews of the Son above the angels (1:5). The Father's voice at Jesus' baptism echoes it: "You are My beloved Son" (Mark 1:11 · BSB).
- **Psalm 16, the Holy One.** "For You will not abandon my soul to Sheol, nor will You let Your Holy One see decay" (16:10 · BSB). David died and was buried, Peter argues, so he spoke of someone else.
- **Psalm 22, the sufferer who is heard.** Jesus cried its first line from the cross: "My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?" (Matthew 27:46 · BSB). The soldiers did what it describes: "They divided My garments among them, and cast lots for My clothing" (John 19:24 · BSB). Then the psalm turns: "He has not hidden His face from him, but has attended to his cry for help" (22:24 · BSB), and Hebrews gives Jesus its next words, "I will proclaim Your name to My brothers" (Hebrews 2:12 · BSB).
- **Psalm 110, David's Lord.** "The LORD said to my Lord: 'Sit at My right hand until I make Your enemies a footstool for Your feet'" (110:1 · BSB). Jesus asked, "So if David calls Him 'Lord,' how can He be David's son?" (Matthew 22:45 · BSB). Hebrews builds Christ's priesthood on verse 4, "You are a priest forever in the order of Melchizedek" (Hebrews 5:6 · BSB).
- **Psalm 118, the rejected stone.** "The stone the builders rejected has become the cornerstone" (118:22 · BSB): Jesus applied it to Himself (Matthew 21:42), Peter preached it (Acts 4:11) and wrote it (1 Peter 2:7). The Palm Sunday crowds sang its verses 25–26, "Blessed is He who comes in the name of the Lord!" (Matthew 21:9 · BSB); *Hosanna*, the Berean notes, means "save, we pray".

### How the New Testament reads them

Peter gives the key:

> But he was a prophet and knew that God had promised him on oath that He would place one of his descendants on his throne. Foreseeing this, David spoke about the resurrection of the Christ, that He was not abandoned to Hades, nor did His body see decay.

Acts 2:30–31 · BSB

So the apostles read David as a prophet, whose psalms could say more than was true of himself. The royal psalms find their King: what was promised to David's son, and never fully true of any king in Jerusalem, is true of Jesus. (Study 41, Christ in the Old Testament, follows the king and the priest; Study 37, Long Memory, walks Peter's sermon.) And Jesus did not only fulfil the psalms; He prayed them, on the cross (Psalm 22:1, and Psalm 31:5 in Luke 23:46).

### Where the reading stops

Not every line of every psalm is spoken by Christ. Psalm 69:9 is quoted of Him: "Zeal for Your house will consume Me" (John 2:17 · BSB). Yet the same psalm confesses, "You know my folly, O God, and my guilt is not hidden from You" (69:5 · BSB), which the sinless Son could not say of Himself. Follow the New Testament where it reads a psalm of Christ; elsewhere, read the psalm first as its singer's prayer, which Christ's people now share.

**Consider:** Read all of Psalm 22. Where does it turn, and what does the second half add to the first verse?

**Reflect:** Jesus took a psalm's words for His darkest hour. Which psalm would you want for yours?

## 07 The hard psalms: when the singer curses

**Main readings:** Psalms 137; 109:1–8; 35:11–14; 139:19–24; Acts 1:20; Romans 12:19; Revelation 6:10.

A song that begins by the rivers of Babylon ends with words most readers cannot say aloud. Why are they in the Bible?

### What the text shows

Psalm 137 begins, "By the rivers of Babylon we sat and wept when we remembered Zion" (137:1 · BSB). It ends:

> O Daughter of Babylon, doomed to destruction, blessed is he who repays you as you have done to us. Blessed is he who seizes your infants and dashes them against the rocks.

Psalm 137:8–9 · BSB

Psalm 109 prays against an accuser, "May his days be few; may another take his position" (109:8 · BSB); Psalm 69, "Pour out Your wrath upon them" (69:24 · BSB); Psalm 139, "I hate them with perfect hatred" (139:22 · BSB). Do not soften these words, and do not skip them. Look closely first.

### Look closely

1. **They are prayers, not deeds.** The singer asks God to judge; he does not take revenge. "In return for my love they accuse me, but I am a man of prayer" (109:4 · BSB). Paul tells Christians the same: "Do not avenge yourselves, beloved, but leave room for God's wrath" (Romans 12:19 · BSB).
2. **They come from the wronged.** Psalm 137 is the cry of people who had seen their city destroyed, asking that Babylon be repaid "as you have done to us" (137:8).
3. **The same singers loved their enemies.** "Yet when they were ill, I put on sackcloth; I humbled myself with fasting" (35:13 · BSB). Psalm 139's "perfect hatred" is followed at once by "Search me, O God, and know my heart" (139:23 · BSB).
4. **The New Testament quotes them.** Peter applies Psalms 69:25 and 109:8 to Judas (Acts 1:20); Paul uses Psalm 69:22–23 (Romans 11:9–10); the martyrs in heaven still cry, "How long, O Lord, holy and true, until You avenge our blood" (Revelation 6:10 · BSB).

### How Christians have read them

Christians agree on what these psalms are not: permission to hate a neighbour or take revenge. Jesus said, "love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you" (Matthew 5:44 · BSB). Beyond that, three readings are held, and they can be combined:

- **A prophecy of God's judgment.** Spurgeon wrote of Psalm 137:9, "We shall be wise to view this passage as a prophecy", granting that "the desire for righteous retribution is rather the spirit of the law than of the gospel", and adding, "A feeling of universal love is admirable, but it must not be divorced from a keen sense of justice" (*The Treasury of David*, on Psalm 137:9). Acts 1:20 reads Psalms 69 and 109 this way, as fulfilled in Judas.
- **Turned against spiritual enemies.** Isaac Watts, setting the psalms for church singing (1719), wrote: "Where the Psalmist uses sharp invectives against his personal enemies, I have endeavoured to turn the edge of them against our spiritual adversaries, sin, Satan, and temptation."
- **An honest prayer for justice, left with God.** The psalms teach the wronged to bring their anger to God instead of acting on it, as Psalm 109:4 does and the martyrs of Revelation 6:10 still do.

The first reading follows the New Testament's own use; the third follows the psalms' own words; the second is a way of singing them more than a reading of their first meaning. All three keep these psalms as God's word, and none lets a Christian curse a person Christ calls him to love. Paul could call down a curse on a false gospel (Galatians 1:8) and still say of a man who harmed him, "The Lord will repay him according to his deeds" (2 Timothy 4:14 · BSB), and leave it there.

**Consider:** In Psalm 109:4–5, what had the singer given his accusers, and what did he do when they repaid him with evil?

**Reflect:** Is there a wrong done to you that you have been trying to settle yourself? What would it mean to hand it, honestly, to God?

## Read the Psalms again

The book that begins "Blessed is the man" ends "Let everything that has breath praise the LORD!" The road between runs through every kind of human cry, and the church has never stopped singing it: "Speak to one another with psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs" (Ephesians 5:19 · BSB). John Calvin called the book "An Anatomy of all the Parts of the Soul", "for there is not an emotion of which any one can be conscious that is not here represented as in a mirror" (*Commentary on the Book of Psalms*, author's preface). The Hymns study shows how Isaac Watts and others turned the psalms into songs churches still sing.

**Your question:** Somewhere in these 150 psalms is the prayer you need this week: a lament, a thanksgiving, a confession like Psalm 51, a quiet trust like Psalm 23. Which psalm is it, and will you pray it, aloud, to the God who gave it?

Read the passage again before you answer.
