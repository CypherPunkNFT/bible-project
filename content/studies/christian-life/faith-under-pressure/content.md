# Faith under pressure

Concepts: Trials · Temptation · Anxiety · Opposition · Grief · Need

Motto: A small light. Two hands. / Held, not explained.

Lead: The letters were written to people who were being tested, tempted, worried, slandered, bereaved and left hungry. They do not give one answer to all of it. This study walks six pressures and teaches you four questions: which pressure is this, what does this passage call for, what ground does it stand on, and what does it not promise?

> Cast all your anxiety on Him, because He cares for you.

1 Peter 5:7 · BSB

Frame: These were not written from a safe distance. Paul tells the Corinthians, "We were under a burden far beyond our ability to endure, so that we despaired even of life", and in the same paragraph speaks of trusting God, who raises the dead, and of the help of their prayers (2 Corinthians 1:8–11). So do not make every hardship tell the same story. A temptation to do wrong is not the same as mourning someone who has died. Suffering for doing good is not the same as facing the results of doing wrong. Each has its own passage, and each passage asks something different.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. These six pressures were chosen because the letters give each of them a paragraph of its own. Other lists differ: the Psalms of lament, Job and Lamentations are the Bible's fullest words on suffering and are not walked here, and illness, depression, abuse and long unanswered prayer have no reading of their own. This study reads Scripture; it is not medical or professional guidance.

## 01 Trials and endurance

Slug: trials

Hook: James says joy, and in the next breath he says: ask.

Line: Testing is given a direction, and the person inside it is told to ask for wisdom, not to pretend.

Read: James 1:2–8; Romans 5:1–5

Setting: James writes to believers scattered abroad (James 1:1). Romans 5 comes straight after Paul's long argument that God justifies by faith.

Pressure: "trials of many kinds" and "our sufferings" (James 1:2; Romans 5:3).

Call: Let perseverance finish its work, and ask God for wisdom (James 1:4–5).

Ground: God "gives generously to all without finding fault", and has poured out his love into our hearts (James 1:5; Romans 5:5).

Limit: It does not say the trial feels good, and it does not tell you why this one came to you.

### What endurance is aimed at

James says to consider it joy when trials come, "because you know that the testing of your faith develops perseverance" (James 1:2–3). Look where the joy points. It points at what perseverance is doing, so that you may be "mature and complete" (James 1:4). He does not call the pain pleasant.

Then comes the surprise. The person in the trial is not expected to understand it already. If you lack wisdom, ask, because God gives "without finding fault" (James 1:5). You will not be scolded for asking.

[source:calvin-james] Calvin takes the word in this verse broadly, "as including all adverse things", and calls such things "tests of our obedience to God".

[care] James also says the one asking must not doubt, and calls the doubter "double-minded" (James 1:6–8). Read that beside the invitation just before it. The warning is against divided loyalty to God. It does not give anyone a formula for explaining why another person's prayer has not been answered, and it does not make an honest question the same thing as double-mindedness.

### Hope that stands on God

Romans 5 opens with peace, before it mentions any suffering: "we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 5:1). Only then does Paul trace the chain, "suffering produces perseverance; perseverance, character; and character, hope" (Romans 5:3–4). The hope does not rest on how well the chain is going. It holds "because God has poured out His love into our hearts through the Holy Spirit" (Romans 5:5).

Put in this site's words: these passages give suffering a horizon. They do not let you name the reason for someone else's pain, or decide how quickly their endurance ought to show. The language of growth is an encouragement to keep going, not a licence to judge from a distance.

Look closely: What does James tell the person who lacks wisdom to do? What comes before the account of suffering in Romans 5, and what comes after it?

Your response: Name one decision inside a present difficulty for which you need wisdom. Ask God for that one thing, without claiming to have explained the whole situation.

## 02 Temptation

Slug: temptation

Hook: Nobody falls all at once. James draws the steps.

Line: Temptation is traced from desire to death, God is cleared of it, and the promise comes with a door marked exit.

Read: James 1:12–18; 1 Corinthians 10:1–14

Setting: James turns from trials outside a person to the pull inside one. Paul is writing to Corinthians at risk of sharing in idol feasts (1 Corinthians 8:10), and retells Israel's wilderness years as a warning.

Pressure: "his own evil desires", and the craving of "evil things" (James 1:14; 1 Corinthians 10:6).

Call: Be careful not to fall, and "flee from idolatry" (1 Corinthians 10:12–14).

Ground: "God is faithful", and every good gift comes from above (1 Corinthians 10:13; James 1:17).

Limit: It does not promise that temptation will feel small, or that every kind of hardship will stay within your strength.

### Seeing the steps

When tempted, do not say God is doing it, for "nor does He tempt anyone" (James 1:13). James then draws a line of births: desire conceives, it gives birth to sin, and sin "gives birth to death" (James 1:14–15). James describes the steps. In the same paragraph God is the giver of "Every good and perfect gift" (James 1:16–18).

[care] James has just said trials are to be counted joy, and now says God tempts no one. The Greek uses one family of words for both, which the Berean separates as "trials" and "tempted" (James 1:2; James 1:13). So the two sentences do not clash, but they are about two things.

[source:calvin-james] Calvin sees this. "Here, no doubt, he speaks of another kind of temptation": not outward testing but "the inordinate desires which entice to sin".

An opportunity, a pressure or a desire can be named before it is obeyed. Naming it early is this site's application, not a command from James.

### The warning, the promise and the door

These three sentences belong together, and in this order:

> So the one who thinks he is standing firm should be careful not to fall.

1 Corinthians 10:12 · BSB

> No temptation has seized you except what is common to man. And God is faithful; He will not let you be tempted beyond what you can bear. But when you are tempted, He will also provide an escape, so that you can stand up under it.

1 Corinthians 10:13 · BSB

> Therefore, my beloved, flee from idolatry.

1 Corinthians 10:14 · BSB

The promise sits between a warning and a command to run. It gives no reason to stay close to the thing it warns about.

[care] Does God never give more than we can bear? This verse is about temptation. Elsewhere Paul says he was "under a burden far beyond our ability to endure", and he gives a reason: "in order that we would not trust in ourselves, but in God, who raises the dead" (2 Corinthians 1:8–9). So do not hand this verse to a suffering person as a promise that they will always feel able to cope.

[source:calvin-cor] Calvin says God helps in two ways: "he supplies us with strength, and he sets limits to the temptation".

[source:calvin-2cor] On Paul's burden, Calvin explains that Paul "does not measure his strength in connection with help from God, but according to his own personal feeling of his ability".

[care] Paul's warning does not undo the believer's security. The confession this site writes under holds that those God has accepted in Christ "will never fall away from the state of grace" (Article 5), and it also says believers can fall into sin and grieve the Spirit. Hold both: the warning is real, and it is one of the ways God keeps his people.

[other] Other Christians, in the Wesleyan and Arminian line, read warnings like this one as showing that a true believer can finally fall away. That view is theirs, and it is not the view of the confession this site writes under. No named source arguing it has been read for this page, so none is quoted.

[care] Paul says "twenty-three thousand" died in one day; Numbers says 24,000 died in the plague (1 Corinthians 10:8; Numbers 25:9). The numbers differ, and this page says so. The confession holds Scripture to be truth without error (Article 1), and this site reads the two together.

[source:calvin-cor] Calvin says that when a writer is not counting exactly, it is "no unusual thing" to give a number near the total, so that "Moses has set down the number above the mark, and Paul, the number below it". Paul also says "in one day", and Numbers counts those who died in the plague, so the two may not be the same count. Both are explanations, and the text does not state either.

Look closely: What warning comes before the promise in verse 13, and what action follows it? Where does James locate the responsibility when desire moves toward sin?

Your response: Name a situation in which you keep moving toward a harmful choice. Choose one concrete way to step away from it, and tell someone you trust.

## 03 Anxiety and prayer

Slug: anxiety

Hook: Be anxious for nothing, and bring God everything.

Line: A worry is not hidden or denied: it is named before God as a request, with thanks, and the same passages go on to say what to do next.

Read: Philippians 4:4–9; 1 Peter 5:6–10

Setting: Paul writes in chains (Philippians 1:13). Peter writes to believers who know that others "throughout the world" are suffering the same things (1 Peter 5:9).

Pressure: "anxiety", and an adversary who "prowls around like a roaring lion" (1 Peter 5:7–8).

Call: Present your requests with thanksgiving; think on what is true; put it into practice; be alert and resist (Philippians 4:6–9; 1 Peter 5:8–9).

Ground: "The Lord is near", and "He cares for you" (Philippians 4:5; 1 Peter 5:7).

Limit: It gives no date on which the feeling will stop, and it does not say that a heavy heart proves weak faith.

### Bringing the real concern

[care] If worry has become so heavy that you are thinking of harming yourself or of not wanting to live, please reach out now. Tell someone you trust today. In the United States, call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline [check before publishing]. In Brazil, call the CVV on 188 [check before publishing]. In Spain, call 024 [check before publishing]. In Mexico, call the Línea de la Vida on 800 911 2000 [check before publishing]. In other Spanish-speaking countries, call your local emergency number or your national crisis line [check: the crisis line for each other Spanish-speaking country the site serves]. Feelings this heavy do pass, and people do recover, often with help they did not expect to find.

> Be anxious for nothing, but in everything, by prayer and petition, with thanksgiving, present your requests to God.

Philippians 4:6 · BSB

Notice the two words that face each other: nothing and everything. The command does not ask you to act as though there were no need. It asks you to put the need into words and give it to God. The next verse promises "the peace of God", which will guard hearts and minds in Christ Jesus (Philippians 4:7).

Then Paul goes on: think on what is true, honourable, right and pure, and practise what you have learned (Philippians 4:8–9). Prayer, thought and conduct all belong to the same paragraph.

[care] Paul was no stranger to this himself. He writes that he would have had "sorrow upon sorrow" if Epaphroditus had died (Philippians 2:27), and that he despaired even of life (2 Corinthians 1:8). So these verses do not make a heavy heart proof of weak faith. What follows is this site's own word to you, not a quotation from Paul, and it is not professional guidance. Anxiety can also be an illness, and seeing a doctor or a counsellor does not contradict this passage.

This part reads anxiety as one pressure among six. Fear that will not lift, long anxiety and despair have a study of their own, with the Psalms that give them words.

Read more in *Fear, anxiety and despair*.

### Care inside a larger passage

First Peter joins two things that are often pulled apart: "Humble yourselves" and "Cast all your anxiety on Him" (1 Peter 5:6–7). A reader might hear the words of Psalm 55:22 in that command, though Peter does not cite it. The reason he gives is simply God's care.

[source:calvin-peter] Calvin reads verse 7 with the humility before it. People fear that meekness will leave them trampled, but "as soon as we are convinced that God cares for us, our minds are easily led to patience and humility".

The next verses are not gentle: "Your adversary the devil prowls around like a roaring lion", and the reader is told to resist him, standing firm (1 Peter 5:8–9). Trust and watchfulness go together. Handing your concern to God does not mean ceasing to notice what needs attention. And the paragraph closes with an end that is God's work, not yours: he "will Himself restore you, secure you, strengthen you, and establish you" (1 Peter 5:10).

Look closely: What can be made known to God in Philippians 4:6? How do the surrounding verses connect prayer with thought and practice?

Your response: Put one concern into words, make one particular request, and give thanks for one true thing, without pretending the difficulty is gone. Then ask what practical step or what help belongs beside the prayer.

## 04 Persecution

Slug: opposition

Hook: Peter asks first: what did you suffer for?

Line: Suffering for doing good is blessed, suffering for doing wrong is not, and the answer you give is to be gentle either way.

Read: 1 Peter 3:13–17; 1 Peter 4:12–19

Setting: Believers being slandered and insulted for the name of Christ, in a letter that tells them not to be surprised by it.

Pressure: "the fiery trial", and being "insulted for the name of Christ" (1 Peter 4:12–14).

Call: Answer "with gentleness and respect", and "continue to do good" (1 Peter 3:15; 1 Peter 4:19).

Ground: "the Spirit of glory and of God rests on you", and you may entrust your soul to a "faithful Creator" (1 Peter 4:14; 1 Peter 4:19).

Limit: It does not promise safety, and it does not say that being opposed proves you were right.

### Suffering for doing good

"But even if you should suffer for what is right, you are blessed" (1 Peter 3:14). Peter then quotes Isaiah 8:12, "Do not fear what they fear", and turns it toward Christ: "in your hearts sanctify Christ as Lord" (1 Peter 3:15). Isaiah goes on to say the LORD of Hosts is the One to regard as holy (Isaiah 8:13), and Peter gives that place to Christ.

Be ready to give a reason for your hope, and give it "with gentleness and respect" (1 Peter 3:15–16). The way you answer belongs to the instruction. Defending hope does not set gentleness aside. Chapter 4 tells readers not to be surprised, and connects their suffering with "the sufferings of Christ" and hope at "the revelation of His glory" (1 Peter 4:12–13).

### Suffering for good, suffering for wrong

Peter says so straight away: "none of you should suffer as a murderer or thief or wrongdoer, or even as a meddler" (1 Peter 4:15). So being opposed does not prove by itself that you were right. This site's application: before you call a conflict persecution, ask what caused it. That is a question for your own conduct, and it is not an accusation; many who suffer have done nothing to deserve it.

> So then, those who suffer according to God’s will should entrust their souls to their faithful Creator and continue to do good.

1 Peter 4:19 · BSB

[care] Does endurance mean staying in danger? No. Jesus told his disciples, "When they persecute you in one town, flee to the next" (Matthew 10:23), and Paul's disciples lowered him through a window in the wall in a basket (Acts 9:25; 2 Corinthians 11:33). This passage is about being opposed for Christ's name. It does not tell anyone being harmed at home to stay and bear it; getting to safety and asking for help does not disobey it. That last sentence is this site's application, and it is marked as one.

[open] Peter adds that "it is time for judgment to begin with the family of God", and he quotes Proverbs 11:31 (1 Peter 4:17–18). Just what that judgment is, this study does not settle; it belongs to the planned study The hope to come.

Look closely: What distinction does Peter make about the reason for suffering? What should stay visible when someone questions a believer's hope?

Your response: Ask what prompted the conflict you are in, without assuming the answer either way. Where faithfulness really is being opposed, ask how to answer truthfully and gently while you go on doing good.

## 05 Grief and hope

Slug: grief

Hook: A young church has buried its dead, and it has questions.

Line: Paul does not say do not grieve. He says do not grieve like those without hope, and he tells you why.

Read: 1 Thessalonians 4:13–14

Setting: Believers worried about those who had died before the Lord's coming. The verses that follow suggest they feared the dead would miss it; that is an inference from Paul's answer.

Pressure: Grief for "those who sleep in death" (1 Thessalonians 4:13).

Call: Grieve, but not "like the rest, who are without hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13).

Ground: "Jesus died and rose again", and God "will bring with Jesus those who have fallen asleep in Him" (1 Thessalonians 4:14).

Limit: It does not say how long mourning should last, and it does not give a chart of future events.

### Not without hope

[care] If you have lost someone and the grief has brought thoughts of not wanting to go on, please tell someone today. The crisis lines in part 03 are there for you: in the United States, call or text 988 [check before publishing]; in Brazil, call the CVV on 188 [check before publishing]; in Spain, call 024 [check before publishing]; in Mexico, call 800 911 2000 [check before publishing]; elsewhere, your local emergency number [check: the crisis line for each other Spanish-speaking country the site serves]. Grief this sharp does ease with time and company, even when that is hard to believe.

> Brothers, we do not want you to be uninformed about those who sleep in death, so that you will not grieve like the rest, who are without hope. For since we believe that Jesus died and rose again, we also believe that God will bring with Jesus those who have fallen asleep in Him.

1 Thessalonians 4:13–14 · BSB

The contrast is with grief that has no hope. It is not a ban on grief. Jesus wept at a friend's tomb (John 11:35); devout men "mourned deeply" over Stephen (Acts 8:2); and believers are told to "weep with those who weep" (Romans 12:15).

[source:calvin-thess] Calvin allows grief, but says it does not become believers "to mourn otherwise than in moderation", and so reads the verse as setting a limit. This site lays no measure on anyone's mourning, because of the mourning Scripture itself records, and because this verse names none.

The ground is not that death is small. It is "since we believe that Jesus died and rose again" (1 Thessalonians 4:14). If you are not yet sure you believe that, this is where to look first, because the comfort stands on it.

[care] Asleep is the gentle word Paul uses for believers who have died. It does not mean they are unconscious: Paul also calls death being "at home with the Lord" and being "with Christ" (2 Corinthians 5:8; Philippians 1:23). And this paragraph speaks of those who have fallen asleep "in Him" (1 Thessalonians 4:14). It does not say how to grieve someone whose faith you did not know, and this page will not say more than it does. Your grief for them is real. Abraham's question stands: "Will not the Judge of all the earth do what is right?" (Genesis 18:25). A pastor can walk through that question with you.

### Where the paragraph goes next

Paul goes on to describe the Lord's coming and the meeting of the living and the dead with him, and he ends by telling the church to encourage one another with these words (1 Thessalonians 4:15–18). He was not writing a timetable for people who were curious. He was writing to people who were burying someone. That passage, and the questions Baptists answer in more than one way about when this meeting happens, are read in full in another study.

Read more in *The hope to come*.

Hope does not make the person who died unimportant, and it does not hurry the one who misses them. You can speak this promise and still listen to what they remember. That is this site's application, not a timetable for someone else's sorrow. Mourning, dying and how to stay beside someone through both have a study of their own.

Read more in *Grief and dying*.

Look closely: What concern does Paul address, and what does he give as the basis of hope in verse 14? Why does he set grief with hope against grief without it, rather than telling the church not to grieve?

Your response: If you are beside someone who is grieving, ask whether they need you to listen before you speak. Offer this hope with care, and do not use it to close a conversation about their loss.

## 06 Contentment

Slug: contentment

Hook: The most quoted verse about strength is about being hungry.

Line: Contentment is learned in plenty and in need, and it does not make other people's help unnecessary.

Read: Philippians 4:10–20; Hebrews 13:5–6

Setting: Paul thanking the Philippians for money they sent him while he was in chains. Hebrews sets contentment against the love of money.

Pressure: "being filled and being hungry", "having plenty and having need" (Philippians 4:12).

Call: Learn contentment, share in one another's affliction, and keep free from the love of money (Philippians 4:11–14; Hebrews 13:5).

Ground: Christ "who gives me strength", and God's word, "Never will I leave you" (Philippians 4:13; Hebrews 13:5).

Limit: It does not promise wealth, and it does not promise success at whatever goal you choose.

### Strength in plenty and in need

Paul says he has "learned to be content regardless of my circumstances". He learned it. He names the circumstances: being filled and being hungry, having plenty and having need (Philippians 4:11–12). Then comes the sentence people put on walls:

> I can do all things through Christ who gives me strength.

Philippians 4:13 · BSB

Read it where Paul put it. All things, here, means living through what he has just listed. It is not a guarantee of success at any goal you choose.

[source:calvin-php] Calvin says the same: "When he says all things, he means merely those things which belong to his calling."

[source:maclaren-php] Alexander Maclaren, a Baptist preacher, draws the contrast with self-reliance: "Stoicism breaks down because it tries to make men apart from God sufficient for themselves, which no man is."

And Paul's next word is "Nevertheless": "you have done well to share in my affliction" (Philippians 4:14). He has just said he can do all things, and he still thanks them for the money. Depending on Christ and receiving help from people stand in one paragraph, so receiving help is no failure of contentment.

### What can be relied on

"And my God will supply all your needs according to His glorious riches in Christ Jesus" (Philippians 4:19). That sentence follows a church's generous giving, and it comes from a man who has just told them he has been hungry.

[care] This verse is sometimes taught as a return on giving. The paragraph has already named real hunger and real need (Philippians 4:12). The confession teaches stewardship, giving "cheerfully, regularly, systematically, proportionately, and liberally" (Article 13). It does not promise riches as a reward for faith, and neither does this passage.

Hebrews gives contentment the same footing: "be content with what you have", and it quotes God's own promise, then answers it from Psalm 118:6 (Hebrews 13:5–6). The older words behind that promise are Moses' to Israel (Deuteronomy 31:6). Confidence rests in the Lord's help, not in having more.

Look closely: Which circumstances frame Philippians 4:13? Why does Paul commend the gift after saying he has learned contentment?

Your response: Separate a real need from a comparison that is feeding dissatisfaction. What help may you rightly ask for, what can you receive with thanks, and what do you have to share?

## Receiving and sharing comfort

Way: Name the pressure. Hear what this passage calls for. Find the ground it stands on. Notice what it does not promise. Then go back and read it again.

Close: Paul calls God "the Father of compassion and the God of all comfort", who comforts us "so that we can comfort those in any trouble" (2 Corinthians 1:3–4). Faith under pressure can mean enduring, asking for wisdom, running from temptation, naming a worry, mourning, receiving help or sitting beside someone else. You do not have to make every hardship tell the same story.

Question: Which pressure are you actually under this week, and what is the one thing its passage asks of you?

Again: Read the passage again before you answer.
