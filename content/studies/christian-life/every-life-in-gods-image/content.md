# Every life in God's image

Subtitle: why every person counts, from the womb to the last breath

Concepts: The womb · The table · The gate · The bedside

Motto: Knit in secret. / Seated at the table.

Lead: The Bible says every human being is made "in the image of God". That means each person is made to reflect God and to belong to Him, and so is never just a body to be used or a cost to be weighed. This study teaches one reading habit. When a passage tells you how to treat a person, ask who is in the room who cannot speak for themselves: the unborn child, the disabled man, the poor stranger, the dying patient.

> For You formed my inmost being; You knit me together in my mother’s womb.

Psalm 139:13 · BSB

Frame: Most of us agree that people matter. The hard part is deciding which people we notice. The passages in this study keep pointing at the ones who are easy to overlook. One was not yet born. One was lame in both feet and living in a town far from the palace. One came to church in shabby clothes. One was a foreigner in a lawsuit with no one to speak for him. Scripture does not let any of them drop out of sight.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. It walks five passages that this site's other studies do not walk: Psalm 139:13–16, 2 Samuel 9, James 2:1–9, Exodus 23:6–9 and Proverbs 31:8–9. Other passages are quoted briefly and linked to the study that reads them in full. The fuller teaching on what the image of God is belongs to *Humanity, sin and renewal*. This study is not medical, legal or professional advice. Where it touches a decision about treatment, a pregnancy or a fertility clinic, it gives Scripture and the church's teaching, and it sends you to the people who can advise you.

## 01 In His own image: the ground under every life

**Main readings:** Genesis 1:26–27; Genesis 9:6 (quoted; read in full in *Humanity, sin and renewal* and *Genesis 1–11: the questions readers bring*)

Hook: Before anyone had done anything, God had already said what they were.

The first page of the Bible says what a human being is before it says anything a human being does.

> So God created man in His own image; in the image of God He created him; male and female He created them.

Genesis 1:27 · BSB

An image, in the ancient world, was a likeness that stood for someone, the way a king's statue in a far city showed whose land it was. Christians have long debated exactly what the image of God consists of. That debate belongs to *Humanity, sin and renewal*. For this study, one thing is enough. The verse says it of "man", which in this verse means humankind, "male and female". It does not say it of the strong, the clever, the useful or the healthy. It says it of everyone.

After the flood, when the world has been badly broken by violence, God says it again:

> Whoever sheds the blood of man, by man his blood will be shed; for in His own image God has made mankind.

Genesis 9:6 · BSB

[read] Look at the reason God gives: "for in His own image God has made mankind". By then people had gone badly wrong, and still the image is the reason a human life may not be taken lightly. Sin damages the image. Scripture never says it erases it.

[read] The Baptist Faith and Message 2000, the statement of faith this site writes under, draws the same line. Its article III (on humanity) says that because God made man in His image and Christ died for man, "every person of every race possesses full dignity and is worthy of respect and Christian love". Its article XV (the Christian and the social order) asks believers to "speak on behalf of the unborn and contend for the sanctity of all human life from conception to natural death". "Sanctity" means that life is set apart as belonging to God, and so is not ours to end.

[open] Genesis 9:6 is also read as bearing on how a society punishes murder. The Baptist Faith and Message does not speak to the death penalty, and this study does not take it up. [check: whether *The Christian and the state* covers it, and link there if so]

**Consider:** What reason does Genesis 9:6 give for the worth of a human life? Who does Genesis 1:27 leave out?

**Reflect:** Whose worth do you find yourself measuring by what they can do? What changes if you begin instead with what God has said they are?

## 02 Knit together: the unborn child

**Main readings:** Psalm 139:13–16

Hook: Before anyone saw his face, Someone was watching.

Psalm 139 is a song of David, written as prayer. It begins with God knowing him wherever he goes. Then, in the middle of the song, he goes back to the place before he can remember.

> For You formed my inmost being; You knit me together in my mother’s womb. I praise You, for I am fearfully and wonderfully made. Marvelous are Your works, and I know this very well.

Psalm 139:13–14 · BSB

Notice the words he chooses: formed, knit, woven. These are words for careful handwork. And notice the person speaking. The one praying as a grown man says "my inmost being" and "me" about the child in the womb. He does not speak of something that later became him. He speaks of himself.

> My frame was not hidden from You when I was made in secret, when I was woven together in the depths of the earth. Your eyes saw my unformed body; all my days were written in Your book and ordained for me before one of them came to be.

Psalm 139:15–16 · BSB

"The depths of the earth" is a poetic picture. It is a hidden place, unseen, and here it stands for the womb, as the verse before has just said. "Your eyes saw my unformed body" means that God saw him before he had a shape anyone else could recognise.

[read] This is a song, not a textbook on how babies grow, and it should be read as one. A song can still say what is true. What it says here is that God's care, God's sight and God's purpose rest on a person before birth. This is one of the places the Baptist Faith and Message stands when it speaks of life "from conception". The site holds that view in its own voice: the child in the womb is a human life made by God, and is to be protected.

[read] Verse 16 says "all my days were written in Your book". That speaks of every life, not only a long or healthy one. A child who lives a few hours, or who is never born, is also one whose days God saw. This site does not say more than the psalm says about such a child. It can say that this child was known, seen and made by God.

[care] Some readers will hear this psalm as a judgement on something they did. Read on to the question below on abortion and forgiveness. The same God who knit you together is the One who forgives.

**Consider:** What words does David use for how God made him? Who is speaking in verse 13, and about whom?

**Reflect:** If God saw you before anyone else did, what does that say about the people who have not yet been seen, or who cannot be seen at all?

## 03 At the king's table: disability and belonging

**Main readings:** 2 Samuel 9; John 9:2–3 (quoted; read in full in *The signs and works of Jesus*)

Hook: The new king asks a question no new king asks: is anyone left from the old one's family?

In the ancient Near East a new dynasty usually cleared away the old one's heirs. David is now king in place of Saul. But he once made a promise to Saul's son Jonathan, his closest friend, and he remembers it.

> So the king asked, “Is there anyone left of Saul’s family to whom I can show the kindness of God?” Ziba answered, “There is still Jonathan’s son, who is lame in both feet.”

2 Samuel 9:3 · BSB

Ziba answers a question about a person with a fact about his feet. Earlier in the book we are told how it happened: as a small child, Mephibosheth was dropped as his nurse fled with him after news of Saul's and Jonathan's deaths (2 Samuel 4:4). He has been living in Lo-debar, far from the court, in another man's house (2 Samuel 9:4).

When he is brought in, he falls on his face, and David says his name before anything else: "Mephibosheth!" (2 Samuel 9:6). Then:

> “Do not be afraid,” said David, “for surely I will show you kindness for the sake of your father Jonathan. I will restore to you all the land of your grandfather Saul, and you will always eat at my table.”

2 Samuel 9:7 · BSB

Mephibosheth's answer shows how he sees himself: "What is your servant, that you should show regard for a dead dog like me?" (2 Samuel 9:8). David does not argue with him. He acts. The land is given back, servants are set to work it, and the chapter says three times that Mephibosheth will eat at the king's table (2 Samuel 9:7, 10, 11, 13). It ends with a sentence that holds two things together:

> So Mephibosheth lived in Jerusalem, because he always ate at the king’s table, and he was lame in both feet.

2 Samuel 9:13 · BSB

[read] The narrator does not say "and so his feet no longer mattered", and he does not say "but he was lame". The two facts simply stand side by side. His disability is not hidden, and it is not what decides where he sits. He eats "like one of the king’s own sons" (2 Samuel 9:11).

[read] "The kindness of God" (verse 3) is the faithful love God shows to those He has bound Himself to by promise. Here David shows it to someone who could give him nothing back. Many Christian preachers have heard in this chapter a picture of God's grace, His undeserved kindness to people who come to Him as Mephibosheth came. The New Testament does not draw that picture from this chapter, so this study offers it as an illustration, not as the chapter's meaning.

[limit] The story goes on, and it is not simple. Ziba later accuses Mephibosheth of disloyalty, and David's response is hasty (2 Samuel 16 and 19). This study does not read those chapters. They are a reminder that the people around a disabled person can speak over them, and that welcome must keep listening.

Jesus' disciples once met a man born blind and asked the question many people still ask:

> and His disciples asked Him, “Rabbi, who sinned, this man or his parents, that he was born blind?” Jesus answered, “Neither this man nor his parents sinned, but this happened so that the works of God would be displayed in him.

John 9:2–3 · BSB

Read more in *The signs and works of Jesus*.

[care] If you are the parent of a disabled child, or disabled yourself, John 9:3 is a word for you: Jesus refuses to blame the man or his parents. This site will not tell you why your situation is as it is, because Scripture does not say. It does say that you belong at the table. And it says the article XV calling, to "provide for... the helpless, and the sick", is the church's work, not yours alone.

**Consider:** How does the chapter describe Mephibosheth at its beginning and at its end? What does David do, rather than say, about Mephibosheth's view of himself?

**Reflect:** Who in your church or street is in their own Lo-debar, out of sight? What would it take to set a place for them, and then to listen to them?

## 04 No seat of honour: favouritism, race and the nations

**Main readings:** James 2:1–9; Ephesians 2:14 and Revelation 7:9 (quoted)

Hook: Two men walk into church. One is handed a cushion.

James writes to believers scattered abroad, and he pictures a scene they would have recognised.

> Suppose a man comes into your meeting wearing a gold ring and fine clothes, and a poor man in shabby clothes also comes in. If you lavish attention on the man in fine clothes and say, “Here is a seat of honor,” but say to the poor man, “You must stand” or “Sit at my feet,” have you not discriminated among yourselves and become judges with evil thoughts?

James 2:2–4 · BSB

Notice where this happens: "your meeting", the gathering of the church. Notice what the welcomers judge by: a ring and clothes, what can be seen at the door. And notice James's word for them: "judges with evil thoughts". They have set themselves up to decide a person's worth, and they have decided it by appearance.

James gives two reasons this is wrong. First, it is backwards to how God works: "Has not God chosen the poor of this world to be rich in faith and to inherit the kingdom He promised those who love Him?" (James 2:5). Second, it breaks what he calls "the royal law", the King's own law:

> If you really fulfill the royal law stated in Scripture, “Love your neighbor as yourself,” you are doing well. But if you show favoritism, you sin and are convicted by the law as transgressors.

James 2:8–9 · BSB

The command he quotes comes from the law of Moses (Leviticus 19:18). Favouritism, often called partiality, means treating people as worth more or less because of something about them that has nothing to do with right and wrong. James does not call it bad manners. He calls it sin.

[read] James's example is wealth and clothing. The same habit of judging by what can be seen at the door shows up as judging by skin, accent, nation or tribe. Applying James 2 to racism is this site's application, not a new command put in James's mouth. It is the application the Baptist Faith and Message makes when article XV says Christians "should oppose racism", and when article III says every person "of every race" has full dignity.

[limit] James 2:5 does not say that being poor saves anyone, or that the rich cannot be saved. It says God has not chosen by the world's measures, and so the church may not either.

[read] The New Testament speaks of this as part of what Christ came to do. Of Jews and Gentiles (non-Jews), Paul writes:

> For He Himself is our peace, who has made the two one and has torn down the dividing wall of hostility

Ephesians 2:14 · BSB

Read more in *The church and its calling*. And the Bible's last book pictures where it ends:

> After this I looked and saw a multitude too large to count, from every nation and tribe and people and tongue, standing before the throne and before the Lamb.

Revelation 7:9 · BSB

Every nation, standing together before Jesus, "the Lamb". No one is told to stand at the back.

[read] Southern Baptists have had to repent here, which means to turn away from a wrong and back to God. The Southern Baptist Convention was formed in 1845 in a split in which slavery was a central issue. In 1995 the convention adopted a resolution on racial reconciliation that repented of racism and of its own past in this, and asked forgiveness [check: the resolution's exact title and key wording at sbc.net]. This study names that history because James 2 asks churches to look first at their own meeting.

**Consider:** What does the welcomer in James 2 judge by? What two reasons does James give against it?

**Reflect:** When someone new walks into your gathering, what do you notice first? Who might feel told, in so many words, "You must stand"?

## 05 Justice in the gate: the poor and the foreigner

**Main readings:** Exodus 23:6–9; Deuteronomy 10:17–19, Amos 5:24 and Micah 6:8 (quoted)

Hook: A courtroom, a bribe, and a foreigner with no one to speak for him.

These verses come from the laws God gave Israel at Mount Sinai, after bringing them out of slavery in Egypt. In Jewish tradition they are part of the Torah, God's teaching, and Jewish teachers have long noticed how often the Torah returns to the stranger. One saying in the Talmud counts the warnings about wronging the foreigner in thirty-six places, "and some say forty-six" [check: Bava Metzia 59b, and the wording of the English translation used]. These are rules for the law court, which in Israel often sat at the town gate.

> You shall not deny justice to the poor in their lawsuits. Stay far away from a false accusation. Do not kill the innocent or the just, for I will not acquit the guilty.

Exodus 23:6–7 · BSB

> Do not accept a bribe, for a bribe blinds those who see and twists the words of the righteous. Do not oppress a foreign resident, since you yourselves know how it feels to be foreigners; for you were foreigners in the land of Egypt.

Exodus 23:8–9 · BSB

Look at the people in the room. The poor man in a lawsuit, who cannot pay for help. The innocent, who can be put to death by a false word. The foreign resident, who does not belong to any family in town. Each is someone the court can most easily wrong. And look at what is most likely to wrong them: a lie, and money. A bribe, the law says, "blinds those who see". It makes sharp people stop seeing.

Verse 9 gives Israel a reason from its own story: "you yourselves know how it feels to be foreigners". God's people are to remember being on the bottom, and act from that memory.

[limit] This law does not tell judges to side with the poor whatever the facts. A few verses earlier it says not to favour a poor man in his lawsuit either (Exodus 23:3). The point is the same as James's: justice that cannot be bought and does not look at who you are. That cuts both ways.

[read] The command rests on what God Himself is like. Deuteronomy says so:

> For the LORD your God is God of gods and Lord of lords, the great, mighty, and awesome God, showing no partiality and accepting no bribe. He executes justice for the fatherless and widow, and He loves the foreigner, giving him food and clothing. So you also must love the foreigner, since you yourselves were foreigners in the land of Egypt.

Deuteronomy 10:17–19 · BSB

Read more in *God: who He is and what He does*. The prophets later held Israel to this. Amos cried out, "let justice roll on like a river, and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream" (Amos 5:24 · BSB). Micah summed it up:

> He has shown you, O man, what is good. And what does the LORD require of you but to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God?

Micah 6:8 · BSB

Read more in *Prophets in their times*.

[read] How the laws of Moses apply to Christians is a question Baptists answer in more than one way, and *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice* shows the views. On this point they agree, because the New Testament repeats it: God shows no partiality, and His people may not either. Article XV asks Christians to "provide for the orphaned, the needy, the abused, the aged, the helpless, and the sick". How a country should write its immigration laws is a further question that Scripture does not settle verse by verse, and this study does not settle it. The command to love the foreigner who lives among you is not in doubt. [check: link *The Christian and the state* if it treats immigration]

[care] If you are poor, or a foreigner, and you have been treated unjustly, this passage is on your side. Telling your story to someone who can help, or taking it to the courts, is not a failure of faith.

**Consider:** Which people does Exodus 23:6–9 name? What two things does it warn will wrong them?

**Reflect:** Where do you have a little power over someone who has less: an employee, a tenant, a neighbour from another country? What would justice that cannot be bought look like there?

## 06 Open your mouth: the voiceless and the dying

**Main readings:** Proverbs 31:8–9; Luke 10:33–37 (quoted; read in full in *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice*)

Hook: A queen mother tells her son what a king's mouth is for.

The last chapter of Proverbs opens with words King Lemuel's mother taught him (Proverbs 31:1). She warns him away from things that dull a ruler's judgement, and then she tells him what his voice is for.

> Open your mouth for those with no voice, for the cause of all the dispossessed. Open your mouth, judge righteously, and defend the cause of the poor and needy.

Proverbs 31:8–9 · BSB

"Those with no voice" are people who cannot speak up for themselves in the place where things are decided. "The dispossessed" are those who have lost what they had. A king is not to wait until they find a way to be heard. He is to speak for them, and to "judge righteously", which means to judge rightly and fairly.

[read] The command was first spoken to a king. Its principle reaches anyone with a voice where it counts. This site's application, marked as one: the unborn child has no voice. Neither does the person with a severe disability who cannot speak, or the patient at the end of life who can no longer say what they want. Speaking for them is part of what article XV means by contending for life "from conception to natural death".

[read] Speaking for the dying includes protecting them from being hurried out of life. The Baptist Faith and Message's phrase "natural death" rules out deliberately ending a life, whether by assisted suicide or by euthanasia, even when it is offered as kindness. In 2026 the convention adopted a resolution against assisted suicide [check: title and wording]. This site holds that view.

[read] Speaking for the dying also includes protecting them from being kept from death at any cost. Allowing natural death is not killing. When treatment can no longer heal, and only lengthens suffering, a patient or family may rightly stop it. They may choose hospice care, which looks after comfort, pain and the whole person rather than trying to cure. They may sign a do-not-resuscitate order, a doctor's instruction not to attempt to restart the heart if it stops. None of these sets out to end a life. Each accepts that a life is ending, and cares for the person while it does. Psalm 139:16 says "all my days were written in Your book". Those days are not ours to cut short, and we are not required to fight for every possible hour at any cost.

[source:ccc] Roman Catholic teaching draws a similar line in its own words. The Catechism of the Catholic Church, paragraph 2278, says that "Discontinuing medical procedures that are burdensome, dangerous, extraordinary, or disproportionate to the expected outcome can be legitimate", and that in this "one does not will to cause death; one's inability to impede it is merely accepted" [check: wording against the Vatican's English text]. This is Catholic teaching, shown as theirs. On this distinction Baptists and Catholics agree.

[care] These decisions belong with the patient, the family, the doctors and, where it helps, a pastor. This site does not give medical advice about any particular treatment. If you are caring for someone who is dying, *Grief and dying* walks that road with you. If you are the one who is ill, *What Scripture promises the sick* reads what God does and does not promise.

[care] Some people who are very ill, or who feel they have become a burden, begin to think about ending their life. If that is you, please tell someone today, and use one of the lines at the top of this page. You are not a burden God regrets. These feelings can change, and people who have felt them do find their way through.

Jesus once told a story to a man who wanted to know who counted as his neighbour. The priest and the Levite passed by the beaten man on the road. Then:

> But when a Samaritan on a journey came upon him, he looked at him and had compassion.

Luke 10:33 · BSB

At the end Jesus asks which one "was a neighbor", and the lawyer answers, "The one who showed him mercy" (Luke 10:36–37 · BSB). Read more in *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice*. The man on the road could not speak for himself either. Someone stopped.

**Consider:** Whom does Lemuel's mother tell him to speak for? What is the difference between ending a life and allowing it to end?

**Reflect:** Who near you has no voice where decisions about them are made? What could you say, or ask, on their behalf this month?

## Question: Can God forgive me for an abortion?

Text: Psalm 139:13

[care] If this is your question, you are not reading this page by accident. Many people carry it for years, alone. You do not have to.

[read] Yes. The gospel, the good news about Jesus, is that God forgives sinners who turn to Him through Jesus Christ, who died for sins and rose again. Abortion takes a human life, and Scripture and this site say so plainly. Scripture also says there is no sin that Christ's death cannot cover for the one who trusts Him. Those who come to God are told to repent, which means to turn from sin and toward God, and to believe. They are not told to earn their way back. The Baptist Faith and Message's article IV (salvation) speaks of justification, which is God's declaring a sinner right with Him by grace, His undeserved kindness, through faith in Christ. It does not list sins too great for that.

[read] This is true for a mother, a father, a grandparent who pressed for it, a doctor or nurse, a friend who drove someone to the clinic. It is true for those who felt they had no choice, and for those who chose freely. Grief may come with forgiveness, and that grief is not a sign that the forgiveness failed. David, who wrote Psalm 139, was himself forgiven for taking a life (2 Samuel 12:13).

[care] You do not have to work through this alone. The site's help page lists groups that walk with people after abortion, without condemnation [check: link to the help page's after-abortion group]. A pastor can also pray with you. *The good news: how a person is made right with God* explains more about forgiveness.

## Question: What about fertility treatment?

Text: Psalm 139:16

[care] If you and your spouse have longed for a child and none has come, Scripture sees you. Hannah wept and prayed in the house of the LORD for a son (1 Samuel 1). Rachel cried out to Jacob for children (Genesis 30:1). The Bible does not call their longing selfish. This site will not promise you a child, because Scripture does not make that promise to every couple. It does say that God hears.

[read] Many treatments for infertility raise no question of life at all: treating an illness, correcting a hormone problem, surgery. In vitro fertilisation (IVF), where eggs are fertilised outside the body, does raise questions, because it often creates more embryos than are placed in the womb. An embryo is a human life at its earliest stage. If life is God's from conception, as Psalm 139 and article XV say, then what happens to embryos that are frozen, discarded or used for research is not a small matter.

[read] At its June 2024 meeting in Indianapolis, the Southern Baptist Convention adopted a resolution on reproductive technologies and the dignity of the human embryo [check: exact title, likely "On the Ethical Realities of Reproductive Technologies and the Dignity of the Human Embryo"]. It affirmed the value of every human being from the embryonic stage and expressed sympathy for couples facing infertility. It called on Southern Baptists to use reproductive technologies only in ways that honour that value, with particular concern about how many embryos are created and what becomes of them [check: wording of the resolution's calls, and whether it addressed adoption of frozen embryos]. A resolution is not part of the confession. It does show where the convention stands.

[care] If you have already been through IVF and have embryos stored, or are unsure what was done, do not carry that alone either. A pastor, and doctors who share your convictions, can help you think through what to do now. If something was done that you now regret, the forgiveness in the question above is for you too. This site does not give medical advice about any treatment.

## Question: Does treating someone as equal mean ignoring what is wrong?

Text: James 2:9

[read] No. James forbids favouritism by wealth and appearance, and in the same letter he calls people to turn from sin. Treating every person as made in God's image means taking their worth seriously. It does not mean pretending that every choice they make is good. Exodus 23 asks for a court that cannot be bought, not one that cannot see. The same dignity that forbids contempt also asks us to tell one another the truth, gently.

## Close: Who is in the room?

Way: Here is the habit to carry to the next passage. When Scripture tells you how to treat people, look around the scene for the one who cannot speak: the child in the womb, the man from Lo-debar, the poor man asked to stand, the foreigner in the lawsuit, the patient at the bedside. Then ask what the passage asks you to do for them. The answer will often be small and costly: a seat, a meal, a word spoken in the right room, a refusal to be bought.

Close: David asked, "Is there anyone left?" and went looking. God knit each of us in secret before anyone else could see us, and He saw. If you have never trusted Christ, start there. The God who made you in His image sent His Son to die and rise for people who could give Him nothing back. Turn to Him and trust Him as Lord. Then go and set a place at the table.
