# Every life in God's image (62): passage notes

Study folder: christian-life/every-life-in-gods-image. Doctrinal anchor: Baptist Faith and Message 2000, article XV (the Christian and the social order), with article III (man) and article IV (salvation). Builds on *Humanity, sin and renewal* (29), which owns Genesis 1:26–31 and the image-of-God teaching.

## Owned passages

### Psalm 139:13–16

**Context.** A psalm of David (by its heading), prayer addressed to God throughout. Verses 1–12 speak of God's knowledge and presence everywhere. Verses 13–16 turn to the psalmist's formation before birth. Verses 17–24 move to wonder at God's thoughts and then to a prayer against the wicked and for searching. The study walks 13–16 only.

**Main readings.**
- *Jewish reading.* Read as the psalmist's praise of God as Maker and as One who knows him wholly. The image of God shaping a person in the womb also appears in Job 10:8–12 (not quoted).
- *Christian pro-life reading (the site's view).* The psalmist speaks in the first person of himself in the womb. That continuity of the person, and God's sight and purpose resting on the unborn, support the Baptist Faith and Message's "from conception". Held by Southern Baptists generally and stated in article XV.
- *Reading that the poetry does not settle when personhood begins.* Some Christian writers say a psalm of praise should not be used to fix a point in development. The study answers by agreeing it is poetry, while holding that poetry can still teach truth. - *Verse 15, "the depths of the earth".* Commonly read as a figure for the hidden place of the womb, paralleling verse 13. [check: a named commentator, e.g. Calvin on the Psalms, before citing any]
- *Verse 16, "unformed body".* The Hebrew word (golem) occurs only here in the Hebrew Bible. [check: confirm]

### 2 Samuel 9

**Context.** After David's kingdom is established (2 Samuel 8), he seeks out Saul's house for Jonathan's sake. This recalls the covenant between David and Jonathan, a binding agreement made with promises (1 Samuel 20:14–17, 42; not quoted). Mephibosheth's injury is told in 2 Samuel 4:4. The story continues in 2 Samuel 16:1–4 (Ziba's accusation) and 19:24–30 (Mephibosheth's answer and David's divided ruling). None of these is quoted.

**Main readings.**
- *Covenant faithfulness.* "The kindness of God" (9:3) is read as covenant loyalty (Hebrew hesed) kept beyond the death of the one it was promised to. Widely held across commentators.
- *Political reading.* Some read David's kindness as also keeping a possible rival heir under watch at court. The study does not adopt or argue this reading. [check: whether to mention it, with a named source]
- *Grace illustration.* Popular in Christian, including Baptist, preaching: Mephibosheth as a picture of the sinner welcomed by grace. The New Testament does not draw this, so the study labels it an illustration. [check: a named preacher, e.g. Spurgeon, if a source is to be cited; none is cited at present]
- *Disability reading.* The study's own emphasis: the closing verse sets "ate at the king's table" beside "lame in both feet" without letting either cancel the other.
- "Lo-debar" is often explained as meaning "no pasture". [check: before adding it; not used in content]

**Links.** *Rulers and their choices* (39), which owns 2 Samuel 11–12; *The signs and works of Jesus* (18), for John 9; *What Scripture promises the sick* (45).

### James 2:1–9

**Context.** James writes to believers "scattered" (James 1:1). Chapter 1 ends on pure religion as care for orphans and widows (1:27). 2:1–13 addresses favouritism in the gathering. 2:14–26 turns to faith and works. The study walks 2:1–9 and refers to 2:10–13 only implicitly.

**Main readings.**
- *Favouritism by wealth.* The plain sense. The setting is the gathering (literally "synagogue", 2:2). [check: confirm the Greek term before stating it in content; not used there]
- *Application to race and nationality.* The site's application, labelled as such, following article XV ("oppose racism") and article III ("every race").
- *2:5, God choosing the poor.* Read by most as God's choice not following the world's ranking. It does not mean poverty saves. The study states that limit.
- *"The royal law"* (2:8). Read as the King's law (Christ's), or as the supreme law, quoting Leviticus 19:18. Both readings are held. The study uses "the King's own law" without choosing between them closely.

**Links.** *The church and its calling* (28), for Ephesians 2:14; *Genesis 1–11: the questions readers bring* (50), for Revelation 7:9; *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice* (47), for Leviticus 19:18 and Luke 10; *Money, possessions and generosity* (63).

### Exodus 23:6–9

**Context.** Part of the Book of the Covenant (Exodus 20:22–23:33), given at Sinai and ratified in Exodus 24 (*Covenants and promises*, 26, owns 24:1–11). 23:1–3 forbid false witness and favouring the poor in court. 23:6–9 forbid denying justice to the poor, killing the innocent, bribery and oppressing the foreigner.

**Main readings.**
- *Jewish reading.* Torah law for Israel's courts. Rabbinic tradition stresses the repeated command concerning the ger (resident foreigner). The study cites a Talmudic saying. [check: Bava Metzia 59b, "thirty-six places, and some say forty-six", and the English translation used]
- *Christian readings of how Mosaic law applies.* These are the three-kinds-of-law reading, new covenant theology / progressive covenantalism, and dispensational readings, all held by Baptists. The study does not choose. It notes that the principle of impartial justice and love for the foreigner is repeated in the New Testament.
- *23:7, "Do not kill the innocent".* Read in context as judicial (no death sentence on a false charge). The study applies it broadly to protecting innocent life, as an application.

**Links.** *God: who He is and what He does* (21), for Deuteronomy 10:17–19; *Prophets in their times* (38), for Amos 5:24 and Micah 6:8; *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice* (47); *The Christian and the state* (64).

### Proverbs 31:8–9

**Context.** "The words of King Lemuel, the oracle his mother taught him" (31:1, not quoted). Verses 2–9 warn against women who ruin kings and against wine and strong drink, then commend speaking for the voiceless. 31:10–31 (the woman of strength) follows and belongs to *The wisdom books* (65).

**Main readings.**
- *Royal duty.* First addressed to a king. Read as the ruler's obligation to uphold justice for those unable to plead their own cause. Held across Jewish and Christian readers.
- *Wider application.* Christians commonly extend it to anyone with influence. The site applies it to the unborn, the disabled and the dying, labelled as application.
- "Those with no voice" (Hebrew, literally "the mute"). [check: confirm before stating it in content; not used there]

**Links.** *The wisdom books* (65); *Grief and dying* (57); *What Scripture promises the sick* (45).
