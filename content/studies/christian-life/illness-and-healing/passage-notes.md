**The way of reading taught.** Three questions repeated in every part: is this a promise, a prayer or a story; now, or
not yet; who is speaking, and does God agree? They come from the texts themselves: Psalm 103:3 is praise, 3 John 1:2 a
greeting, Hezekiah's healing a story; Romans 8:23 and Revelation 21:4 put the body's full healing at the resurrection;
Job's friends and the healing tract speak confidently and are contradicted by Scripture (Job 42:7).

## Context, readings and limits

1. **2 Timothy 4:20.** A line in Paul's closing news, with no explanation. The draft uses it as the opening because it
   is the plainest case of an apostle through whom God healed others (Acts 19:11–12; 28:8–9) leaving a friend ill. The
   draft says no more about why than the verse says.
2. **Job, read for his sickness only.** Job 1:1 (blameless), 2:7 (the boils), 2:13 (the friends' silence), 13:15 (his
   hope) and 42:7 (God's verdict on the friends). The friends' argument is given as a paraphrase and marked as one; it
   summarises the line taken by Eliphaz, Bildad and Zophar across the dialogues (for example Job 4:7–8; 8:4–6; 11:13–15,
   read for this note) without quoting them. Job 13:15 has a Berean footnote ("Or I have no other hope"); the draft
   quotes the main text. The limit is stated in the draft: Job does not say sickness is never linked to sin; it forbids
   reading another person's sin from their sickness, as John 9:3 does.
3. **The psalms of sickness.** Psalm 6 and Psalm 38 open with the same line. Psalm 38:3–5 and 41:4 connect the
   psalmist's illness with his own sin; the draft treats both as confessions a person makes about himself, not a rule
   for judging others. Psalm 41:1–3 (the LORD sustains the one who cares for the poor on his sickbed) was in the first
   version as an example of a blessing that is not a contract; it was cut for length and is listed below. Psalm 102:25–27
   is quoted in Hebrews 1:10–12 as spoken "about the Son" (Hebrews 1:8), shown under shared rule T3 (Scripture
   interpreting Scripture). Psalm 88 is the only one of the five that ends with no turn to relief; it is quoted briefly
   (88:1, 88:18), as the correction asks.
4. **Hezekiah (Isaiah 38).** The prophet's word "you will not recover" (38:1) is reversed after prayer (38:5). Isaiah
   38:21 places the fig poultice after the psalm, as the instruction Isaiah "had said"; 2 Kings 20:7 gives it in its
   order in the story. The draft says only that God healed the king and a paste of figs was applied, without claiming
   the figs did the healing or that they did not. Isaiah 39 (the Babylonian envoys) was cut for length from the reader's
   text; 2 Kings 20:21 (his death) stays, to make the point that healing in this life is not the end of the story.
5. **Medicine.** 1 Timothy 5:23 (a remedy for Timothy's stomach) and Colossians 4:14 ("Luke, the beloved physician")
   are read in their own sentences. The draft's plain statement, "Going to the doctor is not a lack of faith, and praying
   is not a substitute for treatment", is this site's application, drawn from those verses and from Isaiah 38:21, and
   agrees with drafts 03, 04, 14 and 18, which already tell readers to keep seeing their doctor. Mark 5:26 (the woman
   who spent everything on physicians and grew worse) is quoted in part 05 as part of her story, not as a judgment on
   medicine.
6. **The thorn (2 Corinthians 12:7–10).** What the thorn was is not stated; the draft does not guess. Galatians 4:13
   (Paul first preached in Galatia "because of an illness") is sometimes linked to it; the draft does not draw the link.
   Study 04 walks the passage under "What if the answer is no?", with Calvin; this study links there.
7. **Epaphroditus (Philippians 2:25–30).** Paul attributes the recovery to God's mercy (2:27) and does not say he
   healed him. The draft's point ("recovery is a gift, not a wage") is drawn from the word "mercy".
8. **Mark 5:25–34.** Read only for the faith question the study now owns; the healing itself is on the Miracles page and
   in Studies 14 and 18. The draft keeps three observations from the text: Jesus says it to her, of her (5:34); the power
   was His (5:30); her faith was trembling (5:33). It sets beside it Mark 9:24 (a father's mixed faith, his son healed),
   the thorn, and Mark 14:36 (Jesus' own prayer in Gethsemane, "Take this cup from Me", not granted). Mark 6:5–6
   (unbelief at Nazareth) is acknowledged so that the draft does not say faith does not matter; Study 18 has a section
   on it. The sentence "The question to ask is not 'How much do I believe?' but 'Whom do I trust?'" is marked in the
   draft as this site's reading.
9. **James 5:13–18.** The sick person calls the elders; the elders pray and anoint; the Lord raises up. "If he has
   sinned" (5:15) is conditional, which the draft uses to say not every sickness comes from sin. "Will restore" is
   treated as a real promise, granted "as often and as far as" God knows is good (Calvin), and finally kept at the
   resurrection; the draft takes care not to say it means every illness ends now, as Study 18 also says. "Elders" is
   explained as the church's pastors, following Article VI of the Baptist Faith and Message (pastor, elder and overseer
   one office); Calvin read the word more widely, to include lay elders, and the draft does not raise the point.
10. **The oil.** Three readings are shown without choosing: medicine (one of the commentary views Criswell reports);
    a sign tied to the gift of healing in the apostles' day (Calvin); and the Roman Catholic sacrament, labelled as its
    teaching. The draft states the Baptist position only as far as the confession does: two ordinances, baptism and the
    Lord's Supper (Article VII). It does not say whether Baptist elders should use oil; that is left open.
11. **Isaiah 53:4–5, Matthew 8:16–17 and 1 Peter 2:24.** The textual answer in part 07: Matthew applies Isaiah 53:4 to
    Jesus' healings on an evening in Capernaum, before the cross; Peter applies 53:5 to sins borne "so that we might die
    to sin and live to righteousness". Both readings are accepted as true; the draft draws from them that the cross does
    win the body's healing (Article IV, "the redemption of the whole man"; glorification) and that the time of its full
    gift is the resurrection (Romans 8:23; 2 Corinthians 5:4–5; Revelation 21:4). The Berean has "carried our sorrows"
    at Isaiah 53:4 and "carried our diseases" in Matthew 8:17; the draft quotes each in its place and does not discuss
    the difference (the Berean's own footnote gives the Greek Old Testament's different wording). Study 41, Christ in the
    Old Testament, already notes that Matthew reads Isaiah 53 "for His ministry as well as His death".
12. **3 John 1:2.** A greeting ("I pray that ..."). The draft says it is sometimes read as a promise of health and
    wealth; **no prosperity teacher's own text was read for that statement** (see below), so it is kept general and
    names no one.
13. **Romans 8:18–26 and 2 Corinthians 4:16–5:5.** Studies 29 and 30 walk Romans 8 at length. This study reads them only for one point: the believers who groan are the ones who have the
    Spirit, so groaning in a failing body is not a failure of faith.
14. **Revelation 21:3–5.** Study 30 already quotes 21:4 in its account of the new creation. Here it closes the study as
    the promise every sick believer has, resting on belonging to God (21:3). The Berean footnote on 21:4 notes the
    quotation of Isaiah 25:8.

## Where Christians differ: the views shown, and who holds them

**Whether gifts of healing continue** (part 06). The confession says the Spirit "bestows the spiritual gifts" (Article
II) and does not say which continue; the [doctrinal foundation](../../../../../DOCTRINAL-FOUNDATION.md) lists this as a
point where Baptists differ. The draft shows two views and does not choose:

| View | Holder quoted in the draft | Where read | Baptist? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| The gift of healing was a sign gift for the apostles' time and has ceased; God still heals in answer to prayer | W. A. Criswell, "The Gifts of Healing", 1966; John Calvin on James 5:14 | Local copies, read directly | Criswell yes (First Baptist Church, Dallas; twice president of the Southern Baptist Convention, 1968–1970 — this office is from general knowledge, not from a source read today, and is not stated in the draft). Calvin no (Reformed) |
| Gifts of healings continue in the church today | John Piper, sermon "Gifts of Healings and Workings of Miracles", 4 March 1990 | Desiring God page, local copy | Yes: then pastor of Bethlehem Baptist Church, Minneapolis (Converge, formerly the Baptist General Conference; not Southern Baptist) |

Both views agree on what the draft says they agree on: Criswell, "God heals today" (1966); Piper, that only some have the
gift (1 Corinthians 12:29–30, in his 2022 answer below) and that the greatest need is "to care that people are sick".

**The claim that healing is guaranteed in the atonement** (part 07) is not a difference among Baptists that the site
leaves open. The confession teaches that salvation includes "the redemption of the whole man" and that glorification is
its culmination; it nowhere says believers are owed physical healing now. The [doctrinal
foundation](../../../../../DOCTRINAL-FOUNDATION.md) rules out prosperity teaching. So the site's voice answers the claim
from the text, after stating it fairly. One nuance is kept on purpose: Criswell, a Southern Baptist, said the atonement
includes "the healing of our bodies" while also saying "God does not always heal". The draft uses this to show that the
real question is when, not whether.

**Labelling.** The correction asks for the claim to be labelled as "Word of Faith and some Pentecostal teaching". The
draft does so in one sentence and names no teacher. **No Word of Faith or Pentecostal text was read for this draft**:
the claim is stated from the texts its holders use (Isaiah 53:5; Matthew 8:16–17; 1 Peter 2:24; 3 John 1:2) and from
the 1966 tract Criswell read aloud. The label rests on the owner's reviewed study plan, not on a source read here.
Candidate primary sources, **located by name only and not read**: the Assemblies of God *Statement of Fundamental
Truths* (its article on divine healing); A. J. Gordon, *The Ministry of Healing* (1882), by a Boston Baptist pastor of
the healing movement; A. B. Simpson, *The Gospel of Healing* (1885); F. F. Bosworth, *Christ the Healer* (1924); and the
writings of Kenneth Hagin and Kenneth Copeland for the Word of Faith movement. None is held in the local library (a
filename search found none). Reading at least one Pentecostal statement and one Word of Faith text directly, and
quoting each in its own words, would make part 07 fairer; see the open questions.

## Doctrine

- Article IV (salvation): "the redemption of the whole man"; glorification "the culmination of salvation". The
  ground for saying the cross wins the body's healing, given in full at the resurrection.
- Article X (last things): "The righteous in their resurrected and glorified bodies will receive their reward" (local
  text line 345). Behind part 07; not quoted in the reader's text.
- Article II (the Holy Spirit): bestows the spiritual gifts, without saying which continue. Part 06 shows the two views
  and does not choose.
- Article VI (the church): pastor, elder and overseer one office; used for the explanation of "elders" in part 06.
- Article VII (baptism and the Lord's Supper): two ordinances. The Catholic sacrament is labelled as Catholic teaching.
- Prosperity teaching: the doctrinal foundation records that the site has no prosperity teaching (Article XIII,
  stewardship, never wealth as the reward of faith). Part 07 says so in the site's voice.
- Not taken up: whether God sends illness as discipline (1 Corinthians 11:30; Hebrews 12:5–11). Criswell's 1974 sermon
  names it as one possibility among several. The draft only says sickness may be connected to sin (Psalm 38; James
  5:15) and that no one may read it in another; a fuller treatment would need care and may belong in the open questions.

## Open editorial questions

1. **Length.** About 3,430 words raw (headings, references and block quotations included), about 3,100 without the
   block quotations: above the 2,000–2,600 aim. The brief's eleven reading groups and the correction's added part (the
   faith question) are the reason. First cuts if the design pass needs room: the Psalm 102 and Hebrews paragraph (part
   02), the oil paragraph (part 06, which could become a short note), and the Piper summary in part 07.
2. **A Pentecostal and a Word of Faith voice in their own words.** Part 07 states the claim fairly from its texts but
   quotes no holder of it. Before publishing, read at least the Assemblies of God statement on divine healing and one
   Word of Faith text, and quote each, or confirm the owner is content with the claim stated without a named holder.
3. **A Southern Baptist voice for the continuing view.** The continuationist holder quoted is Piper (Converge). A
   Southern Baptist who holds it would balance Criswell; none was found in the local library in the time given.
4. **Illness as discipline.** Should the study say more about 1 Corinthians 11:30 and Hebrews 12, or leave the draft's
   one careful line? Pastorally it is easy to misuse.
5. **Psalm 41:1–3.** Cut for length; it is the clearest example of a psalm blessing that is not a contract. Restore it if
   part 02 needs a third example for the first question.
6. **Oil in Baptist practice.** The draft does not say whether Baptist elders should anoint with oil. Southern Baptist
   churches differ in practice, but no source for that was read, so the draft says only "With oil or without, the
   command is to pray."
7. **Studies 14 and 18.** They still carry their own answers to the Mark 5:34 question. When those drafts are edited
   under the study plan, their sections can shrink to a link to part 05 here.
