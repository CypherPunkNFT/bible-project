## Direction

Teach one habit: **before you hold a verse as God's promise to you in your illness, ask what kind of word it is, when it is kept, and who is speaking.** These are the study's own three questions, and the page is built so the reader uses them, not only reads them.

The shape comes from the study's own material (stage 2 of the study prompt, "a question real readers bring"). The study does not walk Jesus' healings. It reads the sick people Scripture leaves in the story, from Job to Paul's friends, and Scripture records what happened to each without smoothing it: Publius's father healed through Paul's hands, Trophimus left sick by the same Paul. So the page sets those people out as a register: what was wrong, what was prayed or said, and what the text says happened, and no more. Four of the ten recover in the text. The other six are left ill, kept in their weakness, or not told. Beneath every row runs one line that holds for all who are His: the body's redemption and every tear wiped away (Romans 8:23; Revelation 21:4).

One direction was built, because the text already existed.

## The page

- **Opening.** The standard heading: study trail (Study / Living the Christian Life / What Scripture promises the sick), the concepts in gold capitals (Promise · Prayer · Story · Now and not yet), the title *What Scripture* in ink with *promises the sick* in gold italic, and the lead: Trophimus left sick in Miletus by the Paul through whom God healed others. Beside it, in a faint circle and dotted orbit, a bedside at night: a window with a thin moon and a faint line of dawn on the horizon, the edge of a bed with a folded blanket, a small table with an oil lamp whose flame moves slowly, a flask of oil (James 5:14), a dish of pressed figs (Isaiah 38:21) and a folded letter. No one is drawn in the bed, and no face or figure of God or of Christ. The motto is Isaiah 38:5, "I have heard your prayer; / I have seen your tears.", checked in that verse. Under it, four counts computed from the page's own lists, each with what it means, and a row of links to every part.
- **What this study asks.** The second paragraph of the opening, then the three questions set as three lines joined by a fine rule, each with its own small mark (a seal for a promise, a horizon for now or not yet, a quotation mark for who is speaking), then the "With care" note that this is not medical advice.
- **00 The sick Scripture leaves in the story (the signature).** Ten people, one row each, in the order the study reads them: Publius's father, Job, the psalmist of Psalm 6, the psalmist of Psalm 88, Hezekiah, Timothy, Paul and his thorn, Epaphroditus, Trophimus, and the woman who touched Jesus' cloak. Each row is three phrases on one fine line, each in its verse's own words: what was wrong, what was prayed or said, and what the text says happened. The end of the line carries a mark whose shape says the outcome (a full dot for recovered, a ring for kept or left, a dashed ring for not told) and a word saying it, so colour is never the only carrier. Two large choices above the rows, with counts, light one group or the other: "Recovered in the text · 4" and "Left ill, kept, or not told · 6"; a third shows all ten. Each row links to the part that reads it. Under the rows, one wide line marked "Not yet": Romans 8:23 and Revelation 21:4, the promise "without conditions", for all who are His. A note says this is the study's selection, not every sick person in Scripture.
- **Test the words offered to the sick.** Nine sayings a sick believer is likely to be handed, Scripture and not Scripture: Psalm 103:3, Isaiah 53:5, Mark 5:34, James 5:15, 2 Corinthians 12:9, 3 John 1:2, Revelation 21:4, the argument of Job's friends (in the study's paraphrase, marked as one) and the 1966 tract Criswell read. Each is a line in serif with its three answers on a rule beneath: what kind of word it is, when it is kept, and who is speaking and whether God agrees. Every answer is this study's reading, labelled as such, and names the part that argues it.
- **01–07 The parts.** Each part opens across the full width: a large number, the title, the hook in italic, the main readings and a small drawing. The reading runs in one column; each part's figure breaks out to the full width where the text calls for it. Passages print in full; "With care" and "Read differently" notes are marked; Consider and Reflect close each part side by side.
  - 01 Job. No figure: Job 2:13 printed in full, the friends' silence.
  - 02 Where each prayer ends. Psalms 6, 38, 41, 102 and 88, each as a row of ticks, one per verse (counted from the Berean). The verse the study quotes is marked; the last verse is given in its own words. Psalm 88 ends in the dark, and the page says so in words.
  - 03 Hezekiah, in five steps: "you will not recover" (Isaiah 38:1), "Hezekiah wept bitterly" (38:3), "I will add fifteen years to your life" (38:5), the figs on the boil (38:21), "Hezekiah rested with his fathers" (2 Kings 20:21), with the fifteen years drawn as a span.
  - 04 Side by side, as the part's own question asks: "But God had mercy on him" (Philippians 2:27) and "Trophimus I left sick in Miletus" (2 Timothy 4:20), joined by one rule, with "My grace is sufficient for you" (2 Corinthians 12:9) beneath.
  - 05 Faith is not a measure. Four prayers in their own words, each with what followed: the woman's trembling faith and her healing; the father's "help my unbelief" and his son standing up; Paul's three pleas and the answer of grace; and Jesus' "not what I will" in the garden, followed by "And they crucified Him".
  - 06 Who acts in James 5:14–16: the sick person calls, the elders pray and anoint, the church confesses and prays, the Lord raises up, each verb in the verse's words. Then the two views on gifts of healing side by side under "Shown, not chosen", each with its holder.
  - 07 One song, two readings: Isaiah 53:4 taken by Matthew 8:17 to an evening of healings before the cross, and Isaiah 53:5 taken by 1 Peter 2:24 to sins borne on the tree. Then the two claims built on the song, each answered from the text as the study answers it.
- **Close.** "What God promises, and what He does not" as two columns on fine rules: the three things He does not promise, in the study's words, and the six He does, each with its verse. Then the question for the reader, "Read the passage again before you answer.", the six promises printed in full, the bedside again with the lamp out and the dawn up, the sources and the way back to Living the Christian Life.
- **Every reference** opens its verses in a sheet from the right, in the Berean, and puts the reference in the address.

## Colour

The Prophets' blue. The page's drawing and motto come from Isaiah 38 (Hezekiah's sickbed and the word "I have heard your prayer"), and the hinge of the last part is Isaiah 53. The site's gold marks the care notes and the heading's turn. Outcomes in the register are told by shape and word, never by colour alone.

## Unlike its neighbours

Christian Life pages already use a fold and a thread (A new creation), doors (Bearing witness), rooms (Faith in daily life), dark layers (Faith under pressure), prayers read upward in columns (Prayer in the letters), the Psalter's strokes (The book of Psalms) and a table (Life together). This page uses a register of the sick on fine lines and one horizon beneath them. The page most likely to be confused with it is The hope to come, which also speaks of "now" and "not yet"; the README says why it is not the same.

## Doctrine and care

The site's voice follows the Baptist Faith and Message 2000: salvation "involves the redemption of the whole man" and "Glorification is the culmination of salvation" (Article IV), so the page places the body's full healing at the resurrection and receives healing now as mercy God gives as He wills. Two ordinances only (Article VII); the Roman Catholic reading of the oil is shown as its own. Whether gifts of healing continue is a point where Baptists differ: both views are shown with their holders, and the page does not choose. The claim that health is guaranteed now is answered from the text, as the study answers it. No prosperity teaching. The page never says or implies that a reader is still ill because their faith is too small, and it repeats the study's word that nothing here replaces a doctor. No drawn face or figure of God, of Christ or of the Father.
