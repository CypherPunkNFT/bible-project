# What Scripture promises the sick

**Study 45 · Christian Life · First content draft · 2026-11-05**

Near the end of his last letter, Paul passes on news of his friends. Then, without apology: "Trophimus I left sick in Miletus" (2 Timothy 4:20 · BSB). God had healed the sick through Paul's hands (Acts 28:8). This time Paul left his friend ill, and wrote it down.

This study asks one question: what does God promise a believer who is ill, and what does He not promise? It does not walk through Jesus' healings; the [Miracles page](/study/miracles) lists all 35 of His miracles. It reads the sick whom Scripture leaves in the story, from Job to Paul's friends. The site writes in agreement with the Baptist Faith and Message 2000, the statement of faith of the Southern Baptist Convention. Where Christians differ, on whether gifts of healing continue today, this study shows the views and does not choose.

Carry three questions through every part:

1. **Is this a promise, a prayer or a story?** "He who forgives all your iniquities and heals all your diseases" (Psalm 103:3 · BSB) is praise. A story tells what God did once, not what He must do every time.
2. **Now, or not yet?** Some promises are kept in this life, some when Christ returns and raises the dead.
3. **Who is speaking, and does God agree?** Some confident speeches in Scripture are ones God says were wrong.

[care] If you are ill now, read slowly. This study is not medical advice. Nothing in it tells you to stop seeing your doctor; part 03 shows Scripture putting prayer and medicine side by side.

## 01 Job: a sick man no one could explain

**Main readings:** Job 1:1; 2:7–13; 13:15; 42:7.

The reader is told why Job suffers. Job never is.

Job is "blameless and upright, fearing God and shunning evil" (Job 1:1 · BSB). In a scene in heaven he never sees, Satan is allowed to strike him "with terrible boils from the soles of his feet to the crown of his head" (Job 2:7 · BSB). His friends come, and at first they do the wisest thing in the book:

> Then they sat on the ground with him for seven days and seven nights, but no one spoke a word to him because they saw how intense his suffering was.

Job 2:13 · BSB

Then they talk. Their argument comes down to this (paraphrase): God is just, so a man who suffers this much must have sinned. Job holds on to God without accepting it: "Though He slay me, I will hope in Him" (Job 13:15 · BSB). At the end God tells the friends, "you have not spoken about Me accurately, as My servant Job has" (Job 42:7 · BSB).

### Where the passage stops

Job does not teach that sickness is never connected to sin (part 02 shows a man who says his is). It teaches that no one can read another person's sin from their sickness. Jesus said of a man born blind, "Neither this man nor his parents sinned" (John 9:3 · BSB). Nor does Job explain your illness: in it you stand where Job stood, not where the reader sat. (The whole book, and God's answer from the whirlwind, belong to a planned study of the wisdom books.)

**Consider:** The friends said many true things about God and were still wrong about Job. Where did they go wrong?

**Reflect:** Have you been a silent friend to someone who is ill, or a talking one? Which did they need?

## 02 The psalms of sickness: prayers for the sick bed

**Main readings:** Psalm 6; Psalm 38; Psalm 41; Psalm 102 (and Psalm 88, briefly).

God put the words of sick people into His book, and not all of them end happily.

### Asking, and asking how long

These psalms are laments: prayers that bring grief and complaint to God, not away from Him.

> Be merciful to me, O LORD, for I am frail; heal me, O LORD, for my bones are in agony. My soul is deeply distressed. How long, O LORD, how long?

Psalm 6:2–3 · BSB

Before Psalm 6 ends, the sufferer can say, "The LORD has heard my cry for mercy" (Psalm 6:9 · BSB). Being heard comes before anything is said about being well.

### When the sufferer names his own sin

Psalm 38 ties the sufferer's illness to his sin: "there is no rest in my bones because of my sin" (Psalm 38:3 · BSB). Psalm 41 prays, "heal me, for I have sinned against You" (Psalm 41:4 · BSB). Both are confessions a person makes about himself; neither lets anyone say it about someone else.

### Short days, and the God who remains

Psalm 102 is a sick man's prayer: "my days vanish like smoke, and my bones burn like glowing embers" (Psalm 102:3 · BSB). He turns from his short days to God's endless ones: "But You remain the same, and Your years will never end" (Psalm 102:27 · BSB). Hebrews quotes these lines as spoken "about the Son" (Hebrews 1:8 · BSB; 1:10–12). The Lord the sick man prayed to is Christ.

Psalm 88 even ends in the dark, "darkness is my closest companion" (Psalm 88:18 · BSB), yet it is prayed to "the God of my salvation" (Psalm 88:1 · BSB). A planned study of fear, anxiety and despair reads it in full.

**Consider:** Psalms 6, 38 and 102 all ask for help. What does each say about God before the help comes?

**Reflect:** Have you thought lament is a failure of faith? Which of these psalms could you pray this week as your own?

## 03 Hezekiah: a prayer answered, and a remedy used

**Main readings:** Isaiah 38:1–21; 2 Kings 20:21; 1 Timothy 5:23; Colossians 4:14.

The prophet said, "you will not recover." The king turned his face to the wall.

### Heard

Isaiah's word was hard: "Put your house in order, for you are about to die; you will not recover" (Isaiah 38:1 · BSB). Hezekiah prayed, "And Hezekiah wept bitterly" (Isaiah 38:3 · BSB). God sent Isaiah back:

> I have heard your prayer; I have seen your tears. Behold, I will add fifteen years to your life.

Isaiah 38:5 · BSB

Then a detail many readers miss. Isaiah had said, "Prepare a lump of pressed figs and apply it to the boil, and he will recover" (Isaiah 38:21 · BSB). God healed the king, and a paste of figs went on the boil.

### Medicine is not unbelief

Scripture says this plainly more than once. Paul tells Timothy, "use a little wine instead, because of your stomach and your frequent ailments" (1 Timothy 5:23 · BSB): a remedy, not a rebuke. He calls his companion "Luke, the beloved physician" (Colossians 4:14 · BSB). W. A. Criswell, for many years pastor of First Baptist Church in Dallas, pointed to Hezekiah's figs and said of doctors and medicines, "let us use all of the means that we can, for they also are of God" (sermon "Divine Healing", 1974). Going to the doctor is not a lack of faith, and praying is not a substitute for treatment.

### Fifteen years, not forever

In time "Hezekiah rested with his fathers" (2 Kings 20:21 · BSB). Healing in this life is a real mercy, not the end of the story. And this is a story, told of one king, with a word from a prophet: it shows that God hears and can reverse even a stated outcome, not that every prayer for healing will add years.

**Consider:** Why do you think the chapter keeps the detail of the figs?

**Reflect:** Do you treat prayer and your doctor as rivals? How would Hezekiah's story change that?

## 04 Paul's friends: one spared, one left behind

**Main readings:** 2 Corinthians 12:7–10; Philippians 2:25–30; 2 Timothy 4:20.

Several people in Paul's letters are unwell. One recovers, and Paul calls it mercy.

### The thorn

Paul was given "a thorn in my flesh" and "Three times I pleaded with the Lord to take it away from me" (2 Corinthians 12:7–8 · BSB). He does not say what it was. The answer was no, with this:

> My grace is sufficient for you, for My power is perfected in weakness.

2 Corinthians 12:9 · BSB

(Study 04, Prayer in the letters, walks this passage under "What if the answer is no?")

### Epaphroditus and Trophimus

Epaphroditus carried the Philippians' gift to Paul in prison and fell ill.

> He was sick indeed, nearly unto death. But God had mercy on him, and not only on him but also on me, to spare me sorrow upon sorrow.

Philippians 2:27 · BSB

Paul does not say he healed him. He says God had mercy: recovery is a gift, not a wage. Trophimus was left behind, sick (2 Timothy 4:20).

God worked healings through Paul (Acts 19:11–12; 28:8), yet Paul, one of the apostles, the men Jesus sent out as His first witnesses, could not heal at will. John Calvin says of the early church, "It is, indeed, certain that they were not all healed" (on James 5:14). Criswell: "Even the apostles had not the gifts of healing indiscriminately" (sermon "The Gifts of Healing", 1966).

**Consider:** Put Philippians 2:27 beside 2 Timothy 4:20. What do the two teach together that neither teaches alone?

**Reflect:** If God has said no to your prayer for healing, which would help you more today: Paul's thorn, or his friend Trophimus?

## 05 "Your faith has healed you": was my faith too small?

**Main readings:** Mark 5:25–34; Mark 9:24; Mark 14:36.

She had spent everything on doctors. Jesus told her that her faith had healed her. If you are still ill, what does that say about you?

### What Jesus said, and to whom

The woman had bled for twelve years and "had spent all she had, but to no avail" (Mark 5:26 · BSB). She touched His cloak from behind, and then came "trembling in fear" (Mark 5:33 · BSB). He said:

> "Daughter," said Jesus, "your faith has healed you. Go in peace and be free of your affliction."

Mark 5:34 · BSB

He says it to her, of her. He gives no amount of faith, and makes no rule for everyone who is ill. The power was His: He "was aware that power had gone out from Him" (Mark 5:30 · BSB). Her faith was what brought a frightened woman to Him.

### Scripture will not let this verse become a measure

Faith matters; at Nazareth Jesus "was amazed at their unbelief" (Mark 6:6 · BSB), as Study 18 shows. But a father cried, "I do believe; help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24 · BSB), and his son was healed. Paul, whose faith no one doubts, kept his thorn. And Jesus Himself, in the garden, prayed the most faithful prayer ever prayed: "Take this cup from Me. Yet not what I will, but what You will" (Mark 14:36 · BSB). The cup was not taken.

So, plainly: if you are still ill, Scripture does not say it is because your faith was too small. Healing comes from Christ's power and mercy; it is not paid for with a quantity of faith. The question to ask is not "How much do I believe?" but "Whom do I trust?" (this site's reading).

[care] Being told you are still sick because you did not believe enough is a cruelty, and the Gospels never say it. If someone has said it to you, read Mark 14:36 again.

**Consider:** What did the woman's faith actually do in Mark 5? What did Jesus' power do?

**Reflect:** Have you measured your faith by your health? What would it mean to trust Him with the outcome instead?

## 06 James 5: what the sick and the church should do

**Main readings:** James 5:13–18; 1 Corinthians 12:9, 28–30.

James does not argue about whether God heals. He tells a sick believer what to do.

### Call, pray, confess

"Is any one of you suffering? He should pray" (James 5:13 · BSB). Then:

> Is any one of you sick? He should call the elders of the church to pray over him and anoint him with oil in the name of the Lord. And the prayer offered in faith will restore the one who is sick. The Lord will raise him up. If he has sinned, he will be forgiven.

James 5:14–15 · BSB

The elders are the church's pastors, the men who lead and care for a congregation. The sick person calls; the elders come; the Lord heals. Note the "if" in "If he has sinned" (James 5:15 · BSB): not every sickness comes from sin, but where it is part of the story it is dealt with too: "confess your sins to each other and pray for each other so that you may be healed" (James 5:16 · BSB). The example is ordinary: "Elijah was a man just like us" (James 5:17 · BSB).

[open] What is the oil? Criswell found in the commentaries "a bedlam of differing voices": medicine, or a sign. Calvin read it as a sign that belonged with the gift of healing in the apostles' day. The Roman Catholic Church reads it as a sacrament, a rite through which it teaches grace is given; the Council of Trent (1545–1563) counted "Extreme Unction" among seven. The Baptist Faith and Message names two ordinances, rites Christ gave the church to keep: baptism and the Lord's Supper. With oil or without, the command is to pray.

"Will restore" is a real promise. Calvin says the Lord granted healing "as often and as far as he knew it would be expedient"; Trophimus shows it does not mean every illness ends now. And "The Lord will raise him up" will be true of every believer at the last (part 07).

### Where Christians differ: do gifts of healing continue?

Spiritual gifts are abilities the Holy Spirit gives believers to serve. Paul lists "gifts of healing" and asks, "Do all have gifts of healing?" (1 Corinthians 12:30 · BSB), expecting no. Christians differ on whether this gift is still given:

- **It ceased with the apostles.** Criswell: "God heals. God healed yesterday. God heals today." But the gift of healing was "a sign gift" that confirmed the apostles' message, and "When the office passed away, the sign passed away" (1966). Calvin held "That the gift of healing was temporary".
- **It continues.** John Piper, then pastor of Bethlehem Baptist Church in Minneapolis: "So I believe that 'gifts of healings' and 'workings of miracles' are for the church today" (sermon, 1990). He adds: "The greatest need is to care that people are sick".

The Baptist Faith and Message says the Holy Spirit "bestows the spiritual gifts by which they serve God through His church" (Article II), without saying which continue. This study does not choose. Both views agree that God heals today in answer to prayer, that James 5 is for the church now, and that no one heals at will.

**Consider:** In James 5:14–16, who acts? The sick person, the elders, the church, the Lord: what does each do?

**Reflect:** When you are ill, do you tell your church or hide it? What would it take for you to "call the elders"?

## 07 Healing at the cross: now, or later?

**Main readings:** Isaiah 53:4–5; Matthew 8:16–17; 1 Peter 2:24; Romans 8:18–26; 2 Corinthians 4:16–5:5; Revelation 21:3–5.

Some teach that healing was bought at the cross, so every believer may claim it today. Read the verses they use.

### The claim, stated fairly

The atonement is what Christ's death accomplished: our sins borne and paid for, and sinners brought back to God. The Word of Faith movement, which teaches that believers can claim health and wealth by faith, and some Pentecostal teaching say physical healing is guaranteed in the atonement. They begin with real texts. Isaiah says of the servant, "by His stripes we are healed" (Isaiah 53:5 · BSB). Matthew says Jesus healed the sick "to fulfill what was spoken through the prophet Isaiah: 'He took on our infirmities and carried our diseases'" (Matthew 8:17 · BSB). So, the claim runs, healing is the believer's now as surely as forgiveness, and sickness that remains shows faith or obedience lacking. A tract Criswell read to his church in 1966 put it bluntly: "When you make up your mind to obey God, you will be healed." Prosperity teaching widens the promise to money, reading a greeting as a promise: "I pray that in every way you may prosper and enjoy good health" (3 John 1:2 · BSB). That is a prayer in a letter's opening (the first question).

### What the texts say

Matthew applies Isaiah 53:4 to an evening in Capernaum, before the cross, when Jesus "healed all the sick" (Matthew 8:16 · BSB). Peter applies Isaiah 53:5 to sin:

> He Himself bore our sins in His body on the tree, so that we might die to sin and live to righteousness. "By His stripes you are healed."

1 Peter 2:24 · BSB

Both are true readings of one song. And the claim is right about this much: Christ's death does win the healing of the body. The Baptist Faith and Message says salvation "involves the redemption of the whole man" and that "Glorification is the culmination of salvation" (Article IV): glorification is the last stage, when believers are raised with new bodies. Criswell said "A part of the atonement of Christ is for the healing of our bodies", and in the same sermon, "God does not always heal." The question is not whether the cross heals the body, but when.

### Not yet: the body's groaning

> Not only that, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for our adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies.

Romans 8:23 · BSB

"Firstfruits" are the first part of a harvest, the pledge of the rest; "redemption" means being bought back and set free. Those who groan are the ones who have the Spirit. Groaning in a failing body is not faith failing. "Though our outer self is wasting away, yet our inner self is being renewed day by day" (2 Corinthians 4:16 · BSB), and God "has given us the Spirit as a pledge of what is to come" (2 Corinthians 5:5 · BSB). (Studies 29 and 30 walk Romans 8 at length.)

Piper puts it in three sentences: God "will heal them in the age to come. He heals them often now. And when he doesn't, they will get healing that is even better" ("God Heals All Our Diseases, Really?", 2014).

### The promise without conditions

> 'He will wipe away every tear from their eyes,' and there will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain, for the former things have passed away."

Revelation 21:4 · BSB

This promise hangs on nothing but belonging to Him: "They will be His people, and God Himself will be with them as their God" (Revelation 21:3 · BSB). So this site does not teach that health or wealth is owed to faith or to a gift of money. Criswell warned his church against "paid healers—men who make money off of the illnesses of the people".

**Consider:** Matthew 8:17 and 1 Peter 2:24 both quote Isaiah 53. What does each apply it to?

**Reflect:** Where are you groaning now? What changes if you hear Romans 8:23 as a picture of faith, not its failure?

## What God promises, and what He does not

He does not promise that every illness will end in this life, that healing follows enough faith or a large enough gift, or that you will be told why. He does promise that He hears (Psalm 6:9), that His grace is enough (2 Corinthians 12:9), that His church will pray (James 5:14), that He often restores now (Philippians 2:27), and that your body will be redeemed and every tear wiped away (Romans 8:23; Revelation 21:4). The three questions work on any promise you are offered: a promise, a prayer or a story; now or not yet; and who is speaking, and does God agree?

**Your question:** If you are ill, or love someone who is, which promise do you most need to hold this week, and which false one do you need to let go?

Read the passage again before you answer.
