# Witness and the nations: telling others about Jesus

Concepts: Sent · Heard · Grown

Motto: Many feet. One message. / Every nation in view.

In a courtroom, a witness is someone who tells what they have seen and know. The New Testament uses the same word for people who tell others about Jesus. They tell who He is, what He did, and why it matters. This study is about that telling, done by one friend to another and carried by whole churches to the far side of the world. It teaches one reading habit. **In any passage about witness, ask whose part is whose.** What does God do? What is given to the person who speaks? What belongs to the person who hears? Most mistakes about witness come from mixing these up. One mistake is pressing people as if their answer were ours to produce. The other is staying silent as if speaking were not ours to do.

> I planted the seed and Apollos watered it, but God made it grow.

1 Corinthians 3:6 · BSB

This is a selection, not a census. It walks seven passages: the risen Jesus' charge on a mountain in Galilee, two paragraphs from Romans, two from Paul's letters to Corinth, one from Philippians and one from Colossians. Each was chosen because it answers a different question about telling others: why, what, to whom, by what means, at what cost and in what manner. A few other verses are quoted for one point each, and the studies that read them in full are linked. Paul's prayers for open doors belong to *Prayer across Scripture: learning to pray*. Peter's words on answering with gentleness belong to *Faith under pressure*. The good news itself, told in full, is the subject of *The good news: how a person is made right with God*. This page is not a method that guarantees results, because no passage here offers one.

Two words first. The **gospel** is an old English word for "good news": the news that Jesus Christ died for sins, was raised from the dead, and is Lord. **Missions** is the church's word for carrying that news across distances and between cultures. A **missionary** is someone a church sends to do it.

## 01 The Great Commission: make disciples of all nations

**Main readings:** Matthew 28:16–20; with Genesis 12:3 and Acts 1:8 quoted

Christians call these verses the Great Commission. A *commission* is a task handed over with the authority to do it. The name is not in the Bible, but it fits.

The scene is plain and honest. Eleven men climb a mountain in Galilee; Judas is no longer with them. "When they saw Him, they worshiped Him, but some doubted" (Matthew 28:17). Matthew does not tidy the group up. The task is given to worshippers who still have questions.

Jesus begins with authority before He gives any command: "All authority in heaven and on earth has been given to Me" (Matthew 28:18). The command follows from it, and its first word says so.

> Therefore go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey all that I have commanded you. And surely I am with you always, even to the end of the age.

Matthew 28:19–20 · BSB

Look at the shape of the sentence. Commentators often point out that in Matthew's Greek the main command is "make disciples". The words go, baptizing and teaching describe how it is done. A **disciple** is a learner who follows a teacher, so the aim is more than a decision on one day. It is people who keep learning to obey Jesus. To **baptize** is to immerse in water. Baptists hold that Christian baptism is the immersion of a believer, as article VII (baptism and the Lord's Supper) of the Baptist Faith and Message 2000 says. Baptism is read in full in *Baptism and the Lord's Supper: the two things Jesus told His church to do*.

"All nations" translates a Greek phrase that means all peoples, not modern countries with borders and flags. The idea is old. God's first promise to Abraham ended this way:

> I will bless those who bless you and curse those who curse you; and all the families of the earth will be blessed through you.

Genesis 12:3 · BSB

Jewish and Christian readers both treasure this as God's word to Abraham and his descendants. Christians also hear in it the first sign that the whole world was always in view. Read more in *Covenants and promises*. (A **covenant** is a binding promise that God makes and keeps.) Luke records the same charge with its map attached:

> But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit comes upon you, and you will be My witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth.

Acts 1:8 · BSB

Read more in *The church and its calling*.

Was the commission only for the eleven? The promise answers that question. "To the end of the age" lasts longer than their lives, so the task does too. In 1792 the English Baptist William Carey argued exactly this in *An Enquiry into the Obligations of Christians to Use Means for the Conversion of the Heathens*, against those who thought the command had ended with the apostles. The Baptist Missionary Society was formed that same year, and Carey sailed for India. The Baptist Faith and Message puts it in article XI (evangelism and missions): it is "the duty and privilege of every follower of Christ and of every church of the Lord Jesus Christ to endeavor to make disciples of all nations". **Evangelism** is telling the gospel so that people may believe.

**Its limits.** The passage does not say every believer must cross an ocean, and it gives no method. It does not promise that every hearer will become a disciple. Whose part is whose: the authority and the presence are Christ's. The going, baptizing and teaching are given to His people.

**Consider:** What does Jesus say before He gives the command, and what does He promise after it? Why might it matter that some who heard Him were still doubting?

**Reflect:** Making disciples includes teaching after the first step. Is there someone a little newer to faith than you whom you could help keep learning?

## 02 How will they hear? Sending and the word of Christ

**Main readings:** Romans 10:9–17

Paul wrote Romans 9–11 with grief over his own people, the Jewish people, most of whom had not believed in Jesus. Paul was himself a Jew, as were Jesus, the apostles and the whole first church. He speaks as a brother with a broken heart, never as an outsider passing judgment. Romans 10 opens with his prayer that they may be saved (Romans 10:1).

He first says what is to be told and believed: "if you confess with your mouth, 'Jesus is Lord,' and believe in your heart that God raised Him from the dead, you will be saved" (Romans 10:9). To **confess** is to say openly what you believe. To be **saved** is to be rescued from sin and its judgment and brought to God. Paul adds that with the heart "you believe and are justified" (Romans 10:10). To be **justified** is to be declared right with God, as a judge declares someone not guilty. Here that happens through trusting Christ, not by earning it. These words are not a password. The mouth says what the heart has come to trust. The fuller account is in *Saved by grace: how God forgives and changes us*.

Then the doors open wide: "there is no difference between Jew and Greek: The same Lord is Lord of all, and gives richly to all who call on Him" (Romans 10:12). Paul quotes the prophet Joel: "Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" (Romans 10:13). In Joel, "the Lord" stands for God's own name, and Paul gives it to Jesus.

Then comes the chain, read backward from calling on Him to being sent:

> How then can they call on the One in whom they have not believed? And how can they believe in the One of whom they have not heard? And how can they hear without someone to preach? And how can they preach unless they are sent? As it is written: “How beautiful are the feet of those who bring good news!”

Romans 10:14–15 · BSB

The beautiful feet come from Isaiah 52:7, where runners bring Jerusalem news that God is coming back to His people after the exile. Paul hears the same kind of news in the message about Christ.

"But not all of them welcomed the good news. For Isaiah says, 'Lord, who has believed our message?'" (Romans 10:16). This line comes from Isaiah 53:1, so the Jewish reading of that chapter should be named fairly first. In Jewish tradition, notably in the medieval commentator Rashi, the suffering servant of Isaiah 52–53 is usually understood as the people of Israel, despised among the nations and finally vindicated. Some older Jewish writings, such as the Aramaic paraphrase called the Targum, connect the servant with the Messiah while reading his suffering differently. Christians read the passage of Jesus, as the New Testament does. Read more in *Christ in the Old Testament*. Paul does not make unbelief a mark against one people. Two chapters later he says God has not rejected His people (Romans 11:1–2), and many Gentiles in every age have not welcomed the message either.

The paragraph ends: "Consequently, faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of Christ" (Romans 10:17).

**Its limits.** The chain shows why sending matters. It does not rank the one who goes above the one who stays and sends. It does not promise that hearing always leads to faith; the next verse says it did not. Whose part is whose: God gives richly to all who call. The sent one preaches. The hearer is to believe and call on Him.

**Consider:** Which link in Paul's chain comes first, and which last? What two things does Romans 10:9 say a person who is saved believes and says?

**Reflect:** Every believer heard because someone spoke. Who was that for you, and have you ever told them?

## 03 Where Christ was not known: the unreached

**Main readings:** Romans 15:18–21; with Revelation 7:9 quoted

Near the end of Romans, Paul looks back over about twenty years of work. He refuses to boast about himself: "I will not presume to speak of anything except what Christ has accomplished through me in leading the Gentiles to obedience by word and deed" (Romans 15:18). **Gentiles** means everyone who is not Jewish. Christ does the work; Paul is the one He works through. It was done by word and deed, "by the power of signs and wonders, and by the power of the Spirit of God" (Romans 15:19). The circle stretched "from Jerusalem all the way around to Illyricum", a Roman province on the Adriatic coast where Albania and the countries of former Yugoslavia lie today.

> In this way I have aspired to preach the gospel where Christ was not known, so that I would not be building on someone else’s foundation.

Romans 15:20 · BSB

His reason is another line from Isaiah's servant passage (Isaiah 52:15): "Those who were not told about Him will see, and those who have not heard will understand" (Romans 15:21). The Jewish reading of that passage is named in part 02.

Mission agencies today speak of **unreached peoples**. These are people groups (a group sharing a language and culture) with so few followers of Jesus that they are not likely to hear the gospel unless someone comes from outside. One common measure counts a group as unreached when fewer than about two in a hundred are evangelical Christians [check: current definitions used by the Joshua Project and the International Mission Board]. The term is modern; Paul's ambition is where it comes from. Taking his ambition as a call to the unreached is this site's application, and a widely shared one. The site's people-group explorer [check: link and page name] lets you look up peoples by country and language and see where the gospel has hardly been heard.

Scripture also shows where this is heading:

> After this I looked and saw a multitude too large to count, from every nation and tribe and people and tongue, standing before the throne and before the Lamb.

Revelation 7:9 · BSB

The Lamb is Jesus, named for His sacrifice. Read more in *Genesis 1–11: the questions readers bring*, which takes up how the nations and their languages began.

[open] Signs and wonders accompanied Paul's work. Whether such miraculous gifts continue today is a question on which Baptists differ, because the Baptist Faith and Message does not settle it. Some other Christians, classical Pentecostals among them, hold that they do. This page does not choose. Read more in *The Holy Spirit*.

**Its limits.** Paul's ambition was his own calling. It is not a rule that no one should build on another's foundation. In the next part he praises Apollos for watering what he planted. The passage does not say the unreached are more precious than the person next door. It says they are no less precious, and harder to reach.

**Consider:** Who does Paul say accomplished the work? What does he give as his reason for going where Christ was not known?

**Reflect:** Look up one people group on the explorer. Pray for them by name this week, and ask what part you might play in their hearing.

## 04 Not ourselves: honest words and jars of clay

**Main readings:** 2 Corinthians 4:1–7

Some in Corinth were questioning Paul's work. He answers by describing how the gospel is told rightly. "Since God in His mercy has given us this ministry, we do not lose heart" (2 Corinthians 4:1). **Ministry** means service. Paul received this service as mercy. He did not win it as a prize.

Then he names what he has given up: "we have renounced secret and shameful ways. We do not practice deceit, nor do we distort the word of God" (2 Corinthians 4:2). In practice this rules out a hidden agenda, a friendship offered only as bait, pressure tactics, gifts given to buy agreement, and leaving out the hard parts of the message to make it easier to accept. In their place: "by open proclamation of the truth, we commend ourselves to every man’s conscience in the sight of God" (2 Corinthians 4:2). Paul speaks to the conscience, the inner sense of right and wrong. He does not try to get around it.

He knows that not everyone sees: "The god of this age has blinded the minds of unbelievers so they cannot see the light of the gospel of the glory of Christ, who is the image of God" (2 Corinthians 4:4). Most readers take "the god of this age" as Satan, ruling over a world turned from God. Read more in *Angels, demons and spiritual powers*. So unbelief is more than a lack of information, and a better argument alone cannot cure it.

> For we do not proclaim ourselves, but Jesus Christ as Lord, and ourselves as your servants for Jesus’ sake.

2 Corinthians 4:5 · BSB

The cure comes from the God who made light on the first day: He "made His light shine in our hearts to give us the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ" (2 Corinthians 4:6). **Glory** is the shining greatness of who God is. Paul closes the paragraph with a cheap pot: "we have this treasure in jars of clay to show that this surpassingly great power is from God and not from us" (2 Corinthians 4:7).

**Its limits.** Your own story may help explain the message, but it is not the message. The message is "Jesus Christ as Lord". The passage does not promise that honest telling will be welcomed. Whose part is whose: God shines the light. An enemy blinds. The messenger tells the truth openly and steps aside.

**Consider:** What four things does Paul refuse in verse 2? Why does it matter that the treasure is kept in clay?

**Reflect:** Is there any way you have tried to win someone that you would be ashamed to have them find out about? What would open, honest telling look like instead?

## 05 Planting and watering: God gives the growth

**Main readings:** 1 Corinthians 3:5–9; with Colossians 4:3–4 and Ephesians 6:19 quoted

The church at Corinth was splitting into fan clubs: some for Paul, some for Apollos, another teacher (1 Corinthians 3:4). Paul cuts both men down to size. "What then is Apollos? And what is Paul? They are servants through whom you believed, as the Lord has assigned to each his role" (1 Corinthians 3:5). Then comes the verse at the top of this study: one planted, one watered, and "God made it grow" (1 Corinthians 3:6).

His first point is against rivalry: "neither he who plants nor he who waters is anything, but only God, who makes things grow" (1 Corinthians 3:7). His second point keeps the work from looking pointless: "He who plants and he who waters are one in purpose, and each will be rewarded according to his own labor" (1 Corinthians 3:8). Then: "we are God’s fellow workers; you are God’s field, God’s building" (1 Corinthians 3:9). Careful readers take "God's fellow workers" in two ways. It may mean working alongside God, or it may mean fellow workers who all belong to God. Either way, the field is His.

Using this passage for everyday witness is an application. Paul is first speaking about teachers in a church. But it carries over well. Planting and watering are yours to do. The growth was never yours. So a person's answer is not a score of your worth, and no amount of pressure can do what only God does. This is also why the apostles asked for prayer. Paul asked the Colossians to pray "that God may open to us a door for the word, so that we may proclaim the mystery of Christ" and "that I may declare it clearly, as I should" (Colossians 4:3–4). Here a **mystery** is something once hidden that God has now made known. He asked the Ephesians to pray "that whenever I open my mouth, words may be given me so that I will boldly make known the mystery of the gospel" (Ephesians 6:19). Read more in *Prayer across Scripture: learning to pray*.

**Its limits.** The passage does not let anyone off planting because God gives the growth. It does not let anyone take credit when growth comes. And it does not say how long God takes. Some seed grows after the planter has gone home.

**Consider:** What does Paul say about the planter and the waterer, and what does he say about God? What reward does he mention?

**Reflect:** Is there someone whose slow response you have taken as your own failure? What changes if their growth is God's work and your planting is faithful?

## 06 Courage in chains

**Main readings:** Philippians 1:12–30

Paul writes this letter as a prisoner. Where he was held is an open historical question: Rome is the traditional answer, and some scholars suggest Ephesus or Caesarea. He wants his friends to know something surprising: "my circumstances have actually served to advance the gospel" (Philippians 1:12). The soldiers guarding him have learned why he is there: "it has become clear throughout the whole palace guard and to everyone else that I am in chains for Christ" (Philippians 1:13). Other believers have taken heart:

> And most of the brothers, confident in the Lord by my chains, now dare more greatly to speak the word without fear.

Philippians 1:14 · BSB

Not all of them have good motives. "Some preach Christ out of envy and rivalry, but others out of goodwill" (Philippians 1:15). Some even hope "to add to the distress of my chains" (Philippians 1:17). Paul's answer surprises: "whether by false motives or true, Christ is preached. And in this I rejoice" (Philippians 1:18). He rejoices that Christ is preached. He does not approve the motive, which he has just called envy, and this is no welcome for any message at all. Elsewhere he says a different gospel must be refused outright (Galatians 1:8–9).

His hope is that "Christ will be exalted in my body, whether by life or by death" (Philippians 1:20). He says, "For to me, to live is Christ, and to die is gain" (Philippians 1:21). The gain he means is being "with Christ" (Philippians 1:23). He chooses to stay for the sake of others: "it is more necessary for you that I remain in the body" (Philippians 1:24).

Then the letter turns to the readers. "Conduct yourselves in a manner worthy of the gospel of Christ", so that "you stand firm in one spirit, contending side by side for the faith of the gospel, without being frightened in any way by those who oppose you" (Philippians 1:27–28). Courage here is not one lone hero. It is people standing side by side. And it comes with a hard gift: "it has been granted to you on behalf of Christ not only to believe in Him, but also to suffer for Him" (Philippians 1:29). The church at Philippi had seen this for itself. Acts tells of Paul being beaten and jailed in their city (Acts 16:22–24).

[care] This is what happened in Paul's case, not a promise that your hardship will turn out the same way. Courage does not mean staying in danger you could leave. Jesus told His followers to flee to the next town when persecuted (Matthew 10:23). If telling others about Jesus could put you in danger where you live, or if someone at home is harming you, getting to safety and asking for help does not disobey this passage. Paul himself looked forward to being free and coming to them again (Philippians 1:26).

**Its limits.** The passage does not tell anyone to seek out suffering, and it does not say all opposition proves you are right. Whose part is whose: through their prayers and "the provision of the Spirit of Jesus Christ" (Philippians 1:19), God holds the outcome. The believers' part is to stand together and not be frightened.

**Consider:** What did Paul's chains do for others? What does he rejoice in, and what does he still call wrong?

**Reflect:** Where has fear kept you quiet? Who could stand beside you as you take the next small, truthful step?

## 07 Speech seasoned with salt

**Main readings:** Colossians 4:5–6; with 1 Peter 3:15 and 1 Peter 2:12 quoted

Right after asking for prayer for his own open door, Paul turns to the Colossians' everyday dealings with people who do not believe:

> Act wisely toward outsiders, redeeming the time.

Colossians 4:5 · BSB

> Let your speech always be gracious, seasoned with salt, so that you may know how to answer everyone.

Colossians 4:6 · BSB

**Grace** is God's kindness given to people who have not earned it. Gracious speech carries some of that kindness. Salt gives flavour and keeps food from spoiling, so salty speech is neither dull nor rotten. To redeem the time is to buy up the moment and not waste it. That does not mean forcing every meeting into a sermon. It does mean not letting every meeting slip by.

"How to answer everyone" assumes that people differ. An answer is given to someone. It cannot be recited at them. One person wants to understand what you believe. Another is testing a claim. Another is telling you why trust has been hard for them. Those are this site's examples; the verse does not sort people into kinds. But you cannot answer well without listening first.

Peter gives the same counsel to Christians under pressure:

> But in your hearts sanctify Christ as Lord. Always be prepared to give a defense to everyone who asks you the reason for the hope that is in you. But respond with gentleness and respect,

1 Peter 3:15 · BSB

To **sanctify** Christ here is to honour Him as holy, above every fear. Read more in *Faith under pressure*. Peter also says that a life lived honourably speaks: "Conduct yourselves with such honor among the Gentiles that, though they slander you as evildoers, they may see your good deeds and glorify God on the day He visits us" (1 Peter 2:12). Read more in *Faith in daily life*. The Baptist Faith and Message keeps both, in this order: "verbal witness undergirded by a Christian lifestyle" (article XI).

Putting these passages together, here is this site's practical counsel for telling the gospel. It is application, not command:
- **Listen first.** Learn what the person is actually asking.
- **Use plain words.** Say "made right with God" before you say "justified", or explain the word when you use it.
- **Tell about Jesus, not only about yourself.** Say who He is, that He died for sins and rose, and that He is Lord (Romans 10:9). Your story can show why it matters to you.
- **Say what you do not know.** "I need to go and read about that" is more honest than bluffing. Peter asks for "the reason for the hope that is in you", not an answer to every objection.
- **Invite a response.** Ask what they think, and whether they would like to read a Gospel with you.
- **Leave them free.** A person may say they have heard enough, and that must be honoured.

**Its limits.** Gracious does not mean vague. The paragraph has just asked for the message to be declared "clearly" (Colossians 4:4). Salt is no licence for cutting remarks, and grace comes first in the verse.

**Consider:** What are the three things Colossians 4:5–6 asks of believers toward outsiders? What does Peter say the answer is about?

**Reflect:** Write down, in five plain sentences, the reason for the hope you have in Christ. Mark one point you could explain well, and one question to study before you try.

## Questions readers ask

### What about people who never hear?

[read] This question is honest and painful, and Romans 10 is where it comes from. The Baptist Faith and Message says plainly: "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord" (article IV, salvation). Paul says creation shows enough of God to leave people without excuse (Romans 1:20), and that people have a conscience (Romans 2:14–15). He does not say these alone save. That is why he asks, "How then can they call on the One in whom they have not believed?" (Romans 10:14). For Baptists, this is the urgency behind missions, not a reason to stop caring. Abraham's question also stands: the Judge of all the earth will do what is right (Genesis 18:25).

[other] Other Christians answer differently, and their views are labelled as theirs. The Roman Catholic Church's Second Vatican Council, in *Lumen Gentium*, paragraph 16 (1964), teaches that those who through no fault of their own do not know the gospel, but sincerely seek God and try to do His will as their conscience shows it, may attain salvation. Some Protestant writers hold a similar view, called inclusivism. One example is Clark Pinnock in *A Wideness in God's Mercy* (1992). These are not the confession's view, and this site does not adopt them.

### Isn't it disrespectful to try to persuade someone about my faith?

[read] It can be done disrespectfully, and Scripture condemns that. Paul renounced "secret and shameful ways" (2 Corinthians 4:2), and Peter asks for "gentleness and respect" (1 Peter 3:15). But telling someone what you believe to be true and good is a form of respect. It treats them as able to weigh it. Baptists have long defended this together with freedom. Article XVII (religious liberty) says "God alone is Lord of the conscience". So no one is to be pressured, bribed or forced, and no government should do it either. Christians in past centuries did sometimes force or pressure conversions, and that was a grievous wrong. A person may hear the gospel, think it over and say no. You are free to speak, and they are free to decide.

### If God gives the growth, why speak?

[read] Because the same passages that give the growth to God tell people to plant. Paul says God made it grow, and in the same breath calls himself and Apollos "servants through whom you believed" (1 Corinthians 3:5–6). Faith comes by hearing (Romans 10:17). The Baptist Faith and Message holds these together. It says election, God's gracious choosing, "comprehends all the means in connection with the end" (article V, God's purpose of grace). In other words, God works through people telling the news.

[open] Baptists differ on the details of election, and this page does not choose. The Abstract of Principles (1858), the founding confession of the Southern Baptist Theological Seminary, calls election "God's eternal choice of some persons unto everlasting life". The New Hampshire Confession (1833) says election "encourages the use of means in the highest degree". A Statement of the Traditional Southern Baptist Understanding of God's Plan of Salvation (2012), told here in this site's words, rejects the view that God chose certain individuals for salvation from eternity. It speaks of election as God's plan to save all who repent and believe, and it also calls the preaching of the gospel God's means of bringing people to salvation [check: wording of the 2012 statement's articles on election and the gospel, against the original]. Every one of these keeps the call to preach.

### How do Southern Baptist churches send missionaries?

[read] Southern Baptist churches are self-governing. No headquarters owns them or tells them what to do. They choose to cooperate. Article XIV (cooperation) of the Baptist Faith and Message says churches may form associations and conventions "as occasion requires", and that these are "voluntary and advisory bodies" with no authority over the churches. Paul's picture of planters and waterers who are "one in purpose" (1 Corinthians 3:8) describes the spirit of it.

[read] In practice, many churches give a share of their offerings to a shared fund called the Cooperative Program, begun in 1925. It passes through state conventions to the Southern Baptist Convention's ministries. Two of these send missionaries. The International Mission Board works among the nations; it began as the Foreign Mission Board in 1845, the year the convention was formed, and took its present name in 1997 [check: year of the rename]. The North American Mission Board works in the United States and Canada [check: founding year, 1997]. Churches also give special offerings each year. The Lottie Moon Christmas Offering, named for a missionary to China, goes to international missions, and the Annie Armstrong Easter Offering goes to North America. Missionaries are sent from local churches, which also pray for them and support them. Present numbers of missionaries and of churches taking part change from year to year [check: current figures from the IMB and NAMB before quoting any].

[read] This is one way, not the only way. Other Baptist bodies, other churches and many mission agencies send missionaries too. A church's part in the Great Commission does not depend on belonging to any convention.

## Close: Whose part is whose

You have walked seven passages: a charge on a mountain, a chain of sending, an ambition for places where Christ was not known, honest words in clay jars, planting and watering, courage in chains and speech seasoned with salt. Take the habit with you to the next passage about witness. Ask three questions. What does God do? He has the authority, sends, shines the light and gives the growth. What is given to the one who speaks? To go, tell plainly and honestly, live in a way that backs up the words, pray, stand together and not be afraid. What belongs to the one who hears? To believe and call on the name of the Lord. Mix these up and witness becomes either pressure or silence. Keep them apart and you can speak freely and leave the rest with God.

If you are the one being told, this news is for you too. "Everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" (Romans 10:13). To **repent** is to turn from your sin to God. Turn from your sin and trust Jesus as Lord, believing that God raised Him from the dead (Romans 10:9). This site holds with the Baptist Faith and Message that "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord". Then tell someone, find a church, and ask to be baptized.

Who is one person you could plant a seed with this week, and which part of it have you been trying to do that belongs to God?
