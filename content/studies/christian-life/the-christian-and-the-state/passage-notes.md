## Owned passages

### 1 Samuel 8:4–22: Israel asks for a king

**Context.** This is the end of the period of the judges. Samuel is old and his sons take bribes (8:1–3). The elders ask for a king "like all the other nations" (8:5, 20). God grants the request and also warns them (8:7–9, 22). Samuel's warning (8:11–17) is structured by the verb "take", which appears six times, including two "tenths" (8:15, 17). Chapters 9–12 follow with Saul's anointing and Samuel's farewell speech (ch. 12), which renews the covenant under the new king. A covenant is a binding agreement God makes with His people.

**Main readings.**
- *The sin lies in the motive, not in kingship.* Israel wanted to be like the nations and to replace God's leadership in battle. God Himself appoints the king, and Deuteronomy 17 anticipates kingship. This is the majority Christian reading and the one the study follows.
- *Kingship itself is a concession.* God permits it but it was never His ideal. Some read Deuteronomy 17 in this light as well.
- *Jewish debate.* The Babylonian Talmud (Sanhedrin 20b) records a dispute over whether appointing a king was a command or a concession anticipating complaint [check: tractate, page and the rabbis' names].
- *Political readings.* Some read the passage as a general critique of concentrated power and taxation. The study names the principle (power tends to take) but declines to turn it into a policy.

**Studies linked.** Rulers and their choices (39) reads the kings themselves. Christ in the Old Testament (41) reads the king theme forward.

### Deuteronomy 17:14–20: the law of the king

**Context.** This is part of Moses' laws for Israel's leaders (16:18–18:22: judges, king, priests, prophets). The king must be chosen by God and be an Israelite "brother" (17:15). He must not multiply horses, wives, or silver and gold (17:16–17). He must write and read "a copy of this instruction" (*torah*) all his life (17:18–19), "Then his heart will not be exalted above his countrymen" (17:20). Solomon's breach of all three limits (1 Kings 10–11) is the classic counter-example.

**Main readings.**
- *Jewish practice.* The king writes his own Torah scroll (Mishnah Sanhedrin 2:4 [check]). The Greek translation's rendering of "copy of this law" as *deuteronomion*, "second law", gave the book its Greek name [check before stating: Septuagint rendering and the derivation of the book's name].
- *Christian readings.* (1) An ethical principle: rulers are under God's law and are not above their people. This seed was later invoked in arguments for the rule of law, for example Samuel Rutherford's *Lex, Rex* (1644) [check before citing: whether to name Rutherford at all]. (2) Christological: Jesus as the king who kept the law. Linked to 41.
- *How the Mosaic law applies today.* The confession is silent. The study points to 47 and does not choose.

**Studies linked.** Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice (47); Christ in the Old Testament (41); Rulers and their choices (39).

### Acts 4:1–22: Peter and John before the council

**Context.** This follows the healing of the lame man at the Beautiful Gate and Peter's sermon (Acts 3). The temple authorities and Sadducees arrest the apostles for proclaiming the resurrection in Jesus (4:1–3). The Sanhedrin examines them (4:5–12). The council marvels at their boldness, cannot deny the miracle, and forbids them to speak in Jesus' name (4:13–18). Peter and John reply "Judge for yourselves…" (4:19–20) and are released (4:21–22). Continued in 4:23–31 (the believers' prayer, Psalm 2) and 5:17–42, where 5:29 gives "We must obey God rather than men."

**Main readings.**
- *The limit of obedience.* Civil and religious authority is real, but it cannot command what God forbids or forbid what God commands. This is the near-universal Christian reading. Baptists add that it grounds religious liberty, because the council had no right over the apostles' consciences before God.
- *The manner of disobedience.* Respectful address (4:8), no violence, open refusal, consequences accepted. The study draws this out together with Daniel 3 and 6.
- *Care with "whom you crucified" (4:10).* This is addressed to the particular council. The study notes that the apostles and the first believers were Jews, cites 4:27 (Herod, Pilate, Gentiles and peoples of Israel together) without quoting it, and states that no people bears special guilt for Christ's death.
- *4:12 and the exclusivity of Christ.* This is quoted from inside the owned passage. The good news study (54) quotes it too, and is the home of that teaching.

**Studies linked.** The good news (54) for 4:12; The book of Psalms (48) keeps 4:24–26; Reading Daniel and Revelation (49) for Daniel 3 and 6; Faith in daily life (05) for Acts 5:29 alongside Romans 13 and 1 Peter 2.

### Isaiah 2:2–4: swords into plowshares

**Context.** This is a vision of the "last days" placed between Isaiah's opening indictment (ch. 1) and the oracles of judgement (2:6 onward). The mountain of the LORD's house is exalted. The nations come to learn God's ways. *Torah* goes out from Zion, God judges between nations, and war ends. Micah 4:1–3 is nearly identical; its relation to Isaiah 2 is debated [check: whether to state any view on which text is earlier]. Isaiah 2:5 turns the vision into a call: "come, let us walk in the light of the LORD" (not quoted; not supplied).

**Main readings.**
- *Jewish reading* (named first in the study). A promise of the messianic age. Its unfulfilled state is often cited as a reason the Messiah has not yet come [check: whether to name a specific source, for example Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Laws of Kings 11–12].
- *Christian readings.* Inaugurated in the gospel's going out from Jerusalem (Luke 24:47, Acts 1:8) and completed at Christ's return. Premillennial Baptists read it of Christ's earthly reign. Amillennial and postmillennial readers read it of the new creation or of the gospel's spread. The confession (article X) names no millennial view, so the study shows them and does not choose.
- *Used in the war debate.* Pacifists read it as the shape of the kingdom believers already belong to. Just-war Christians read it as the goal that war must serve and that only God will bring. Joel 3:10 reverses the image ("plowshares into swords"); it is not cited in the study.

**Studies linked.** The hope to come (30); Following Jesus and the Sermon on the Mount (16) for Matthew 5:38–48; Prophets in their times (38) for Isaiah and Micah in their setting.
