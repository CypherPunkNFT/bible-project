# The Christian and the state

Concepts: Rulers · Limits · Conscience · Peace

Motto: A coin for Caesar. / A life for God.

Lead: The Bible is full of rulers. Some kings were asked for and some were warned against. Emperors collected taxes, and councils issued threats. This study teaches one habit for reading all of those passages. Look for two columns: what the ruler is rightly owed, and what belongs to God alone and no ruler may claim.

> Then Jesus told them, “Give to Caesar what is Caesar’s, and to God what is God’s.” And they marveled at Him.

Mark 12:17 · BSB

Frame: In this study, "the state" means government: the people and offices that make laws, collect taxes, keep order and go to war. "Caesar" was the title of the Roman emperor. Jesus was asked whether God's people should pay his tax, and He answered with two columns at once. The first column is real: there are things a government is owed. The second column is larger, because everything, Caesar included, belongs to God. Most mistakes Christians make about government come from reading only one column. Some treat the ruler as if he were owed everything. Others treat him as if he were owed nothing.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. Four passages are read through here: Israel asking for a king (1 Samuel 8), the law for Israel's king (Deuteronomy 17), the apostles before the council in Jerusalem (Acts 4), and the nations coming to learn God's ways (Isaiah 2). Other passages that speak to government are quoted for a verse or two, and each is linked to the study that reads it in full. This page does not tell you how to vote, does not endorse any party, and does not judge any nation's laws. It is not legal advice. The history of how churches and governments have related, from the Reformation to modern constitutions, belongs to the Academic study of the Reformation [check: exact title and link of the Academic Reform study].

## 01 A king like all the nations

**Main readings:** 1 Samuel 8:4–22

Hook: They asked for a king. God gave them one, and told them what it would cost.

Israel had no king at this point. Since the time of Moses, God had raised up leaders called judges to rescue the people in times of trouble. The last of them was Samuel, a prophet, someone who speaks God's message to the people. Now Samuel was old and his sons were corrupt (1 Samuel 8:3 tells how). So the elders came with a request: "Now appoint a king to judge us like all the other nations" (1 Samuel 8:5).

Samuel was displeased, and he prayed. God's answer turns the complaint around: "it is not you they have rejected, but they have rejected Me as their king" (1 Samuel 8:7). The problem was not that Israel wanted order or justice. The problem was the phrase "like all the other nations". God had been their king, and they wanted a king they could see instead.

### "He will take"

God tells Samuel to listen to them, but to "solemnly warn them and show them the manner of the king" (1 Samuel 8:9). Samuel's warning is built on one verb. The king "will take" your sons for his chariots, your daughters for his kitchens, the best of your fields, a tenth of your harvest, your servants and your cattle, and a tenth of your flocks (1 Samuel 8:11–17). The word comes six times. It ends with this: "you yourselves will become his slaves" (1 Samuel 8:17).

> Nevertheless, the people refused to listen to Samuel. “No!” they said. “We must have a king over us. Then we will be like all the other nations, with a king to judge us, to go out before us, and to fight our battles.”

1 Samuel 8:19–20 · BSB

Notice the last phrase. "To fight our battles" was the very thing the LORD had done for Israel since Egypt. They gave a king the job God had been doing.

Then comes the surprise: "Listen to their voice … Appoint a king for them" (1 Samuel 8:22). God grants the request. He does not abolish government because the request was wrong. He gives it, warns about it, and stays King over it.

[read] Two things are true together here. Government is not evil in itself; God appoints this king. But power tends to take, and the more a people expects from a ruler, the more the ruler will take. This passage teaches you to be honest about rulers without despising them. It also warns against handing any ruler the trust that belongs to God.

[open] Jewish teachers have long asked how this chapter fits with Deuteronomy 17, where a king seems to be allowed or even expected. The Talmud records a debate. One rabbi held that appointing a king was a command for Israel once it entered the land. Another held that the law only anticipated the people's complaints. [check: Babylonian Talmud, Sanhedrin 20b, the names (Rabbi Yehudah, Rabbi Nehorai) and the exact positions.] Christian readers have divided in similar ways. Most read the sin of 1 Samuel 8 as being in the motive, "like all the other nations", rather than in kingship itself. The text gives that reading good support, since God Himself appoints the king.

Limits: The passage does not give a rule on tax rates. It does not condemn every monarchy, and it does not prove that any particular government today is good or bad. It describes Israel's king and Israel's sin.

**Consider:** What does God say the people have really rejected? What is the one verb Samuel keeps repeating, and what does it teach about power?

**Reflect:** What are you hoping a leader or a government will do for you that only God can do?

## 02 A king under the book

**Main readings:** Deuteronomy 17:14–20

Hook: Israel's king had to sit on his throne with a scroll in his hand.

Deuteronomy is Moses' last address to Israel before they enter the land. In it, Moses looks ahead to the day when they will say, "Let us set a king over us like all the nations around us" (Deuteronomy 17:14). It is the same phrase the elders will use in 1 Samuel 8. The law does not forbid a king. It fences him in.

First, God chooses him, and he must be "from among your brothers" (Deuteronomy 17:15). The king is a brother first and a ruler second.

Then come three limits. He "must not acquire many horses" (military power), "must not take many wives" (alliances and pride; marriages to foreign princesses sealed treaties), and "must not accumulate for himself large amounts of silver and gold" (wealth) (Deuteronomy 17:16–17). These are the three temptations of every strong ruler: armies, alliances and riches.

### The king's own copy

> When he is seated on his royal throne, he must write for himself a copy of this instruction on a scroll in the presence of the Levitical priests. It is to remain with him, and he is to read from it all the days of his life, so that he may learn to fear the LORD his God by carefully observing all the words of this instruction and these statutes.

Deuteronomy 17:18–19 · BSB

The word translated "instruction" is the Hebrew *torah*, God's teaching given through Moses. Jewish tradition took this literally. The Mishnah says the king writes a Torah scroll of his own [check: Mishnah Sanhedrin 2:4, wording on the king's scroll]. The aim is stated plainly: "Then his heart will not be exalted above his countrymen" (Deuteronomy 17:20).

[read] This is the oldest seed in Scripture of an idea later generations would call the rule of law. The ruler is under the same law as the people, he reads it every day, and it keeps him from thinking he is above them. Israel's history shows how much it mattered. Solomon gathered horses, wives and gold on a vast scale, and his heart turned away (1 Kings 10–11 tells the story).

[read] Christians also read this passage forward. Israel's kings failed the test, and the New Testament presents Jesus as the King who kept God's law perfectly. Read more in *Christ in the Old Testament*.

Limits: This is Israel's law for Israel's king. It is not a constitution for a modern country. How the law of Moses applies to Christians is a question the site's confession, the Baptist Faith and Message 2000, does not settle, and Baptists answer it in more than one way. Read more in *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice*. What carries over plainly is the principle: no ruler stands above God, or above the people as if he were not one of them.

**Consider:** What three things must the king not gather, and why might each be dangerous? What is the stated purpose of the scroll?

**Reflect:** If you hold any authority, at work, at home or in a church, what keeps your heart from being "exalted above" the people you serve?

## 03 Give to Caesar: what government is for

**Main readings:** Mark 12:17; Romans 13:1–7; 1 Peter 2:13–17; 1 Timothy 2:1–2; Jeremiah 29:7

Hook: Paul and Peter wrote under emperors who would one day kill them. Both said: submit, honour, pray.

This part does not walk these passages. Each is read in full in another study, and is quoted here for the line this study needs.

Jesus' answer about the tax was given when He was handed a Roman coin stamped with Caesar's face (Mark 12:15–16 tells the scene). Many readers have drawn a further point from it. The coin bears Caesar's image, but people bear God's image (Genesis 1:27). So the coin can go to Caesar, but the person belongs to God. Jesus does not spell this out. It is an interpretation, but a widely held one. Read more in *Encounters with Jesus*.

Paul writes the New Testament's fullest paragraph on government:

> Everyone must submit himself to the governing authorities, for there is no authority except that which is from God. The authorities that exist have been appointed by God.

Romans 13:1 · BSB

He says what government is for: the ruler "is God’s servant for your good", and "he does not carry the sword in vain" (Romans 13:4). Government exists to restrain wrong and protect the good. So Christians pay "taxes to whom taxes are due", and give "honor to whom honor is due" (Romans 13:7). Read more in *Faith in daily life*.

Peter says the same, and adds a reason:

> Submit yourselves for the Lord’s sake to every human institution, whether to the king as the supreme authority, or to governors as those sent by him to punish those who do wrong and to praise those who do right.

1 Peter 2:13–14 · BSB

"For the Lord’s sake". The Christian's obedience to rulers is part of obeying God, not a rival to it. And Peter keeps both columns in one line: "fear God, honor the king" (1 Peter 2:17). Fear goes to God, and honour goes to the king. Read more in *Faith in daily life*.

Christians also pray for rulers:

> First of all, then, I urge that petitions, prayers, intercessions, and thanksgiving be offered for everyone— for kings and all those in authority—so that we may lead tranquil and quiet lives in all godliness and dignity.

1 Timothy 2:1–2 · BSB

Read more in *Prayer across Scripture: learning to pray*.

Centuries earlier, Jeremiah wrote to Jews carried off to Babylon, the empire that had destroyed their temple:

> Seek the prosperity of the city to which I have sent you as exiles. Pray to the LORD on its behalf, for if it prospers, you too will prosper.”

Jeremiah 29:7 · BSB

Read more in *Reading the Bible well: where to start and how to keep going*.

[read] Put together, these passages give government a real but limited job. It is to punish wrongdoing, protect those who do right, and keep the kind of peace in which people can live quietly. Christians respond by submitting, paying, honouring, praying and working for the good of the place where they live, even when it is not their own and its rulers do not share their faith.

[care] "Submit" in these passages never means "approve of everything". Most scholars date Romans to the reign of the emperor Nero [check: date of Romans and Nero's reign], and Peter wrote to believers facing slander and suffering. Neither apostle was naïve about rulers. And neither passage tells anyone to stay where they are being harmed. Jesus told His disciples that when they were persecuted in one town they should flee to the next (Matthew 10:23).

Limits: These passages describe what government is *for*. They do not promise that every ruler will do it. Nor do they make every command right. For that, the next part.

**Consider:** What does Paul say a ruler is for? Why does Peter say Christians submit "for the Lord’s sake"?

**Reflect:** When did you last pray by name for someone who governs you, especially someone you did not vote for?

## 04 We must obey God rather than men

**Main readings:** Acts 4:1–22

Hook: Two fishermen, one healed man, and a council that cannot deny what happened.

Just before this chapter, Peter and John healed a man who had been lame from birth, at the gate of the temple in Jerusalem, and then preached to the crowd (Acts 3). Now they are arrested.

The men who arrest them are named carefully: "the priests and the captain of the temple guard and the Sadducees" (Acts 4:1). The Sadducees were a priestly party in Jerusalem. They did not believe in the resurrection of the dead (Acts 23:8 says so), the teaching that God will raise the dead to bodily life. So they were "greatly disturbed" that the apostles were "proclaiming in Jesus the resurrection of the dead" (Acts 4:2). The next day Peter and John stand before the Sanhedrin, the highest Jewish council in Jerusalem, made up of priests, elders and scholars of the law (Acts 4:5–6, 15).

### The question and the answer

The council asks, "By what power or what name did you do this?" (Acts 4:7). Peter, "filled with the Holy Spirit" (the Spirit of God, whom Christians confess as fully God), answers. He begins respectfully: "Rulers and elders of the people!" (Acts 4:8). He calls the healing what it was, "a kind service to a man who was lame" (Acts 4:9). He names Jesus as the one who healed him, quotes Psalm 118 about the rejected stone, and says: "Salvation exists in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given to men by which we must be saved" (Acts 4:12).

[care] Peter says "whom you crucified" to the particular council that had handed Jesus over (Acts 4:10). He is speaking to those men about that act. He does not mean the Jewish people as a whole. Peter and John were Jews, and so were the thousands in Jerusalem who believed (Acts 4:4). Later in this chapter, the believers name Herod, Pilate, the Gentiles and the peoples of Israel together (Acts 4:27). The Christian confession is that Christ died for the sins of all people, our own among them. No people bears a special guilt for His death.

The council marvels at "the boldness of Peter and John", because they were "unschooled, ordinary men", and they recognise "that these men had been with Jesus" (Acts 4:13). The healed man is standing right there. "We cannot deny it", they admit (Acts 4:16). So they choose the one thing in their power: "commanded them not to speak or teach at all in the name of Jesus" (Acts 4:18).

> But Peter and John replied, “Judge for yourselves whether it is right in God’s sight to listen to you rather than God. For we cannot stop speaking about what we have seen and heard.”

Acts 4:19–20 · BSB

They are threatened again and released (Acts 4:21). In the next chapter, arrested a second time, Peter puts the principle in one sentence:

> But Peter and the other apostles replied, “We must obey God rather than men.

Acts 5:29 · BSB

### The second column, drawn sharply

Here the two columns meet head-on. The council had real authority, and Peter addressed it as such. But it commanded what God forbids: silence about Jesus, whom God had sent them to proclaim. When the columns collide, God's column wins.

Daniel and his friends had stood on the same ground in Babylon. Commanded to bow to a golden statue, three young men answered:

> If the God whom we serve exists, then He is able to deliver us from the blazing fiery furnace and from your hand, O king. But even if He does not, let it be known to you, O king, that we will not serve your gods or worship the golden statue you have set up.”

Daniel 3:17–18 · BSB

And when a law forbade prayer to anyone but the king, Daniel "got down on his knees, prayed, and gave thanks to his God, just as he had done before" (Daniel 6:10). Read more in *Reading Daniel and Revelation*.

[read] Look at how these believers disobeyed. They did not riot, take up weapons, plot against the rulers or insult them. They spoke respectfully, refused openly, and accepted the consequences. Daniel did not hide his prayers; he kept the windows open. Peter left the outcome with God. "But even if He does not" is the heart of it. Faithfulness does not depend on rescue.

Limits: This passage is about a ruler commanding what God forbids, or forbidding what God commands. It does not give anyone permission to disobey whatever law they dislike, or to call every unwelcome policy persecution.

**Consider:** What exactly did the council command, and why could the apostles not obey it? How did they disobey, and what did they not do?

**Reflect:** Is there anything you are keeping quiet about, not because a law forbids it, but because you fear what people will think?

## 05 God alone is Lord of the conscience: religious liberty

**Main readings:** Acts 4:19–20; Mark 12:17; Daniel 3:16–18

Hook: A faith that is forced is not faith at all.

Your conscience is your inner sense of what is right and wrong before God. Baptists have always believed that no government may rule over it. On this the site's confession speaks plainly, and so does this study.

The Baptist Faith and Message 2000, article XVII (religious liberty), says "God alone is Lord of the conscience", that church and state should be separate, and that "A free church in a free state is the Christian ideal." The state should protect every person's freedom to worship. It should not favour one religious group, punish another for its beliefs, or pay for any church's work [check: confirm paraphrase against the current text of article XVII at bfm.sbc.net].

### Why, from Scripture

Peter's words to the council are the root of it: "Judge for yourselves whether it is right in God’s sight to listen to you rather than God" (Acts 4:19). If God commands a person directly, no ruler can stand between them.

Two Baptist phrases name what this means.

**Soul competency** means that each person is able to answer to God directly, and must. No priest, church or government can believe for you, repent for you, or be judged in your place. To repent means to turn from sin back to God. Scripture says each of us will give an account of ourselves to God (Romans 14:12). The phrase is closely tied to the Baptist theologian E. Y. Mullins and his book *The Axioms of Religion* (1908) [check: Mullins' exact wording, "the competency of the soul in religion"].

**The priesthood of believers** means that every Christian comes to God through Jesus Christ alone, with no other go-between. In Israel, priests offered sacrifices on the people's behalf. The New Testament calls all believers together "a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9; Read more in *Life together: belonging to a church*). This does not mean that every Christian may do as he pleases. It means each one has direct access to God, and all are accountable to one another in the church.

The confession's preamble holds the two together, honouring both principles while affirming "our accountability to each other under the Word of God" [check: exact wording of the 2000 preamble on soul competency and the priesthood of believers].

[read] Religious liberty follows from the gospel itself. The gospel is the good news that God forgives and saves sinners through faith in Jesus Christ. Faith is trust given freely from the heart (Romans 10:9–10), and a sword can make a person bow but cannot make them believe. A government that forces worship produces only pretence. So Baptists defend religious freedom for everyone: for Christians of other traditions, for people of other religions, and for those who have none. We do this while saying plainly that salvation is found in Christ alone (Acts 4:12). Freedom to believe also means freedom to be wrong, and to be persuaded rather than compelled.

[source:helwys] The early English Baptist Thomas Helwys wrote to King James I, in *A Short Declaration of the Mystery of Iniquity* (1612), that men's religion to God is between God and themselves, that the king will not answer for it, and that the king may not be judge between God and man [check: Helwys' original wording and spelling before quoting directly]. He was imprisoned and died in prison [check: date and place of Helwys' death]. Later Baptists such as Roger Williams in Rhode Island and John Leland in Virginia carried the same conviction. That history is told in the Academic study of the Reformation [check: exact title].

[other] Other Christian traditions have reached their own positions in their own words. The Westminster Confession of Faith (1646), a Presbyterian and Reformed confession, originally gave the civil ruler a duty to see "that all blasphemies and heresies be suppressed" [check: wording of Westminster Confession 23.3, original 1646 text]. American Presbyterians revised that chapter in 1788 [check: year of the American revision]. The Roman Catholic Church, at the Second Vatican Council, declared in *Dignitatis Humanae* (1965) "that the human person has a right to religious freedom" [check: wording of the official English translation, section 2].

[care] If you live where your faith is against the law, or where changing your religion puts you in danger, this page cannot judge your situation from a distance. Scripture honours both those who stood firm and those who fled to safety (Matthew 10:23; Acts 9:25). Seek out believers you can trust, and pray for wisdom (James 1:5).

Limits: Separating church and state does not mean keeping faith out of public life. The confession's article XV (the Christian and the social order) calls Christians to work for righteousness in society. What separation means is that the church does not wield the state's power, and the state does not rule the church's faith.

**Consider:** Why can faith not be forced? What do soul competency and the priesthood of believers each mean, and what do they not mean?

**Reflect:** Would you defend the freedom of someone whose beliefs you think are badly wrong? Why does your own freedom depend on theirs?

## 06 Swords into plowshares: peace and war

**Main readings:** Isaiah 2:2–4

Hook: One day, a nation's weapons will be melted down for farming tools.

Isaiah was a prophet in Jerusalem in the eighth century before Christ, in a time of threats from the great empires. In the middle of his warnings he sees something else:

> Then He will judge between the nations and arbitrate for many peoples. They will beat their swords into plowshares and their spears into pruning hooks. Nation will no longer take up the sword against nation, nor train anymore for war.

Isaiah 2:4 · BSB

Read the whole movement. "In the last days" the mountain of the LORD's house will be raised up and "all nations will stream to it" (Isaiah 2:2). The nations come to learn: "He will teach us His ways so that we may walk in His paths" (Isaiah 2:3). "The law will go forth from Zion". Here "law" is *torah* again, the same instruction Israel's king was to copy and read every day in Deuteronomy 17. Peace comes because the nations learn God's ways and accept God as their judge. Only then are the weapons remade. The prophet Micah gives almost the same words (Micah 4:1–3).

[read] In Jewish reading, this is one of the great promises of the messianic age, the time when God's anointed king reigns. Then the nations will learn God's teaching from Jerusalem and war will end. Many Jewish readers point out that the world is plainly not yet like this, and they wait for it.

[open] Christians read the passage as pointing to Christ, but they differ on how and when it comes true. Some see it beginning as the gospel went out from Jerusalem to the nations (Luke 24:47), and being completed when Christ returns. Some Baptists, who are premillennial, expect a literal reign of Christ on earth when it will be fulfilled. Others, who are amillennial or postmillennial, read it of the new creation or of the gospel's spread. The confession affirms Christ's personal, visible return but names no timetable (article X, last things). This study does not choose. Read more in *The hope to come*.

### Until that day

The swords are not yet beaten into plowshares. So what should Christians do about war now? Here the site's confession speaks, but only so far. Article XVI (peace and war) says Christians should seek peace with all people "on principles of righteousness", and that the gospel is the true remedy for the spirit of war [check: confirm wording of article XVI]. It does not choose between the two main Christian answers. Neither does this study.

Jesus' words in the Sermon on the Mount are at the centre of the question:

> But I tell you, love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you,

Matthew 5:44 · BSB

Just before, He says "not to resist an evil person" and to turn the other cheek (Matthew 5:39). Read more in *Following Jesus and the Sermon on the Mount*.

**The just-war tradition.** Most Southern Baptists hold this view [check: a Southern Baptist source stating the just-war position, such as a convention resolution]. It holds that war is always tragic but may sometimes be right, as a last resort, to protect the innocent against grave wrong. It reads Jesus' words in Matthew 5 as governing personal revenge. It reads Romans 13:4, the ruler who "does not carry the sword in vain", as governing what the state may do. The tradition was developed by Augustine and later Thomas Aquinas [check: works and sections where each sets out just-war reasoning]. Its usual tests are a just cause, lawful authority, right intention, last resort, a reasonable hope of success, force in proportion to the wrong, and sparing those who are not fighting. Older Baptists held it. The Second London Baptist Confession (1677/1689), chapter 24, says that rulers "may lawfully now, under the New Testament, wage war upon just and necessary occasion" [check: exact wording of 2LBC 24.2]. The Lutheran Augsburg Confession (1530), article 16, says Christians may "engage in just wars" and serve as soldiers [check: wording in the English translation used].

**Pacifism.** Pacifism is the conviction that Christians should not take part in war or use deadly force. It is a minority view among Baptists. It reads Matthew 5:38–48 as Christ's command to His followers in every setting, and points to Isaiah 2 as the shape of the kingdom Christians already belong to. It is the historic conviction of the Anabaptist peace churches: the Mennonites, the Amish, the Hutterites and the Church of the Brethren. The Anabaptists were sixteenth-century Christians who, like Baptists after them, baptized only believers. In the Schleitheim Confession (1527), an early Anabaptist statement, they wrote that "the sword is ordained of God outside the perfection of Christ": God gives it to rulers, but Christ's followers do not take it up [check: wording of Schleitheim article 6 in the English translation used]. A modern statement is the *Confession of Faith in a Mennonite Perspective* (1995), article 22 [check: title and wording of article 22].

[read] Both views take Scripture seriously, and both agree on a great deal. War is never good in itself. Christians must love their enemies and pray for them. Revenge is forbidden to the believer. Peace is the goal, and Isaiah 2 is the end of the story. They differ over what a Christian may do before that day comes.

Limits: Isaiah 2 is a promise about God's doing, not a policy for any one nation. And neither view of war gives anyone licence to hate the other side, or to doubt the faith of Christians who hold the other view.

**Consider:** What has to happen in Isaiah 2:3 before the weapons are remade in 2:4? Where do the just-war view and pacifism agree, and where do they part?

## Questions readers bring

### Must I obey a law I believe is wrong?

[read] Usually, yes. Romans 13 and 1 Peter 2 ask Christians to submit to laws that are inconvenient, costly or unwise, and to pay taxes to imperfect governments. The line in Acts 4 is narrower: you refuse when a ruler commands what God forbids or forbids what God commands. When you must refuse, the pattern of Peter and Daniel is to do it openly, respectfully, without violence, and with the consequences accepted. Where a law allows you to work for change by lawful means, using them is a way of honouring both columns.

### Should a Christian take part in politics?

[read] Scripture asks Christians to pray for rulers (1 Timothy 2:1–2) and to seek the good of their city (Jeremiah 29:7). Where citizens choose their leaders, voting and serving can be ways of doing both. The confession's article XV calls Christians to work for justice and the good of society. But Scripture does not tell Christians which party to join. No party is the kingdom of God, God's rule over all and over those who willingly own Him as King. And the warning of 1 Samuel 8 still stands: do not ask a ruler to be your saviour.

### Should my country be officially Christian?

[read] The site's confession answers plainly: church and state should be separate, and "A free church in a free state is the Christian ideal" (article XVII). Baptists hold this because faith cannot be forced and because the church's power is the word and the Spirit, not the sword. A Christian may hope that many in their nation will come to faith, and may bring Christian convictions into public life. But Baptists do not ask the state to enforce worship or favour one church. Other traditions have held, and some still hold, a closer bond between church and state, as part 05 shows in their own words.

## Two columns

Way: Here is the habit to take to the next passage about rulers. Find the first column: what is the ruler rightly owed? Usually it is obedience, taxes, honour and prayer. Find the second column: what belongs to God alone? Worship, conscience, the gospel and the final trust of your heart. Ask whether the two columns are at peace in this passage or in collision. Then notice how God's people behave in either case. They are respectful, prayerful, unafraid, and willing to say "even if He does not".

Close: Israel wanted a king to fight its battles, and God warned them what kings take. Moses put a scroll in the king's hand so his heart would not rise above his brothers. Peter and John stood before rulers they honoured and told them, kindly and plainly, that they could not stop speaking. Isaiah saw the day when the nations would come to learn God's ways and melt down their swords. Through all of it, one thing does not change: every ruler answers to a greater King.

Invite: If you have never trusted that King yourself, start there. Jesus Christ died for sinners and rose from the dead, and "there is no other name under heaven given to men by which we must be saved" (Acts 4:12). Turn from your sin and trust Him as Lord. No government can give you that, and none can take it away.

Question: In your own life this week, what are you giving to Caesar that belongs to God? And what do you owe the people who govern you that you have been holding back?
