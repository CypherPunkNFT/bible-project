# Learning to discern

Concepts: Test · Search · Weigh

Motto: Hear the claim. / Open the book.

Lead: To *discern* means to tell things apart: true from false, wise from foolish, what matters most from what can wait. The New Testament asks every believer to do this, not only pastors and scholars. This study teaches one habit. Before you ask whether a speaker sounds convincing, ask what they say about Jesus, and then open the passage to see whether it is so.

> Now the Bereans were more noble-minded than the Thessalonians, for they received the message with great eagerness and examined the Scriptures every day to see if these teachings were true.

Acts 17:11 · BSB

Frame: The first churches had no printed Bibles on their shelves. Travelling teachers came through their towns, some sent by the apostles (the men Jesus chose and sent to carry his message), some not. Letters came telling them how to tell the difference. Those letters keep returning to the same habit, and it has two halves. First, test the message, not the messenger: a famous name, a confident voice or a claim of a vision does not make a teaching true. Second, test it against Scripture, read where it stands and not torn out of its paragraph. The same letters add a third check that is easy to miss. They test the person doing the testing.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. The study walks five passages: 1 John 4:1–6, Galatians 1:6–12, Acts 17:10–12, James 3:13–18 and 2 Peter 2:1–3. It quotes a verse or two from three more that other studies walk. It does not survey every religious group or every false teaching. It names two groups, the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints and Jehovah's Witnesses, because readers most often meet them at the door and because what they teach about God touches the centre of the faith; each is quoted in its own words. Other passages on discernment, among them 1 Thessalonians 5:19–22, Hebrews 5:11–14, Proverbs 18:13–17 and Philippians 1:9–11, are pointed to here, not walked. This study reads Scripture; it is not professional or legal advice.

## 01 Test the spirits: what does it say about Jesus?

**Main readings:** 1 John 4:1–6

Hook: Many voices claim to speak for God. John gives his readers one question to ask first.

### Do not believe every spirit

John writes to churches that have just watched a group of teachers walk out (1 John 2:19). The ones who stayed are unsettled. Who was right?

> Beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits to see whether they are from God. For many false prophets have gone out into the world.

1 John 4:1 · BSB

Two words need explaining. In this passage a *spirit* stands for the power behind a message: a teacher who claims to speak by the Spirit of God is putting forward a spirit to be tested. A *false prophet* is someone who claims to speak for God when God has not sent them. John does not tell his readers to believe nothing, and he does not tell them to believe whoever sounds most spiritual. He tells them to test.

### A test with content

> By this you will know the Spirit of God: Every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God, and every spirit that does not confess Jesus is not from God. This is the spirit of the antichrist, which you have heard is coming and which is already in the world at this time.

1 John 4:2–3 · BSB

*Confess* here means to say openly and agree with. *In the flesh* means in a real human body. Christians call this the *incarnation*: God the Son becoming a real man without ceasing to be God. *Antichrist* means one who opposes Christ, and John says the spirit of it is "already in the world at this time" (1 John 4:3). What the Bible teaches about a final antichrist belongs to *The hope to come*.

Look at what the test asks. It is not "Is this teacher impressive?" or "Did something powerful happen in the meeting?" It is "What does this teaching say about Jesus?" Who he is comes first.

Many readers think John's opponents denied that the divine Christ truly became a human being and truly died. Some early teachers did say something like this, and the later name for the idea is *docetism*, from a Greek word for "seeming", because it held that Christ only seemed to have a body. That is a reconstruction of the background, and the letter does not name its opponents. The test works without knowing exactly who they were.

[care] The confession is not a password. Someone can say the words "Jesus Christ has come in the flesh" and mean something John would not recognise. So listen for what the words mean: whether this Jesus is the eternal Son who became man, or someone else wearing his name.

### Greater is He who is in you

Then John turns from the test to the people taking it: "You, little children, are from God and have overcome them, because greater is He who is in you than he who is in the world" (1 John 4:4). Discernment is not a contest of cleverness that the sharpest person wins. The readers stand on what God has done in them.

[read] Verse 6 says "Whoever knows God listens to us". Readers take this *us* two ways. Some hear all faithful believers together. More commonly, because the letter opens with what "we" have heard and seen and touched (1 John 1:1–3), it is read as the apostles who saw Jesus, whose witness now stands written in the New Testament. This study follows the second reading: the test is whether a teaching listens to the apostles' witness, not to us.

The next paragraph, 1 John 4:7–21, turns to love. Read more in *God: who He is and what He does*.

**Consider:** What question does John tell his readers to ask about every spirit? What does verse 4 say about the people doing the testing?

**Reflect:** Think of a spiritual claim you have heard lately. Leaving aside how confident the speaker was, what did it actually say about Jesus?

## 02 The good news is not ours to change

**Main readings:** Galatians 1:6–12

Hook: Paul puts himself under his own warning. And then he adds the angels.

### A different gospel, which is not a gospel

*Gospel* means "good news". In the New Testament it is the message that God forgives and accepts people through Jesus' death and rising again, received by trusting him. *Grace* is God's kindness given to people who have not earned it. Paul writes to churches in Galatia, in what is now central Turkey, that he planted himself.

He skips his usual thanksgiving and goes straight to the trouble: "I am amazed how quickly you are deserting the One who called you by the grace of Christ and are turning to a different gospel—which is not even a gospel" (Galatians 1:6–7).

Later in the letter it becomes clear what the troublers were saying. Gentile believers (people who were not Jewish) must also be circumcised and keep the law of Moses before God would count them as his people (Galatians 5:2–4; 6:12–13). In Jewish life, circumcision is the sign of God's covenant with Abraham, his binding promise, and Jewish families have kept it with joy for thousands of years. Paul himself was a Jew. His quarrel is not with Jews keeping the covenant sign. It is with the claim that people from the nations must add it to Christ in order to be accepted by God. That, he says, turns good news into something else.

### Not the messenger, the message

> But even if we or an angel from heaven should preach a gospel contrary to the one we preached to you, let him be under a curse!

Galatians 1:8 · BSB

*Under a curse* translates a Greek word, *anathema*, for something handed over to God's judgement. Paul says it twice (Galatians 1:8–9), so no one can miss it.

Notice who is not exempt. Paul is not. An angel is not. The test is not who brings a message or how it arrived. It is whether the message agrees with the gospel "you received" (Galatians 1:9). A later message is measured against the earlier one, not the other way round.

Paul then answers the charge that he is a people-pleaser: "If I were still trying to please men, I would not be a servant of Christ" (Galatians 1:10). He says his gospel "was not devised by man" but came "by revelation from Jesus Christ" (Galatians 1:11–12). *Revelation* means God making known what people could not find out for themselves.

[read] This is Paul's account of his own calling as an apostle. It does not mean anyone today who claims a revelation has the same standing. Galatians puts every later claim, however it arrives, under the test of the gospel already received.

[care] The severity of this passage is for the gospel itself. It is not a weapon for every disagreement about music, church schedules or how often to take the Lord's Supper. Before you reach for Galatians 1, ask whether the dispute really touches how a person is made right with God.

Read more in *The good news: how a person is made right with God* and *Saved by grace: how God forgives and changes us*.

**Consider:** Why does Paul include himself and angels in the warning? What makes the other message "not even a gospel"?

**Reflect:** If someone told you that trusting Christ is not enough and something must be added, what would you want to ask them first?

## 03 Searching the Scriptures every day

**Main readings:** Acts 17:10–12

Hook: They were eager to listen, and they checked everything. Luke calls that noble.

### In the synagogue at Berea

Paul and Silas have been sent out of Thessalonica by night, and they go west to Berea, a town in Macedonia, in northern Greece. "On arriving there, they went into the Jewish synagogue" (Acts 17:10).

A *synagogue* is the Jewish house of prayer and study. On the Sabbath, the seventh day of rest, Jews gathered there to pray, to hear the Torah (the five books of Moses) and the Prophets read aloud, and to hear them explained. The Scriptures the Bereans searched were these, what Christians call the Old Testament. Paul came with a claim about their own Scriptures: that the Messiah, the promised anointed King, had to suffer and rise, and that Jesus is he (Acts 17:2–3).

### Eager, and careful

> Now the Bereans were more noble-minded than the Thessalonians, for they received the message with great eagerness and examined the Scriptures every day to see if these teachings were true.

Acts 17:11 · BSB

*Noble-minded* describes a generous, open way of hearing. Two things stand side by side. They "received the message with great eagerness": they were not suspicious for the sake of it. And they "examined the Scriptures every day": they did not take Paul's word for it, even though he was an apostle. Luke praises both together. The result: "many of them believed" (Acts 17:12).

So the noble listeners in this story are Jewish men and women in a synagogue, holding a new message up against the Scriptures they already loved. That is the habit this study is teaching.

[read] The Bereans checked an apostle against Scripture. No teacher since stands higher than that, so no teacher today is above being checked.

### Reading the verse in its paragraph

The verse does not give a complete method of Bible reading. But some practices follow naturally from it, and these are this study's application, not commands of Luke. When someone gives you a verse, read the whole paragraph around it. Ask who is speaking, to whom, and about what. Ask whether the conclusion actually follows from the words. Read again tomorrow if you need to; the Bereans searched "every day".

Paul later wrote to the Thessalonians, the same city he had just left, telling them not to treat prophecies with contempt but to test everything and hold on to what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:20–21). Openness and testing belong together.

Paul's speech in Athens a few verses later, Acts 17:22–31, belongs to another study. Read more in *God: who He is and what He does*. For how to read a passage in its setting, read *Reading the Bible well: where to start and how to keep going*; for what Scripture claims about itself, read *What the Bible says about itself*.

**Consider:** What two responses does Luke praise in the Bereans? Whose teaching were they checking?

**Reflect:** Take one verse you have heard quoted to support a claim. Read the ten verses around it. Does the claim still stand?

## 04 Wisdom from above: testing the one who tests

**Main readings:** James 3:13–18

Hook: You can be right about the teaching and wrong in your heart. James checks both.

### Shown by conduct

James asks a question that sounds like an invitation to boast: "Who is wise and understanding among you?" Then he removes the boast: "Let him show it by his good conduct, by deeds done in the humility that comes from wisdom" (James 3:13). Wisdom, for James, is not a reputation. It is a way of living that others can see.

He names its counterfeit. "But if you harbor bitter jealousy and selfish ambition in your hearts, do not boast in it or deny the truth" (James 3:14). Such wisdom is "earthly, unspiritual, demonic" (James 3:15). *Unspiritual* means it comes from mere human nature and not from God's Spirit; *demonic* means it resembles the work of evil spirits. Its fruit is "disorder and every evil practice" (James 3:16).

### What wisdom from above looks like

> But the wisdom from above is first of all pure, then peace-loving, gentle, accommodating, full of mercy and good fruit, impartial, and sincere.

James 3:17 · BSB

*First of all pure*: truth and holiness come first, so peace is never bought by pretending a falsehood is true. *Then peace-loving, gentle*: purity does not license harshness. *Accommodating* describes someone who can be reasoned with, who can yield when shown to be wrong. "Peacemakers who sow in peace reap the fruit of righteousness" (James 3:18).

### Pointing the test inward

This passage turns discernment around. The others ask, "Is this teaching true?" James asks, "What is driving me as I judge it?" Envy of a popular teacher, the wish to win an argument, pleasure in someone else's fall: James calls these earthly, even when the conclusion they reach is correct.

Paul says something close to this in Ephesians, about a church that grows steady together:

> Then we will no longer be infants, tossed about by the waves and carried around by every wind of teaching and by the clever cunning of men in their deceitful scheming. Instead, speaking the truth in love, we will in all things grow up into Christ Himself, who is the head.

Ephesians 4:14–15 · BSB

Steadiness against false teaching comes with "speaking the truth in love", inside a church, not alone. Read more in *The church and its calling*. Hebrews adds that discernment is trained by practice, like a muscle (Hebrews 5:14); no one is born with it, and no one finishes learning it.

[read] Gentleness does not make truth unimportant, and being right does not make envy wise. James will not let either excuse the other.

**Consider:** How does James say real wisdom becomes visible? Which of the qualities in verse 17 concern how a person treats others?

**Reflect:** Before you next correct someone, ask: can I still be corrected myself? What would a gentle, accommodating answer sound like?

## 05 False teachers: what they deny and what they want

**Main readings:** 2 Peter 2:1–3

Hook: They do not knock and announce themselves. Peter says they slip in.

### Among you

> Now there were also false prophets among the people, just as there will be false teachers among you. They will secretly introduce destructive heresies, even denying the Master who bought them—bringing swift destruction on themselves.

2 Peter 2:1 · BSB

Peter looks back to Israel's history, where false prophets spoke inside God's people, and says the church should expect the same. A *heresy* is a teaching that denies a central truth of the faith and divides people from it. Peter's portrait has several marks, and they are worth reading slowly.

- **They are inside.** "Among you." False teaching usually comes from people who use Christian words.
- **They come quietly.** "Secretly introduce". Rarely does anyone stand up and say, "I am about to deny Christ."
- **They deny the Master.** *Master* is a word for an owner. Christ owns his people because he paid for them; "bought" points to the price of his death.
- **They gather followers.** "Many will follow in their depravity, and because of them the way of truth will be defamed" (2 Peter 2:2). Outsiders judge the faith by them.
- **They want something.** "In their greed, these false teachers will exploit you with deceptive words" (2 Peter 2:3). Money, power, control or admiration.

And Peter's last word is not fear but justice: "their destruction does not sleep" (2 Peter 2:3). God has not lost track of them.

[open] Who are "the Master who bought them"? Baptists read this two ways. Some Calvinist Baptists, who hold that Christ died to secure the salvation of the elect in particular, read *bought* as the owner's claim on everyone, or as the purchase these teachers professed. Non-Calvinist Baptists read it as showing that Christ's death was for all, including those who finally reject him. Both affirm the confession's article V (God's purpose of grace) and agree that those truly saved "will never fall away". This study does not choose. Read more in *Chosen and kept: can I be sure?*

### By their fruit

Jesus gives a test that sits beside Peter's:

> Beware of false prophets. They come to you in sheep’s clothing, but inwardly they are ravenous wolves.

Matthew 7:15 · BSB

"By their fruit you will recognize them" (Matthew 7:16). Read more in *Following Jesus and the Sermon on the Mount*.

[read] Fruit and doctrine are tested together. A teacher can live a decent life and still teach falsely about Christ; good manners do not make false teaching true. And a sound teacher can sin; one failure does not make someone a false teacher. Peter's portrait is of a pattern: denial of Christ joined to exploitation of people.

[care] Teaching that promises health and riches in return for faith or for giving money is often called *prosperity teaching*, or the Word of Faith movement. The confession this site writes under teaches stewardship, giving "cheerfully, regularly, systematically, proportionately, and liberally" (article XIII), and never wealth as the reward of faith. Prosperity teaching is not the same thing as classical Pentecostal churches, such as the Assemblies of God, which are a separate tradition with their own confession of faith, and the two should not be lumped together.

[care] Greed and control can hurt people badly. If a leader has demanded your money, cut you off from family, or told you that questioning him is questioning God, you are not disloyal to Christ for telling someone you trust. Where a crime has happened, it belongs with the police.

**Consider:** Which marks of false teachers in 2 Peter 2:1–3 concern what they teach, and which concern what they want? Why does it matter that they arise "among you"?

**Reflect:** When you weigh a teacher, do you look at both what they say about Christ and how they treat people? Which of the two do you tend to forget?

## 06 Marks of groups that deny the Trinity or that Jesus is God

**Main readings:** 1 John 4:1–6, carried forward; Galatians 1:6–12, carried forward

Hook: Two neighbours at the door. Both speak warmly of Jesus. Ask what they mean by "God".

### What the Christian faith confesses

The *Trinity* is the Christian name for what the Bible teaches about God: there is one God, and he exists eternally as three persons, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. *Deity* means being God. To say Christ's deity is to say Jesus is not a created being, however exalted, but God the Son, who became man.

The confession this site writes under says, in article II (God), "There is one and only one living and true God", and that "The eternal triune God reveals Himself to us as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, with distinct personal attributes, but without division of nature, essence, or being." Of the Son it says, "Christ is the eternal Son of God." *Triune* means three-in-one. The Nicene Creed, agreed by church councils in 325 and 381 and held by Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant churches alike, calls Christ "true God from true God".

These are not inventions laid on top of the Bible. Scripture says God is one (Deuteronomy 6:4). John's Gospel opens by saying the Word was with God and was God, and then that the Word became flesh (John 1:1, 14). That is why 1 John 4:2 matters so much: the one who "has come in the flesh" came from somewhere. Read more in *Who do you say that I am? Who Jesus really is*, and in the site's articles on the Trinity.

### The Latter-day Saints, in their own words

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, founded by Joseph Smith in 1830, honours Jesus Christ as Saviour and Redeemer, and its members sincerely call themselves Christians. Its first Article of Faith reads: "We believe in God, the Eternal Father, and in His Son, Jesus Christ, and in the Holy Ghost."

Its scriptures explain what that means. Doctrine and Covenants 130:22, one of the church's books of scripture, says: "The Father has a body of flesh and bones as tangible as man's; the Son also; but the Holy Ghost has not a body of flesh and bones, but is a personage of Spirit." The church teaches that Father, Son and Holy Ghost are three separate beings, one in purpose [check: wording of the church's own statement, for example its Gospel Topics entry "Godhead"]. A saying of Lorenzo Snow, a president of the church, is quoted in its own teaching manuals: "As man now is, God once was: As God now is, man may be" [check: exact wording and source in *Teachings of Presidents of the Church: Lorenzo Snow*].

On Scripture, its eighth Article of Faith reads: "We believe the Bible to be the word of God as far as it is translated correctly; we also believe the Book of Mormon to be the word of God."

[read] Here is where the Baptist reading parts from theirs. The Bible teaches one God, not three gods united in purpose, and a God who did not become God (Isaiah 43:10; Psalm 90:2). Galatians 1 teaches that later revelation is measured by the gospel already received, not added to it.

### Jehovah's Witnesses, in their own words

Jehovah's Witnesses, who grew from the Bible Student movement led by Charles Taze Russell in the late 1800s, honour Jesus as God's Son, the Messiah and King, who gave his life as a ransom. They reject the Trinity openly; a booklet they published is titled *Should You Believe in the Trinity?*

Their own Bible translation, the *New World Translation of the Holy Scriptures*, renders John 1:1 this way: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was a god." They teach that Jesus is God's first creation and that before coming to earth he was the archangel Michael [check: wording in their own publications, for example *What Does the Bible Really Teach?* or jw.org]. They describe the holy spirit not as a person but as "God's active force" [check: confirm phrase on jw.org and in the *New World Translation* at Genesis 1:2].

[read] The Baptist reading parts here too. John's Gospel says the Word was God, not a god, and a created Christ is not the Son the confession and the creeds confess. The Holy Spirit in Scripture teaches, speaks and can be grieved, which a force does not do (John 14:26; Ephesians 4:30).

### What to listen for

These marks are drawn from the passages above. They are this site's checklist, not a formula from an apostle.

- **God:** Is God one being, eternally Father, Son and Holy Spirit? Or three separate gods, or one person only?
- **Jesus:** Is he the eternal Son, truly God, who came in the flesh (1 John 4:2)? Or a created being, or a man who became divine?
- **The Holy Spirit:** A person, or a force?
- **Scripture:** Is the Bible the final measure, examined by anyone, as the Bereans did (Acts 17:11)? Or must it be read only through new scriptures, or through one organisation's interpretation?
- **The gospel:** Grace received by trust in Christ (Galatians 1:6), or acceptance earned by adding something?

### Seventh-day Adventists are not in this list

Seventh-day Adventists confess the Trinity. Their Fundamental Belief 2 begins: "There is one God: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, a unity of three coeternal Persons." So they are not among the groups described here. Baptists do differ from Adventists on other matters, such as keeping Saturday as the day of worship and the place they give to the writings of Ellen G. White. Those are disagreements between people who confess the same triune God, and they should be argued as such.

### Neighbours, not targets

The confession's article III (man) says every person "possesses full dignity and is worthy of respect and Christian love", and article XVII (religious liberty) says God alone is Lord of the conscience. Many Latter-day Saints and Jehovah's Witnesses are kind, disciplined and devout. Telling them plainly where we differ is not unkind. Telling them with gentleness and respect is what James 3:17 asks. Read more in *Witness and the nations: telling others about Jesus*.

[care] If you are leaving one of these groups, or any group, you may lose friends, family contact or the only community you have known. That is real grief, and it is not a sign that you are wrong to ask questions. If the loss ever leaves you feeling there is no way forward, or you are thinking of harming yourself, please talk to someone today. ## Questions readers ask

### Isn't it wrong to judge other people?

Jesus warns against judging others while ignoring your own sin (Matthew 7:1–5), and Paul writes:

> Accept him whose faith is weak, without passing judgment on his opinions.

Romans 14:1 · BSB

But the same New Testament commands believers to "test the spirits" (1 John 4:1) and praises the Bereans for checking an apostle (Acts 17:11). The difference is what is being judged and how. Romans 14 is about differences over food and special days, matters of conscience. 1 John 4 and Galatians 1 are about who Jesus is and what the gospel is. Testing a teaching is not condemning a person's heart, which only God sees. And James 3 says how to do it: pure, then gentle. Read more in *Faith in daily life*.

### How do I tell a gospel matter from a disputed one?

Ask what the disagreement touches. Does it change who Jesus is, how a person is made right with God, or whether Scripture is the final word? Then it is a Galatians 1 matter. Is it about practice, conscience or a reading on which faithful Christians who confess the same gospel have long differed? Then Romans 14 speaks:

> So then, let us pursue what leads to peace and to mutual edification.

Romans 14:19 · BSB

*Edification* means building one another up. Some questions take more study before you can tell which kind they are; saying "I don't know yet" is part of discernment.

### Are Latter-day Saints and Jehovah's Witnesses Christians?

Both call themselves Christians, and both should be heard on what they mean by it. This site's answer is that a teaching that denies the Trinity or that Jesus is fully God is not the faith of the New Testament, of the historic creeds or of the Baptist confession, and on that measure these two bodies teach a different faith. That is a judgement about teaching. It is not a judgement on any one person's heart, which belongs to God, and it is no reason to treat anyone with less than respect.

### What if I don't feel able to argue?

You do not need to win. The Bereans did not out-argue Paul; they read. Ask what someone means by "God" and "Jesus", open a passage together, and say honestly what you believe and why. John's comfort stands: "greater is He who is in you than he who is in the world" (1 John 4:4).

## Learning to discern

Way: Here is the habit to carry to the next voice you hear. Test the message, not the messenger. Ask first what it says about Jesus. Hold it up against the gospel you received. Open the passage it quotes and read the whole paragraph, as the Bereans did, every day if you need to. Then turn the test inward: am I judging from envy or from love, and can I still be corrected?

Close: Discernment is not suspicion, and it is not cleverness. It is love for the truth and love for people, held together. The same letter that says "test the spirits" goes on at once to say "let us love one another" (1 John 4:7).

Invite: If you have read this far and are not sure what you believe about Jesus, start where John starts: with him. Read the Gospel of John, and ask who this man is. Trust him as Lord, the eternal Son who came in the flesh for you. Read more in *The good news: how a person is made right with God*.
