# Passage notes: Money, possessions and generosity (63)

Folder: christian-life/money-possessions-and-generosity. Confession anchor: article XIII (stewardship); also article IV (salvation), article X (last things), article XV (the Christian and the social order).

## Deuteronomy 8:11–18 (owned)

**Context.** Moses' address on the plains of Moab before Israel enters the land. Chapter 8 contrasts the wilderness, where Israel depended on God each day (8:1–10, with 8:3 quoted by Jesus in Matthew 4), with the land of plenty. Verses 11–18 warn against forgetting God in prosperity. Verses 19–20 warn of destruction for following other gods.

**Main readings.**
- Plain reading (common to Jewish and Christian interpreters): wealth is God's gift; the danger is pride and forgetting; the cure is remembrance.
- The "covenant" clause in 8:18 ties the power to gain wealth to God's oath to the patriarchs (land and descendants). Prosperity teachers (Word of Faith) quote 8:18 as a promise of individual wealth. The study answers this from the clause itself without quoting any named teacher.

**Links.** *What the Bible says about itself* (27), which quotes 8:3 beside Matthew 4; *The story of the Bible* (55).

## Deuteronomy 14:22–29 (owned)

**Context.** Part of the laws of Deuteronomy 12–26, centred on worship at "the place He will choose". It follows the food laws (14:3–21) and comes before the year of release (15:1–11), which also concerns the poor.

**Main readings.**
- Jewish reading (given first in the study): the rabbinic system of first tithe (Levites, Numbers 18), second tithe (*ma'aser sheni*, eaten in Jerusalem, years 1, 2, 4 and 5) and poor tithe (*ma'aser ani*, years 3 and 6); tithes of produce continue in halakhic form for the land of Israel; *ma'aser kesafim* as a custom of giving a tenth of income to charity.
- Historical-critical scholars debate how Deuteronomy's tithe relates to Numbers 18; the study does not enter this question.
- Christian readings: (a) a moral pattern of joyful, God-fearing giving that cares for ministers and the poor (common ground); (b) evidence for tithing as a continuing standard (the first Baptist reading of the tithe); (c) part of the Mosaic law fulfilled in Christ (the second Baptist reading).

**Links.** *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice* (47), for how the law applies; *The Ten Commandments* (61); *Every life in God's image* (62), for the foreigner, fatherless and widow.

## 1 Chronicles 29:10–19 (owned)

**Context.** The end of David's reign in Chronicles: preparations for the temple (chapters 22–29), the freewill offerings (29:1–9), David's prayer (29:10–19), Solomon's anointing (29:20–25) and David's death (29:26–30).

**Main readings.**
- A prayer of praise establishing God's ownership of all things and stewardship as returning what is His; widely used in Christian (and Jewish) liturgy for offerings. The doxology of 29:11 is echoed in the doxology some manuscripts add to the Lord's Prayer (not stated in the content; noted for the editor).
- Little dispute on the meaning. The point of debate is literary: the Chronicler's idealised portrait of David. The study treats the prayer as given.

**Links.** *Prayer across Scripture: learning to pray* (04); *Grief and dying* (57) may connect with 29:15 (our days "like a shadow"), not cited.

## Malachi 3:6–12 (owned)

**Context.** The post-exilic period, usually set near the time of Nehemiah (fifth century BC) [check: confirm the dating language the site uses elsewhere]. It is the fourth of Malachi's six disputations (question-and-answer exchanges). It is set against Nehemiah 13:10–13, where the Levites were unsupported and the storerooms were restored.

**Main readings.**
- Jewish reading (given first): addressed to Israel concerning the tithes of the land; Talmud Ta'anit 9a treats 3:10 as the exceptional case where testing God is permitted, connected with tithing.
- Christian readings: (a) a direct warrant for the tithe and for "storehouse tithing" to the local church (common in Southern Baptist churches); (b) a covenant text for Israel in the land whose principle (supporting God's servants, God's generosity) carries over but whose agricultural promise does not become a financial guarantee (the study's line); (c) the prosperity reading, a "test" that obliges a financial return. The site rejects this under article XIII and the row ("giving never buys a return").

**Links.** *Prophets in their times* (38); *Covenants and promises* (26).

## Luke 16 (owned; 16:18 referred to 43)

**Context.** Luke 15 (the lost sheep, coin and son, owned by *The good news*) is spoken to Pharisees and scribes. Luke 16 turns to the disciples (16:1), with Pharisees overhearing (16:14). Money ties the chapter together: the dishonest manager (1–8), sayings (9–13), the Pharisees' response (14–15), the law and the kingdom (16–18), the rich man and Lazarus (19–31).

**Main readings, 16:1–13.**
- The manager is dishonest and is commended only for shrewd foresight; the parable argues from lesser to greater (the majority reading in commentaries).
- The reductions removed illegal interest (J. D. M. Derrett) or the manager's own commission (J. A. Fitzmyer), so he acted righteously at cost to himself.
- Bailey's reading from village culture: the manager gambles on his master's generosity and honour.
- The study does not choose between methods, because 16:9 carries the point on every reading. None of these works was read for this page; they are named from general knowledge and must be checked.

**Main readings, 16:19–31.**
- Parable using contemporary afterlife imagery, so its details are not a map, versus an account of real states after death (because a character is named). Article X affirms heaven and hell; it does not settle the intermediate state. The study shows both readings and links *The hope to come*.
- Its link to the law: "Moses and the prophets" points back to the law's care for the poor (Deuteronomy 14:29) and forward to the resurrection.

**Care taken.** The Pharisees are described as Luke describes those particular hearers, and the warning is applied to all, including disciples. There is no generalisation about Jewish people.

**Links.** *Sexuality, singleness and divorce* (43), for 16:18; *The hope to come* (30); *The good news* (54); *The cross, the empty tomb and the throne* (25), for the resurrection echo (not linked in the text; optional); *The kingdom he announced* (15), for 16:16 (not linked; optional).

## Mark 12:41–44 (owned)

**Context.** Jesus' last day of public teaching in the temple (Mark 11:27–12:44). It follows the warning against scribes who "devour widows' houses" (12:38–40) and comes before the prophecy of the temple's destruction (13:1–2).

**Main readings.**
- Praise of the widow's total devotion and the measure of cost (traditional; the study's best-supported reading).
- Lament over a system that exploits widows (Addison G. Wright, *CBQ* 1982), with the scene read between 12:40 and 13:2.
- The study holds both as honouring her and adopts praise as primary, while noting the context.

**Links.** *Encounters with Jesus* (14), owner of Mark 12:13–17 and 10:17–31; *Women of the Bible* (46) may wish to link here.
