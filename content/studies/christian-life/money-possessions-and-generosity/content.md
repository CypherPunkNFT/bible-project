# Money, possessions and generosity

Concepts: Gift · Tithe · Treasure · Need

Motto: Everything comes from You. / From Your hand we give.

Lead: The Bible says more about money than many readers expect, and much of it is a promise or a warning spoken to someone in particular. This study teaches one habit for every passage about money. Before you claim a promise, read the address on it: who it was first spoken to, under which of God's promises, and what it was for.

> But who am I, and who are my people, that we should be able to give as generously as this? For everything comes from You, and from Your own hand we have given to You.

1 Chronicles 29:14 · BSB

Frame: Money is one of the places where the Bible is most often quoted out of its setting. A sentence spoken to the nation of Israel about rain on its fields is printed on an envelope for an offering. A proverb about how life usually goes is sold as a guarantee. A parable about a man who used money to prepare for his future is skipped because one of its characters is dishonest. So this study reads each passage with its address on it. It asks who first heard it, under which of God's binding promises (the Bible calls such a promise a *covenant*), and what it was meant to do. Then it asks what that passage, read honestly, still says to you.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. These six passages were chosen because each is a main reading on money that no other study on this site walks through: two from the law of Moses, one prayer of King David, one prophet, one chapter of Luke's Gospel and one short scene in Mark. Other passages on money belong to other studies, and they are quoted here only briefly with a link: the rich man who asked Jesus about eternal life, Jesus' parable of the bigger barns, Paul's collection for the poor and his charge to the rich. This study reads Scripture. It is not financial, legal or medical advice.

[care] **If money trouble is crushing you, read this first.** Debt, losing a job and not having enough to feed your family are heavy, and they can make a person feel trapped or worthless. Being poor or in debt is not a sign of weak faith, and nothing on this page says that it is. If you are thinking about ending your life, please tell someone today. In Spain, call 024; in Mexico, the Línea de la Vida, 800 911 2000; in other Spanish-speaking countries, call your local emergency number [check before publishing]. In Brazil, call the CVV on 188 [check before publishing]. Feelings like these do pass, and people do recover, including from money trouble that seemed to have no way out. For practical help with debt, a free, non-profit debt counsellor and your local church are good places to begin. The site's help page, [Help for life](/resources/life), lists more. ## 01 When you eat and are satisfied

**Main readings:** Deuteronomy 8:11–18

Hook: The warning comes on the day the barns are full.

Moses is speaking to Israel on the plains east of the Jordan, before the people enter the land God had promised them. For forty years they have lived on what God gave them each day in the wilderness. Now Moses warns them about a danger they have never faced: having plenty.

> Be careful not to forget the LORD your God by failing to keep His commandments and ordinances and statutes, which I am giving you this day.

Deuteronomy 8:11 · BSB

(The word *ordinances* here means the rules God gave Israel. It is not the church word for baptism and the Lord's Supper. LORD in capitals stands for God's own name, given to Moses.)

Look at the order of the next sentences. You eat and are satisfied. You build fine houses. Your herds grow, "your silver and gold increase and all that you have is multiplied" (Deuteronomy 8:13). "Then your heart will become proud, and you will forget the LORD your God" (Deuteronomy 8:14). Nothing in that list is a sin. Food, houses and growing flocks are gifts. The danger is what a full life can do to a heart.

Moses then names the thought that pride puts into words:

> You might say in your heart, “The power and strength of my hands have made this wealth for me.” But remember that it is the LORD your God who gives you the power to gain wealth, in order to confirm His covenant that He swore to your fathers even to this day.

Deuteronomy 8:17–18 · BSB

The cure is not to stop working or to refuse the harvest. The cure is to *remember*. Remember the wilderness, the snakes and scorpions, the water from the rock and the manna "in order to humble you and test you" (Deuteronomy 8:15–16). The strength to earn was itself a gift.

[read] This is the first thing the Bible says about money that this study wants you to carry. What you have came from somewhere. The Baptist Faith and Message, the statement of faith this site writes in agreement with, puts it in its article XIII (stewardship): "God is the source of all blessings, temporal and spiritual; all that we have and are we owe to Him."

[care] Verse 18 is sometimes quoted as if God promises every believer the power to become rich. Read the address. It is spoken to Israel as a nation entering its land, and the reason given is "to confirm His covenant that He swore to your fathers", the promise of land and descendants made to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. It tells you where wealth comes from when it comes. It does not promise that it will come to you.

**Consider:** In Moses' warning, what comes before the proud heart: hardship or plenty? What does he tell Israel to do about the thought "my hands have made this wealth"?

**Reflect:** Think of something you own or earn that you usually credit to your own effort. What would it mean to remember, this week, who gave you the strength to earn it?

## 02 From your own hand: what stewardship means

**Main readings:** 1 Chronicles 29:10–19

Hook: The king has just given a fortune, and he prays as if he has given nothing.

King David has gathered gold, silver and precious stones for the temple his son Solomon will build, and the leaders of Israel have given willingly beside him (1 Chronicles 29:1–9). It is the largest offering in the story of Israel so far. Then David prays in front of everyone.

He begins with God, not with the gift: "Yours, O LORD, is the greatness and the power and the glory and the splendor and the majesty, for everything in heaven and on earth belongs to You" (1 Chronicles 29:11). "Both riches and honor come from You" (1 Chronicles 29:12). Only then does he speak of what the people have done, and he turns it upside down:

> O LORD our God, from Your hand comes all this abundance that we have provided to build You a house for Your holy Name, and all of it belongs to You.

1 Chronicles 29:16 · BSB

This is the heart of what Christians call *stewardship*. A steward is a person who manages what belongs to someone else. David does not think of the gold as his, which he kindly hands over to God. He thinks of it as God's, which passed through his hands for a while and is now returned.

He gives two reasons for humility. The first is that it all came from God (1 Chronicles 29:14). The second is how short life is: "For we are foreigners and strangers in Your presence, as were all our forefathers. Our days on earth are like a shadow, without hope" (1 Chronicles 29:15). A shadow does not keep anything.

David also notices *how* the people gave: "willingly and with an upright heart", "joyfully and willingly" (1 Chronicles 29:17). And he does not take the willingness for granted. He prays that God will "keep this desire forever in the intentions of the hearts of Your people, and direct their hearts toward You" (1 Chronicles 29:18).

[read] Article XIII of the Baptist Faith and Message says that Christians have "a binding stewardship in their possessions" and should recognise their time, talents and possessions "as entrusted to them to use for the glory of God and for helping others." David's prayer is that sentence turned into worship.

Limit: The passage sets no amount. It does not say how much David gave as a share of what he had, and it does not make a temple offering a rule for every later gift. It teaches the posture: owner and manager, joy, and a heart that needs God to keep it generous.

**Consider:** What does David say about God before he says anything about the people's gift? What two reasons does he give for not taking pride in what they gave?

**Reflect:** If everything you have "comes from Your hand", what in your budget would you describe differently: not as mine, but as entrusted?

## 03 A tenth, a feast and the widow at the gate

**Main readings:** Deuteronomy 14:22–29

Hook: A law about giving that tells you to throw a party.

A *tithe* means a tenth. Israel's law asked each household to set aside a tenth of its harvest every year (Deuteronomy 14:22). What surprises most readers is what happened to it.

In Jewish teaching, this passage is read alongside other tithe laws. The rabbis distinguished a "first tithe" given to the Levites, the tribe who served at the sanctuary and owned no land (Numbers 18:21–24), from the tithe described here, which they called the "second tithe" (*ma'aser sheni*). The household itself carried this second tithe to the place God would choose and ate it there. In the third and sixth years of each seven-year cycle it was replaced by the "tithe of the poor" (*ma'aser ani*), left in the towns for those in need. After the temple was destroyed in AD 70, the tithes of produce continued in Jewish law in a changed form for crops grown in the land of Israel, and many Jewish families keep the custom of giving a tenth of their income to charity (*ma'aser kesafim*), as part of *tzedakah*, a word that joins justice and generosity [check: confirm this summary of rabbinic tithe categories and present practice with a Jewish source].

Now read the passage itself. You are to eat the tenth "in the presence of the LORD your God", and the reason is given: "so that you may learn to fear the LORD your God always" (Deuteronomy 14:23). If the place is too far to carry grain and oil, sell it, carry the money, and buy food there:

> Then you may spend the money on anything you desire: cattle, sheep, wine, strong drink, or anything you wish. You are to feast there in the presence of the LORD your God and rejoice with your household.

Deuteronomy 14:26 · BSB

Giving here is a feast. It teaches the family, at a table, that the harvest belongs to God.

But the feast has guests. "Do not neglect the Levite within your gates" (Deuteronomy 14:27). And every third year the tenth stays in the town:

> Then the Levite (because he has no portion or inheritance among you), the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow within your gates may come and eat and be satisfied. And the LORD your God will bless you in all the work of your hands.

Deuteronomy 14:29 · BSB

Notice who is named: the one with no land, the one far from home, the child with no father, the woman with no husband. These are the people with nothing to fall back on. God's law builds them into Israel's giving.

[read] For Christians, this passage shows what giving was for, from the beginning: to teach a heart to honour God, to bring joy, to support those who serve God's people, and to feed those in need. The promise at the end of verse 29 is real, and it is spoken to Israel in its land. It describes God's care for a people who care for the poor. It is not a price list.

Limit: This is part of the law God gave Israel, tied to a land, a harvest year and a place of worship. Whether Christians are bound to give a tenth is a question on which Baptists differ. It is answered in the question *Should Christians tithe?* below.

**Consider:** What did the household do with the tenth in most years? Who is named in verse 29, and what do they have in common?

**Reflect:** Does your giving ever feel like a feast? Who are the people "within your gates" with nothing to fall back on?

## 04 Malachi's storehouse: a promise with an address

**Main readings:** Malachi 3:6–12

Hook: The only place in Scripture where God says, Test Me.

Malachi is the last of the prophets in the order of the Christian Old Testament. He speaks to Judah after the exile, when a small community had come back from Babylon and rebuilt the temple. The book of Nehemiah, from the same years, tells how the Levites had not been given their portions and had gone back to their fields to survive (Nehemiah 13:10). That is the setting for these verses.

The passage begins with God, not money: "Because I, the LORD, do not change, you descendants of Jacob have not been destroyed" (Malachi 3:6). Then comes the call to *repent*, the Bible's word for turning back to God: "Return to Me, and I will return to you" (Malachi 3:7). The people ask how. God names one concrete place:

> Will a man rob God? Yet you are robbing Me! But you ask, ‘How do we rob You?’ In tithes and offerings. You are cursed with a curse, yet you—the whole nation—are still robbing Me.

Malachi 3:8–9 · BSB

> Bring the full tithe into the storehouse, so that there may be food in My house. Test Me in this,” says the LORD of Hosts. “See if I will not open the windows of heaven and pour out for you blessing without measure.

Malachi 3:10 · BSB

Read the address carefully, line by line.
- **Who:** "the whole nation" (Malachi 3:9), Israel under the covenant God made at Sinai.
- **The storehouse:** the storerooms at the temple in Jerusalem, where grain was kept to feed the Levites and priests (Nehemiah 13:12–13). "Food in My house" means food for those who served there.
- **The blessing:** "the windows of heaven" opening means rain. "The devourer" is whatever eats the crops, most likely locusts. The promise is that "the fruits of your land" will not be destroyed and "the vine in your field will not fail" (Malachi 3:11). These are the blessings the law promised Israel for keeping the covenant in its land (Deuteronomy 28:1–12).
- **The result:** "all the nations will call you blessed, for you will be a land of delight" (Malachi 3:12).

In Jewish tradition, these verses are read as God's word to Israel about the tithes of the land. The Talmud, in tractate Ta'anit, notes that this is the one place where testing God is allowed, and connects it with tithing [check: Babylonian Talmud, Ta'anit 9a, wording and context].

[read] What carries over to a Christian? First, the God who "does not change", and who meets people who turn back to Him. Second, that withholding what supports God's servants and God's poor is not a small thing to Him; Paul says that "those who preach the gospel should receive their living from the gospel" (1 Corinthians 9:14). Third, God's open hand toward His people. What does not carry over is a contract: a believer putting money in and God being bound to pay more back. The blessing here was rain on a nation's fields, given by the God of the covenant to a people returning to Him. It is not a rate of return.

[care] If you have heard this verse used to press you to give money you need for rent or food, with a promise that God must repay you more, that is not what Malachi says. Giving never buys a return from God. He gives because He is generous, not because He has been paid.

[open] Many Baptist churches speak of "storehouse tithing", meaning that a believer's regular giving belongs first to the local church where they worship. That is an application by comparison: the church is not the temple, but it is where God's people are taught and its servants supported. Other Baptists do not tie the word storehouse to the local church. This page does not choose.

**Consider:** Who is Malachi speaking to, and what was happening to the Levites? What kind of blessing does verse 11 describe?

**Reflect:** Has anyone ever offered you a promise about money that turned out to belong to someone else's address? Is there a place where God is calling you, not to pay Him, but to return to Him?

## 05 You cannot serve both God and money

**Main readings:** Luke 16:1–18

Hook: Jesus tells a story about a cheat, and then praises the cheat's foresight.

This chapter is Jesus' longest single teaching on money in Luke's Gospel. It begins with a *parable*, a short story Jesus tells to make one point vivid. A rich man's manager is accused of wasting his possessions and is told he will lose his job (Luke 16:1–2). "I am too weak to dig and too ashamed to beg", he thinks (Luke 16:3). So, before the news spreads, he calls in his master's debtors and has them rewrite their bills: a hundred measures of oil become fifty, a hundred of wheat become eighty (Luke 16:5–7). Now they owe him a favour.

> The master commended the dishonest manager because he had acted shrewdly. For the sons of this age are more shrewd in dealing with their own kind than are the sons of light.

Luke 16:8 · BSB

Notice what is praised and what is not. The story still calls him "the dishonest manager". He is commended for being *shrewd*, for seeing that his present position was about to end and using what was in his hands to prepare for what came next. Jesus then draws the point out for His disciples:

> I tell you, use worldly wealth to make friends for yourselves, so that when it is gone, they will welcome you into eternal dwellings.

Luke 16:9 · BSB

Money will be gone. You will be gone from it. So use it now for what will last: for people, and for God's purposes, in view of eternity.

[open] Why did the manager cut the bills? Many readers take it simply: he cheated his master once more, and Jesus uses a crook's foresight as a picture, the way He compares God to an unjust judge in Luke 18. Others have argued that the manager removed interest the law forbade, or his own share of the debt, so that he lost money rather than his master. J. Duncan M. Derrett argued for the interest reading, and Joseph Fitzmyer for the commission reading [check: Derrett, *Law in the New Testament* (1970), and Fitzmyer's article on the dishonest manager in *Theological Studies*; neither was read for this page]. Kenneth E. Bailey, in *Poet and Peasant* (1976), read the story against village custom, the manager staking everything on his master's reputation for generosity [check: not read for this page; confirm summary]. On every reading the point of verse 9 stands, and the story still calls the manager dishonest. This page does not need to settle the method to teach the point.

Then come three short sayings that keep the parable from being misused:
- "Whoever is faithful with very little will also be faithful with much" (Luke 16:10). Money is "very little" beside "true riches" (Luke 16:11), and it is "the belongings of another" (Luke 16:12). That is stewardship again: it is not yours.
- And the line at the centre:

> No servant can serve two masters. Either he will hate the one and love the other, or he will be devoted to the one and despise the other. You cannot serve both God and money.

Luke 16:13 · BSB

Money is not called evil here. It is called a rival master. It can be used, or it can be served, and not both.

Luke adds that "the Pharisees, who were lovers of money, heard all of this and were scoffing at Jesus" (Luke 16:14). The Pharisees were a Jewish movement devoted to keeping God's law carefully; Luke describes the group who heard this particular teaching, and the warning falls on everyone, including the disciples it was first spoken to (Luke 16:1). Jesus answers that "God knows your hearts. For what is prized among men is detestable before God" (Luke 16:15). He then says that the law still stands, down to "a single stroke of a pen" (Luke 16:17), which prepares for the story that follows. Verse 18, about divorce, is read in *Sexuality, singleness and divorce*.

**Consider:** What exactly is the manager praised for, and what is he still called? Why does Jesus call money "the belongings of another"?

**Reflect:** Name one way money is a tool in your life, and one way it may be a master. What would using it "to make friends" for eternity look like this month?

## 06 The beggar at the gate

**Main readings:** Luke 16:19–31

Hook: He did not hit the poor man. He never even looked down.

Jesus now tells of two men. One is rich, "dressed in purple and fine linen", living "each day in joyous splendor" (Luke 16:19). The other has a name:

> And a beggar named Lazarus lay at his gate, covered with sores and longing to be fed with the crumbs that fell from the rich man’s table. Even the dogs came and licked his sores.

Luke 16:20–21 · BSB

The rich man is never said to have harmed Lazarus. He simply stepped past him, every day. Then both die. Lazarus "was carried by the angels to Abraham's side"; the rich man "was buried", and finds himself in torment (Luke 16:22–23). (*Hades* is the Greek word for the realm of the dead.) Abraham's answer is the reversal: "during your lifetime you received your good things, while Lazarus received bad things. But now he is comforted here, while you are in agony" (Luke 16:25).

The rich man asks for Lazarus to be sent to warn his five brothers. Abraham answers:

> ‘They have Moses and the prophets; let your brothers listen to them.’

Luke 16:29 · BSB

Remember what Moses said three parts ago: the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow "may come and eat and be satisfied" (Deuteronomy 14:29). The rich man had the law that told him what to do about the man at his gate. He had heard it and stepped past it. And the last line points beyond the story: "If they do not listen to Moses and the prophets, they will not be persuaded even if someone rises from the dead" (Luke 16:31). Luke's readers knew that someone had.

[read] This story does not teach that the poor are saved because they are poor, or the rich lost because they are rich. Abraham himself was rich (Genesis 13:2). Salvation, as the Baptist Faith and Message says in its article IV (salvation), comes only through "personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord". What the story shows is what a heart that has not listened to God looks like: it can live beside need for years and never see it. The *gospel*, the good news that Jesus died for sinners and rose again, is the "someone rises from the dead" this story waits for. Read *The good news: how a person is made right with God*.

[open] Is this a parable, or Jesus describing real places? Some readers note that it begins like other parables in Luke ("There was a rich man") and uses images of the afterlife familiar to its first hearers, so its details should not be pressed into a map. Others note that no other parable names a character, and read it as describing what happens after death. The Baptist Faith and Message, in article X (last things), affirms that the unrighteous will be consigned to everlasting punishment in hell and the righteous will live with the Lord forever; it does not settle the details of what happens between death and the resurrection. Read more in *The hope to come*.

**Consider:** What did the rich man do wrong, according to the story? Why does Abraham point to "Moses and the prophets"?

**Reflect:** Who lies at your gate: someone you pass so often that you no longer see them?

## 07 Two small coins

**Main readings:** Mark 12:41–44

Hook: The smallest gift of the day, and Jesus calls it the largest.

Jesus is in the temple courts in Jerusalem, "sitting opposite the treasury", where offering chests stood to receive gifts (Mark 12:41). He watches. Many rich people put in large amounts. Then:

> Then one poor widow came and put in two small copper coins, which amounted to a small fraction of a denarius.

Mark 12:42 · BSB

A *denarius* was about a day's wage for a labourer. Her gift was a small part of one. Jesus calls His disciples over, as if this were the lesson of the day:

> “Truly I tell you, this poor widow has put more than all the others into the treasury. For they all contributed out of their surplus, but she out of her poverty has put in all she had to live on.”

Mark 12:43–44 · BSB

Heaven counts differently. The rich gave large sums and went home with plenty left. She gave a tiny sum and went home with nothing. Jesus measures a gift by what it cost the giver, not by how it looks on the ledger.

[open] What is Jesus doing in this scene? The long-standing reading takes it as praise of her devotion, a gift of everything to God. Some scholars have read it as a lament as well: just before, Jesus warned against religious leaders who "devour widows' houses" (Mark 12:40), and just after, He foretells that this very temple will be thrown down (Mark 13:1–2). On that reading Jesus honours her, and grieves over a system that took her last coins. Addison G. Wright argued for this reading in an article on the widow's coins in the *Catholic Biblical Quarterly* [check: Wright, "The Widow's Mites: Praise or Lament?", *CBQ* 44 (1982); not read for this page]. Both readings honour the widow. This study takes Jesus' words as praise, since He contrasts her gift with the others and says she gave "more than all", and notes that the warning in verse 40 sits beside it.

[care] This story is sometimes used to press poor people to give what they need to live on. Jesus describes what she did; He does not command anyone to do the same. Those who lead God's people are warned in the same chapter against taking from widows. If you have little, your small gift is not small to God, and you are not failing Him by feeding your children.

**Consider:** How does Jesus measure the gifts he watches? What did the warning just before this scene say about widows?

**Reflect:** What does your giving cost you? Is there a quiet gift no one sees that you have been tempted to think worthless?

## Question tithe: Should Christians tithe?

[read] The Baptist Faith and Message, in article XIII (stewardship), says that Christians "should contribute of their means cheerfully, regularly, systematically, proportionately, and liberally for the advancement of the Redeemer's cause on earth." It does not say whether a tenth binds Christians. So this site states what the confession states and shows the two readings Baptists hold, without choosing.

**The first reading: the tithe as the starting point.** Many Baptists teach that a tenth is where a Christian's giving begins. They note that giving a tenth comes before the law of Moses: Abraham gave Melchizedek a tenth (Genesis 14:20), and Jacob vowed a tenth (Genesis 28:22). They point to Jesus telling the Pharisees that they should practise justice, mercy and faithfulness "without neglecting" their tithes (Matthew 23:23). And they argue that a Christian under grace should not give less than an Israelite under the law. This has long been the common teaching in Southern Baptist churches [check: a named Southern Baptist source teaching the tithe as the starting point].

**The second reading: generous, proportionate giving without a fixed tenth.** Other Baptists hold that the tithe belonged to the law of Moses, which is fulfilled in Christ, and so does not bind Christians as a rule. They note that when Paul teaches a church about giving at length, he never mentions a tenth. Instead he says each one should give "according to your means" (2 Corinthians 8:11), and:

> Each one should give what he has decided in his heart to give, not out of regret or compulsion. For God loves a cheerful giver.

2 Corinthians 9:7 · BSB

On this reading a tenth may be a good guide, many will give more, and someone in deep need may rightly give less without guilt [check: a named Baptist source for this reading]. Read more in *Faith in daily life*.

[read] Both readings agree on more than they differ. Both say giving is worship, that it should be regular and planned, that it grows with what one has, that it supports the church and the poor, and that it is never a way to earn anything from God. How the law of Moses applies to Christians more broadly is shown in *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice*.

## Question prosperity: Does God promise wealth to those who give?

[read] No. The Bible promises that God sees, provides and rewards, but it does not promise that giving money will make you rich. The confession's article XIII teaches stewardship. It does not teach wealth as the reward of faith, and neither does this site.

The teaching often called the *prosperity gospel* holds that God wills financial wealth and health for every believer who has enough faith, and that giving is a seed that brings a harvest of money. It is found especially in the Word of Faith movement. It should not be confused with classical Pentecostal churches, many of which have rejected it; the Assemblies of God, for example, issued a position paper critical of the "positive confession" teaching [check: Assemblies of God, "The Believer and Positive Confession" (1980), title, date and present status].

Read the passages it uses with their addresses on them. Malachi's windows of heaven were rain on Israel's fields (part 04). Deuteronomy's "power to gain wealth" confirmed a covenant with Israel's fathers (part 01). Proverbs says:

> Honor the LORD with your wealth and with the firstfruits of all your harvest; then your barns will be filled with plenty, and your vats will overflow with new wine.

Proverbs 3:9–10 · BSB

Proverbs teaches how life generally goes under God's hand, not guarantees for every case. The same book prays, "Give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with the bread that is my portion" (Proverbs 30:8). Read more in *The wisdom books*.

Paul does say that "whoever sows generously will also reap generously" (2 Corinthians 9:6). But look at the harvest he names:

> You will be enriched in every way to be generous on every occasion, so that through us your giving will produce thanksgiving to God.

2 Corinthians 9:11 · BSB

The harvest of generosity is more generosity, and thanks to God. And when Jesus promised a hundredfold to those who leave homes and fields for Him, He added "along with persecutions" (Mark 10:30); the hundredfold includes "brothers and sisters and mothers and children", the family of God's people. Read more in *Encounters with Jesus*.

## Question poverty: Is my poverty a sign of weak faith? And what about debt?

[read] No. Poverty is not proof of weak faith. Jesus Himself "though He was rich, yet for your sakes He became poor" (2 Corinthians 8:9). The churches of Macedonia lived in "deep poverty" and gave richly (2 Corinthians 8:2). The widow Jesus praised was poor (part 07), and Lazarus is the one comforted (part 06). Paul knew hunger (Philippians 4:12). Scripture never treats the poor as people God has turned away from.

The Bible does speak honestly about debt:

> The rich rule over the poor, and the borrower is slave to the lender.

Proverbs 22:7 · BSB

This proverb describes how debt can take away freedom. It is a warning to be careful with borrowing, and it is also why God's law protected the poor from lenders, forbidding Israelites to charge interest to a poor brother (Leviticus 25:35–37). It is not a judgement on anyone already in debt.

And Scripture places the poor near God's heart: "Kindness to the poor is a loan to the LORD, and He will repay the lender" (Proverbs 19:17). The Baptist Faith and Message, in article XV (the Christian and the social order), calls Christians to oppose "every form of greed" and to care for the needy.

[care] If you are in debt or poverty, there is no shame in asking for help: from your church, from a non-profit debt counsellor, or from public help you are entitled to. Receiving help is part of the life of God's people (2 Corinthians 8:14). The site's help page, [Help for life](/resources/life), lists places to start. If you feel hopeless, please go back to the help at the top of this page.

## Question rich: Is it wrong to be rich?

[read] Owning things is not sin. Abraham, Job and Joseph of Arimathea were wealthy. But Jesus says riches are dangerous:

> It is easier for a camel to pass through the eye of a needle than for a rich man to enter the kingdom of God.

Mark 10:25 · BSB

When the disciples ask "Who then can be saved?", Jesus answers, "With man this is impossible, but not with God" (Mark 10:26–27). Read more in *Encounters with Jesus*. Jesus also warns, "Guard yourselves against every form of greed, for one's life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions" (Luke 12:15), and "where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (Luke 12:34). Read more in *Following Jesus and the Sermon on the Mount*.

Paul does not tell the rich to stop being rich. He tells them how to be rich:

> Instruct those who are rich in the present age not to be conceited and not to put their hope in the uncertainty of wealth, but in God, who richly provides all things for us to enjoy. Instruct them to do good, to be rich in good works, and to be generous and ready to share,

1 Timothy 6:17–18 · BSB

The danger he names is not money but "the love of money" (1 Timothy 6:10). Read more in *Faith in daily life*.

## Close: Reading the address

Way: Here is the habit to carry to the next passage about money. Read the address first: who heard it, under which of God's covenants, and what it was for. Then ask what it says about God, who owns everything and gives freely. Then ask what it says about your heart, which forgets in plenty and fears in want. Then notice what it does not promise. And then go back and read it again.

Close: David prayed, "from Your own hand we have given to You" (1 Chronicles 29:14). That is true of every gift, because it is true of the greatest gift. Paul grounds all Christian giving in what Jesus gave: "though He was rich, yet for your sakes He became poor, so that you through His poverty might become rich" (2 Corinthians 8:9). *Grace*, the Bible's word for God's undeserved kindness, comes first, and generosity is the answer it calls out.

Invite: If you have not yet trusted Christ, none of this begins with giving anything. Nothing you give can buy God's favour, and you do not need to buy it. Turn from your sin and trust Jesus as Lord, who died and rose again. Read *The good news: how a person is made right with God*.

Question: What do you hold as if it were yours, and what would change if you held it as entrusted?

Again: Read the passage again before you answer.
