# God: who He is and what He does

Concepts: Name · Character · Creation · Providence

Motto: He tells us His name. / He keeps the world He made.

Lead: The Bible rarely describes God the way a textbook would. It tells you His name in the middle of a rescue, His holiness in the middle of a commission, and His love in the middle of a gift. This study teaches one habit: whenever a passage tells you what God is like, look for what He is doing in the same paragraph.

> The LORD, the LORD God, is compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in loving devotion and faithfulness,

Exodus 34:6 · BSB

Frame: Scripture does not hand you a list of God's qualities to memorise and then leave. The God who tells Moses His name has just said He has seen His people's suffering. The Holy One whom Isaiah sees also sends someone to cleanse him. The God whom John calls love is the God who sent His Son. If you separate the description from the deed, the description slowly fills up with your own ideas. If you keep them together, the deed tells you what the word means. So in every part below, ask three questions. What does this passage say God is like? What is He doing while it says so? What response does it ask of the reader?

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. These passages were chosen because each one says something about God's character, His work of creation, or His providence (His continuing care and rule over everything He has made), and because no other study on this site walks them. Many great passages about God are walked elsewhere and only linked here. The teaching that God is one God in three persons, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, has its full home in the site's Trinity articles; this study names it where a passage touches it. Psalm 139 belongs to *Every life in God's image*, Job's speeches from the whirlwind to *The wisdom books*, and the questions people bring to Genesis 1 to *Genesis 1–11: the questions readers bring*. This study reads Scripture; it is not counselling or medical guidance.

## 01 I AM WHO I AM: the God who gives His name

**Main readings:** Exodus 3:1–15

A shepherd turns aside to look at a strange fire. The fire speaks his name.

### Holy ground, and a God who has seen

Moses is keeping his father-in-law's sheep "on the far side of the wilderness" when he sees a bush burning that does not burn up (Exodus 3:1–3). The text says first that "the angel of the LORD appeared to him" in the fire, and then that "God called out to him from within the bush" (Exodus 3:2, 4). Who this angel is has been read in more than one way. *Read more in Angels, demons and spiritual powers.*

God first tells Moses to keep his distance and take off his sandals, "for the place where you are standing is holy ground" (Exodus 3:5). Then He names Himself by His history with people: "I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" (Exodus 3:6). Moses hides his face.

Now watch the habit at work. Before Moses asks a single question, God says what He has been doing:

> The LORD said, “I have indeed seen the affliction of My people in Egypt. I have heard them crying out because of their oppressors, and I am aware of their sufferings.

Exodus 3:7 · BSB

Seen, heard, aware, and then "I have come down to rescue them" (Exodus 3:8). The first thing this holy God tells Moses about Himself, after His name, is that slaves in a brick-yard have not escaped His notice.

Then comes the turn: "Therefore, go! I am sending you" (Exodus 3:10). Moses asks, "Who am I?" God does not answer by telling Moses he is more capable than he thinks. He answers, "I will surely be with you" (Exodus 3:11–12). The question was about Moses. The answer is about God.

### The name

Moses asks what to say when the people ask God's name. God answers:

> God said to Moses, “I AM WHO I AM. This is what you are to say to the Israelites: ‘I AM has sent me to you.’”

Exodus 3:14 · BSB

In the next verse God gives the name by which He will be "remembered in every generation" (Exodus 3:15). English Bibles print it as LORD, in small capitals. Behind it stand four Hebrew letters, often written YHWH, which sound like the Hebrew verb "to be" in "I AM". In Jewish practice the name is not spoken aloud, out of reverence. When Scripture is read in the synagogue, the reader says *Adonai*, "my Lord", and in ordinary speech many Jews say *HaShem*, "the Name". The English habit of printing LORD follows that old Jewish practice.

What does "I AM WHO I AM" mean? Readers have heard two things in it, and they fit together.

- **God depends on nothing.** Many Christian theologians have read the name as saying that God simply is: He was not made, and nothing keeps Him in being. Paul says much the same in Athens: God is not served "as if He needed anything" (Acts 17:25).
- **God will be with them.** The Hebrew can also be rendered "I will be who I will be". Coming straight after "I will surely be with you" (Exodus 3:12), many hear a promise of presence. The Babylonian Talmud reads it this way: God tells Moses that He will be with Israel in this trouble as He will be with them in troubles to come [check: Berakhot 9b, wording of the passage].

The God who needs nothing chooses to be with an enslaved people. Christians also hear this name again when Jesus says, before His opponents, that before Abraham was born, "I am" (John 8:58). The site holds, with the Baptist Faith and Message 2000, article II (God), that there is one God who reveals Himself "as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit".

[care] The name is not a password or a puzzle to be cracked. Verse 15 ties it to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob and to "every generation". It is how God wants to be remembered: as the One who keeps faith with people over time.

**Consider:** What does God say He has seen and heard before Moses asks His name? How does "I will surely be with you" answer "Who am I?"

**Reflect:** When you feel unequal to something you know is right, do you look first at your own strength, or at the One who sends you?

## 02 Holy, holy, holy: the God who is set apart

**Main readings:** Isaiah 6:1–13

The old king has died. Isaiah sees the King who does not die.

### Woe is me

Isaiah dates his vision to "the year that King Uzziah died", the end of a long reign (Isaiah 6:1; 2 Kings 15:2). He sees the Lord on a throne, with seraphim above Him. The name means something like "burning ones"; they are heavenly beings, and even they cover their faces.

> And they were calling out to one another: “Holy, holy, holy is the LORD of Hosts; all the earth is full of His glory.”

Isaiah 6:3 · BSB

*Holy* means set apart: God is different in kind from everything He has made, and He is wholly pure. Hebrew often repeats a word to make it the highest of its kind; here the word is said three times. *Glory* is the weight and brightness of who God is, made visible. And it is not shut up in the temple: "all the earth is full" of it.

These words have long been sung by both Jewish and Christian worshippers. In the synagogue they form part of the *Kedushah* ("sanctification"), a prayer said standing, in which the congregation repeats the seraphim's words. Many churches sing them in the hymn called the *Sanctus*. The heavenly beings of Revelation 4:8 echo them again.

Isaiah's response is not pride at having seen something rare:

> Then I said: “Woe is me, for I am ruined, because I am a man of unclean lips dwelling among a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the LORD of Hosts.”

Isaiah 6:5 · BSB

Holiness is shown here by what it does to a person who sees it. Isaiah does not compare himself with others. He sees God, and then he sees himself.

### Your sin is atoned for

A seraph takes a glowing coal from the altar and touches Isaiah's lips: "your iniquity is removed and your sin is atoned for" (Isaiah 6:6–7). *Iniquity* is wrongdoing. *Atoned for* means the sin is dealt with and covered so that it no longer stands between Isaiah and God. Notice the order. Isaiah does not clean himself up to be fit to approach. He confesses, and cleansing comes to him, applied to the very lips he called unclean.

Only then: "Whom shall I send? Who will go for Us?" And Isaiah says, "Here am I. Send me!" (Isaiah 6:8).

[open] Why "Us"? Jewish interpreters, and many Christian ones, read it as God speaking among His heavenly court, as in 1 Kings 22:19. Many Christians, from early times, have also heard in it a hint of the one God who is Father, Son and Holy Spirit, and John 12:41 says that Isaiah "saw Jesus' glory". This study does not rest the teaching of the Trinity on this one word; its home is the site's Trinity articles.

Do not stop at "Send me". The message Isaiah is given is hard: people will hear without understanding, until "the land is desolate" (Isaiah 6:9–11). Being sent by God does not promise a welcome. The chapter ends with a felled tree, and one word of hope: "the holy seed will be a stump in the land" (Isaiah 6:13).

[care] The New Testament quotes Isaiah 6:9–10 several times, to explain why some who hear the message do not receive it (Matthew 13:14–15; John 12:39–40; Acts 28:26–27). These verses are never a verdict on the Jewish people as a people. Paul asks outright whether God has rejected His people, and answers no (Romans 11:1–2). Part 10 of this study reads where that argument ends.

**Consider:** What happens between Isaiah's "Woe is me" and his "Send me"? How does the rest of the chapter correct a reading that stops at verse 8?

**Reflect:** Is there something you need to confess before you can serve with a clean heart, and do you believe cleansing can be given to you rather than earned?

## 03 Compassionate and gracious: the God who forgives

**Main readings:** Exodus 34:1–9

The first tablets lie broken. God tells Moses to cut new ones.

### The LORD proclaims His name

This scene comes after Israel made a golden calf and worshipped it, only weeks after hearing God's commands at Sinai (Exodus 32). Moses broke the two stone tablets on which the commands were written. They were the written terms of God's *covenant* with Israel: His binding promise to be their God, and their pledge to be His people. Now God says, "Chisel out two stone tablets like the originals" (Exodus 34:1). The covenant they broke will be written again.

God "descended in a cloud, stood with him there, and proclaimed His name" (Exodus 34:5). In Exodus 3 God gave His name. Here He fills it in. The words at the head of this page are His own: compassionate, gracious, slow to anger, "abounding in loving devotion and faithfulness" (Exodus 34:6). *Gracious* means showing kindness that has not been earned; this is what the Bible calls *grace*. "Loving devotion" translates a Hebrew word, *hesed*, for a loyal love that keeps its promises.

> maintaining loving devotion to a thousand generations, forgiving iniquity, transgression, and sin. Yet He will by no means leave the guilty unpunished; He will visit the iniquity of the fathers on their children and grandchildren to the third and fourth generations.”

Exodus 34:7 · BSB

Keep both halves. Mercy here is not indifference to evil, and the warning does not cancel the mercy. Look at the proportions: loving devotion to "a thousand generations", consequences to "the third and fourth". When the commands were first given, the same warning was tied to "those who hate Me" (Exodus 20:5). And Scripture elsewhere is plain that each person answers for their own sin (Ezekiel 18:20). The verse describes how wrongdoing spreads its damage through families; it does not say God punishes innocent children for their parents' sins.

In Jewish worship these verses are known as the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy and are recited on festivals, on fast days and in the prayers for forgiveness before the High Holy Days [check: the occasions as practised across Jewish communities]. Scripture itself returns to them again and again (Numbers 14:18; Psalm 86:15; Psalm 103:8; Joel 2:13). Jonah even quotes them as a complaint: he knew God would be merciful to his enemies (Jonah 4:2).

### Moses prays the name back

Moses "immediately bowed down to the ground and worshiped" (Exodus 34:8). Then he prays:

> “O Lord,” he said, “if I have indeed found favor in Your sight, my Lord, please go with us. Although this is a stiff-necked people, forgive our iniquity and sin, and take us as Your inheritance.”

Exodus 34:9 · BSB

He does not pretend the people are better than they are. He asks God to be what He has just said He is. That is what knowing God's character is for: you pray on the strength of it.

**Consider:** Which words in God's proclamation describe forgiveness, and which keep it from meaning that guilt does not matter? What does Moses ask for?

**Reflect:** Do your prayers make room for both honest confession and real confidence in the mercy God has declared?

## 04 No partiality, no bribe: the God who does justice

**Main readings:** Deuteronomy 10:12–22; Psalm 96

The owner of the heavens notices a widow's empty table.

### God of gods, and the foreigner's friend

Moses asks Israel what the LORD asks of them: "to fear the LORD your God by walking in all His ways, to love Him, to serve" Him (Deuteronomy 10:12). To *fear the LORD* is not to cower. It is the awe that takes God seriously enough to obey Him.

The heavens and the earth belong to Him (Deuteronomy 10:14). Yet He "set His affection" on a small family and chose their descendants (Deuteronomy 10:15). So they must "circumcise" their hearts (Deuteronomy 10:16): circumcision was the bodily sign of belonging to God's covenant, and Moses asks for the same mark on the inside.

Then comes a description of God's greatness, and straight after it a description of what that greatness does:

> For the LORD your God is God of gods and Lord of lords, the great, mighty, and awesome God, showing no partiality and accepting no bribe. He executes justice for the fatherless and widow, and He loves the foreigner, giving him food and clothing.

Deuteronomy 10:17–18 · BSB

The habit again. "Great, mighty, and awesome" is filled in by orphans, widows, food and clothing. The most powerful being there is cannot be bought, and spends His power on people with no one to speak for them. The command follows: "So you also must love the foreigner, since you yourselves were foreigners in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 10:19). *Read more in Every life in God's image.*

### The LORD reigns, and the trees sing

Psalm 96 calls "all the earth" to sing and the nations to hear. It draws a sharp line: "For all the gods of the nations are idols, but it is the LORD who made the heavens" (Psalm 96:5). An *idol* is a god made by human hands, or anything put in God's place. The psalm then gives the nations a message to carry: "The LORD reigns!" (Psalm 96:10).

And why should fields and forests rejoice?

> before the LORD, for He is coming— He is coming to judge the earth. He will judge the world in righteousness and the peoples in His faithfulness.

Psalm 96:13 · BSB

For people who have been wronged and never heard, judgment is good news. A world where wrongdoing never mattered would not be a peaceful world. The psalm's joy rests on a Judge who cannot be bribed.

**Consider:** How does Deuteronomy move from God's greatness to the foreigner's needs? Why does Psalm 96 treat God's coming to judge as a reason to sing?

**Reflect:** Who near you has little influence and no one to speak for them? What would it look like to treat them as the God of Deuteronomy 10 does?

## 05 God is love: the love that came first

**Main readings:** 1 John 4:7–21

"God is love" is one of the best-known lines in the Bible. John does not leave you to guess what it means.

### Love you can point to

John writes, "Whoever does not love does not know God, because God is love" (1 John 4:8). Then, at once, he points to a deed:

> And love consists in this: not that we loved God, but that He loved us and sent His Son as the atoning sacrifice for our sins.

1 John 4:10 · BSB

An *atoning sacrifice* is an offering that deals with sin and turns aside the judgment it deserves, so that people can be put right with God. The love John means has a shape: it is the Father sending Jesus to die for people who had not loved Him first. *Read more in The cross, the empty tomb and the throne.*

So "God is love" cannot be turned around into "love is God", as though any warm feeling were divine. John defines love by God, not God by our feelings. And the whole of God is at work in the paragraph: the Father "has sent His Son", and "He has given us of His Spirit" (1 John 4:13–14).

### The brother you can see

"No one has ever seen God; but if we love one another, God remains in us" (1 John 4:12). The unseen God's love becomes visible in how His people treat each other.

> We love because He first loved us.

1 John 4:19 · BSB

Then the test: anyone who says "I love God" but hates his brother "is a liar" (1 John 4:20). Love for God that never reaches the person in front of you is not what John means by love.

[care] "Perfect love drives out fear, because fear involves punishment" (1 John 4:18). The fear in view is dread of God's judgment; the verse before speaks of "the day of judgment". It is not a rebuke to someone who struggles with anxiety, panic or grief, and it does not mean that a frightened believer has failed. *Read more in Fear, anxiety and despair.*

**Consider:** How does the sending of the Son explain "God is love"? Why does John end with the brother who can be seen?

**Reflect:** Name one person whom you find it hard to love. What one concrete act of love toward them would match what you say you believe about God?

## 06 He spoke, and it came to be: creation from nothing

**Main readings:** Psalm 33:6–9; Isaiah 40:12–31

Before there was anything, there was God. Then He spoke.

### Made by a word

The Bible opens with one plain sentence:

> In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth.

Genesis 1:1 · BSB

Psalm 33 sings the same truth: "By the word of the LORD the heavens were made" (Psalm 33:6). He piles up the seas and stores the deep, and so "all the earth" should fear Him (Psalm 33:7–8).

> For He spoke, and it came to be; He commanded, and it stood firm.

Psalm 33:9 · BSB

Christians call this *creation out of nothing*. God did not take some material that already existed and shape it, as a potter shapes clay. Before He spoke there was nothing beside Him. The letter to the Hebrews says so:

> By faith we understand that the universe was formed at God’s command, so that what is seen was not made out of what was visible.

Hebrews 11:3 · BSB

[source] The Westminster Shorter Catechism (1647), a Presbyterian and Reformed catechism, answers question 9, "What is the work of creation?", with "God's making all things of nothing, by the word of his power, in the space of six days, and all very good." The Baptist Catechism (1695) gives the same answer [check: question number and exact wording in the Baptist Catechism].

The New Testament adds who the Word was through whom all this was made:

> For in Him all things were created, things in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible, whether thrones or dominions or rulers or authorities. All things were created through Him and for Him. He is before all things, and in Him all things hold together.

Colossians 1:16–17 · BSB

The "Him" is the Son, Jesus Christ. *Read more in Who do you say that I am? Who Jesus really is.*

[open] How long did creation take, and how old is the earth? The Baptist Faith and Message affirms God as Creator and man as His special creation; it does not fix a timeline. Southern Baptists hold more than one view, among them a young earth with six days of twenty-four hours, and an older earth read in several ways. This study does not choose. *Read more in Genesis 1–11: the questions readers bring.*

### To whom will you compare Him?

Isaiah 40 asks a string of questions. Who has measured the oceans in his palm, or weighed the mountains? "Who has directed the Spirit of the LORD, or informed Him as His counselor?" (Isaiah 40:12–13). The nations are "like a drop in a bucket" (Isaiah 40:15).

"To whom will you liken God?" (Isaiah 40:18). To an idol? Isaiah pictures a man choosing wood "that will not rot" and hiring a craftsman to make an idol "that will not topple" (Isaiah 40:20). The gentle joke is serious: a god that has to be propped up cannot hold you up. The true God "calls each one" of the stars "by name", and "not one of them is missing" (Isaiah 40:26).

Then the chapter turns to a tired people:

> Why do you say, O Jacob, and why do you assert, O Israel, “My way is hidden from the LORD, and my claim is ignored by my God”?

Isaiah 40:27 · BSB

The point of all that greatness was this person. The God who numbers the stars has not lost track of you. He "will not grow tired or weary", and "He gives power to the faint" (Isaiah 40:28–29). Those who "wait upon the LORD will renew their strength" (Isaiah 40:31).

[care] To *wait upon* the LORD is to trust Him over time. The verse does not set a date when weariness ends, and it does not say that a person who is still exhausted has waited wrongly. Isaiah 40:27 shows that God's people have felt unseen before, and said so aloud.

**Consider:** What does Psalm 33:9 say God needed in order to create? Why does Isaiah 40 move from the stars to Jacob's complaint?

**Reflect:** Where do you feel that your way is "hidden from the LORD"? Read Isaiah 40:26–29 slowly with that place in mind.

## 07 Not far from each one of us: the Creator and the nations

**Main readings:** Acts 17:22–31

In a city full of altars, Paul finds one with no name on it.

### The God who needs nothing

Paul stands before the Areopagus, the council that met on a hill in Athens. He has seen an altar inscribed "TO AN UNKNOWN GOD" (Acts 17:22–23). He does not begin with Israel's Scriptures, which his hearers did not know. He begins with creation.

> The God who made the world and everything in it is the Lord of heaven and earth and does not live in temples made by human hands. Nor is He served by human hands, as if He needed anything, because He Himself gives everyone life and breath and everything else.

Acts 17:24–25 · BSB

Look how the description turns into deeds. He made everything; He needs nothing; He gives everything, down to the breath you just took. From one man He made every nation, and set their times and places (Acts 17:26). And He did this so "that they would seek Him", for "He is not far from each one of us" (Acts 17:27).

Paul then quotes Greek writers his audience knew: "For in Him we live and move and have our being" and "We are His offspring" (Acts 17:28). The second line comes from the poet Aratus [check: Aratus, *Phaenomena*, opening lines]; the first is often linked to Epimenides [check: the attribution is disputed]. Paul uses their words as common ground, and then draws a conclusion they had not drawn: if we are God's offspring, God cannot be "like gold or silver or stone" made by human skill (Acts 17:29).

### Repent, because a day is set

> Although God overlooked the ignorance of earlier times, He now commands all people everywhere to repent.

Acts 17:30 · BSB

To *repent* is to turn: away from sin and false gods, and toward the true God. Paul gives the reason: God "has set a day when He will judge the world with justice by the Man He has appointed", and has given proof "by raising Him from the dead" (Acts 17:31). The Creator is also the Judge, and the Judge is Jesus, raised.

Some laughed, some wanted to hear more, and some believed (Acts 17:32–34). Paul reasoned and invited; he did not compel. The site holds, with article XVII (religious liberty), that God alone is Lord of the conscience.

[care] Muslim readers also confess one God, Creator of all, who needs nothing. Christians and Muslims agree on much in this paragraph and differ on who Jesus is and on God as Father, Son and Holy Spirit. The site's answer on the Trinity written for Muslim neighbours sets out that difference with respect.

**Consider:** Where does Paul begin, and why? What three things does he say God does in verses 24–25?

**Reflect:** Is there an "unknown god" in your life: something you rely on, or serve, that you have never named as such?

## 08 He opens His hand: providence, God's daily care

**Main readings:** Psalm 104; Matthew 10:29–31

The lions roar at night for their food. The psalm says they are asking God.

### A song of a world kept

*Providence* is the Christian word for God's continuing care and rule over everything He has made. He did not wind the world up and walk away. He keeps it in being and steers it toward His purposes. The Baptist Faith and Message, article II, says God as Father "reigns with providential care over His universe, His creatures, and the flow of the stream of human history according to the purposes of His grace."

Psalm 104 sings this. It moves broadly through the order of creation: light and sky, waters pushed back, springs in valleys, grass and crops, moon and sun, and the sea "teeming with creatures beyond number" (Psalm 104:2–25). It gives God's care in ordinary things: "wine that gladdens the heart of man, oil that makes his face to shine, and bread that sustains his heart" (Psalm 104:15).

The young lions "roar for their prey and seek their food from God" (Psalm 104:21). The hunt and the gift are the same event, seen from two sides. God does not stand behind the world as a rival to how it works; He works through it.

> All creatures look to You to give them their food in due season. When You give it to them, they gather it up; when You open Your hand, they are satisfied with good things.

Psalm 104:27–28 · BSB

Life and death are in His hand too: "when You take away their breath, they die and return to dust. When You send Your Spirit, they are created" (Psalm 104:29–30). And God enjoys what He made. He formed Leviathan, a great sea creature, "to frolic" in the sea (Psalm 104:26), and the psalmist prays, "may the LORD rejoice in His works" (Psalm 104:31).

The psalm ends with a hard line: "May sinners vanish from the earth" (Psalm 104:35). In a song about a good world, the psalmist longs for it to be rid of what spoils it. It is a prayer for God's world to be put right, not permission for anyone's private revenge.

[source] The Heidelberg Catechism (1563), a Reformed catechism, answers question 27 in words close to this psalm: providence is God's power by which "as it were by his hand, he upholds and governs heaven, earth, and all creatures; so that herbs and grass, rain and drought, fruitful and barren years, meat and drink, health and sickness, riches and poverty, yea, and all things come, not by chance, but by his fatherly hand." [check: wording varies by English translation; confirm against the site's copy.]

### Not one sparrow

Jesus is sending out His twelve disciples, and He has just warned them of courts, beatings and hatred (Matthew 10:16–28). Then:

> Are not two sparrows sold for a penny? Yet not one of them will fall to the ground apart from the will of your Father. And even the very hairs of your head are all numbered. So do not be afraid; you are worth more than many sparrows.

Matthew 10:29–31 · BSB

Notice what Jesus does not say. Sparrows still fall, and in the same speech He tells His followers they may be killed (Matthew 10:28). The promise is not that nothing bad will happen. It is that nothing happens outside the Father's knowledge and will, and that the one it happens to is valued by Him.

**Consider:** In Psalm 104, how is the lions' hunting also God's giving? What does Jesus promise about the sparrow, and what does He not promise?

**Reflect:** Pick one ordinary thing you received today: food, breath, a working body, a friend. Thank God for it as something from His open hand.

Twenty years after they sold him, Joseph's brothers stand before him again, and their father is dead.

### Am I in the place of God?

Jacob has died, and the brothers are afraid: "What if Joseph bears a grudge?" (Genesis 50:15). They send a message asking forgiveness, then come and bow: "We are your slaves!" (Genesis 50:18). Joseph weeps.

His first words are a question: "Do not be afraid. Am I in the place of God?" (Genesis 50:19). Judgment and revenge belong to God, not to him. Then comes one of the clearest sentences in Scripture about providence:

> As for you, what you intended against me for evil, God intended for good, in order to accomplish a day like this—to preserve the lives of many people.

Genesis 50:20 · BSB

Two intentions sit inside one event. The brothers meant evil, and Joseph calls it evil; he does not pretend it was good. God meant good, and the good was real: many lives kept through a famine. The brothers stay responsible for what they did. God's purpose does not make their sin innocent; it makes their sin unable to have the last word.

Then Joseph acts on what he believes about God: "I will provide for you and your little ones" (Genesis 50:21). Trust in God's providence turns into kindness to the people who hurt him.

Paul says something wider about all who belong to Christ:

> And we know that God works all things together for the good of those who love Him, who are called according to His purpose.

Romans 8:28 · BSB

*Read more in Chosen and kept: can I be sure?*

[care] These verses can be misused. They do not call any evil good, and they do not ask anyone who has been harmed to say that what happened to them was good. Forgiveness, as Joseph shows it, is not the same as trust, and it does not mean returning to danger. Joseph tested his brothers carefully before he made himself known (Genesis 42–44). If someone is harming you, telling a person you trust is right, and where a crime has been done, it belongs with the authorities. Nor do these verses promise that you will see the good in this life, as Joseph did. Romans 8:28 promises God's purpose; it does not promise you the explanation.

[open] How does God's rule over all things fit with human choices? The confession holds that God's knowledge "extends to all things, past, present, and future, including the future decisions of His free creatures" (article II), and that His gracious purpose is consistent with the free agency of man (article V, God's purpose of grace). Within that line, Calvinist and non-Calvinist Baptists explain the details differently, and this study does not choose. Both deny that God tempts anyone to sin (James 1:13). The Second London Baptist Confession (1677/1689), chapter 3, says that God is "neither the author of sin" nor shares in it [check: exact wording of chapter 3, paragraph 1].

**Consider:** What did the brothers intend, and what did God intend? What does Joseph do because of what he believes?

**Reflect:** Is there a wrong done to you that you have never brought before God? You do not have to call it good. Can you ask Him to keep it from having the last word?

## 10 To Him are all things: knowing God, and not knowing everything

**Main readings:** Romans 11:33–36

After three chapters of hard argument, Paul stops explaining and starts singing.

### Depth, not darkness

Romans 9–11 wrestle with why many in Israel have not believed in Jesus, and how God's mercy has reached the nations. Paul insists that God's gifts and calling to Israel stand (Romans 11:29). He ends not with a neat summary but with praise:

> O, the depth of the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are His judgments, and untraceable His ways!

Romans 11:33 · BSB

Then he quotes Scripture: "Who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been His counselor?" (Romans 11:34). That is Isaiah 40:13, from part 06. The next line, "Who has first given to God, that God should repay him?" (Romans 11:35), echoes God's words to Job (Job 41:11). Nobody advises God, and nobody puts Him in their debt.

> For from Him and through Him and to Him are all things. To Him be the glory forever! Amen.

Romans 11:36 · BSB

That one verse gathers up this whole study. *From Him*: creation. *Through Him*: providence. *To Him*: the purpose of everything, His glory.

[care] "Unsearchable" does not mean God has told us nothing. Paul has just spent three chapters teaching. It means what God has made known is true, and He is still greater than all of it. These verses do not forbid hard questions, and they are not a way to silence someone who is suffering. They are the words of a man who asked hard questions all the way to the end, and then worshipped.

**Consider:** What had Paul been discussing just before he began to praise? Which Old Testament passages does he quote, and why do they fit here?

**Reflect:** Where do you need to read more carefully what God has said, and where do you need to admit that your understanding is not complete?

## Reading God in what He does

Way: Here is the habit to carry to the next passage. When Scripture tells you what God is like, look in the same paragraph for what He is doing. "I AM" came with "I have seen". "Holy" came with a coal from the altar. "Gracious" came with new tablets. "Great and awesome" came with a widow's bread. "Love" came with a Son sent. "Creator" came with breath given to everyone, and "providence" came with a sparrow and a famine survived. Let the deed tell you what the word means.

Close: Knowing God, in these passages, never stays in the head. Moses hides his face and then goes. Isaiah confesses and then is sent. Moses bows and then prays for a stubborn people. Israel is told to love the foreigner. John's readers are told to love the brother they can see. Athens is told to repent. Joseph forgives and feeds his brothers. Paul worships.

Invite: The *gospel*, the good news, is that this God has made Himself known most fully in Jesus Christ: sent by the Father's love as "the atoning sacrifice for our sins" (1 John 4:10), and raised from the dead as proof that God will judge the world by Him (Acts 17:31). If you have not yet trusted Him, the call of Acts 17:30 is for you: turn from your sin and trust Christ as Lord. *Read more in The good news: how a person is made right with God.*
