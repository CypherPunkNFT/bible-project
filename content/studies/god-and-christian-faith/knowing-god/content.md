# Knowing God

**Study 21 · God and Christian Faith · First content draft · 2026-10-13**

Scripture speaks of God through encounters, declarations, commands, songs, and acts of deliverance. His names and descriptions belong within those accounts. The God who speaks to Moses also hears the afflicted; the Holy One whom Isaiah sees also provides cleansing; the God whose wisdom Paul praises is the God whose mercy he has been describing.

These readings introduce six connected themes. Ask what each passage says God is like, how that description appears in his actions, and what response follows. Knowing God involves receiving what he makes known, while recognising that receiving a true word about him is not the same as comprehending him without limit.

## 01 I AM hath sent me unto you

**Main reading:** Exodus 3:1–15.

### I have seen, and have heard

Moses is keeping Jethro’s flock when he comes to Horeb and sees the bush burning without being consumed. God calls him by name and tells him to remove his shoes because the ground is holy. He identifies himself as the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Moses hides his face in fear.

The encounter does not remain a spectacle for Moses to observe. The LORD speaks of Israel’s affliction in Egypt: he has seen it, heard their cry, and knows their sorrows. He announces deliverance and sends Moses to Pharaoh. Knowledge of God is connected here with God’s knowledge of the oppressed and his declared purpose to act.

When Moses asks who he is to undertake this task, the answer centres on God’s presence: he will be with him. The commission rests on more than Moses becoming impressed with his own ability. The one who sends him promises to accompany him.

### This is my name for ever

Moses asks what he should say when Israel asks the name of the God who sent him. God answers:

> And God said unto Moses, I AM THAT I AM: and he said, Thus shalt thou say unto the children of Israel, I AM hath sent me unto you.

Exodus 3:14 · KJV

The next verse again identifies him as the LORD God of their fathers and speaks of his name and memorial through generations. Read the declaration with that continuity, the promised presence, and the announced deliverance. The name is not offered as a detached puzzle with no relation to the people being addressed.

The scene begins with God making himself known and sending a messenger. Moses receives words to carry; he does not invent an identity for the one he has encountered. His questions are answered within a relationship and a task.

**Consider:** What does God say he knows before Moses asks his name? How does the promise of presence answer Moses’ concern about his own adequacy?

**Reflect:** Consider whether your reading attends to God’s stated actions as carefully as it attends to striking names and phrases.

## 02 Holy, holy, holy

**Main reading:** Isaiah 6:1–13.

### Mine eyes have seen the King

Isaiah dates the vision to the year King Uzziah died, then describes the Lord enthroned, high and lifted up. The seraphims call to one another:

> And one cried unto another, and said, Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts: the whole earth is full of his glory.

Isaiah 6:3 · KJV

The declaration accompanies shaking and smoke. Isaiah’s response is not to congratulate himself on unusual access. He confesses unclean lips and his place among a people of unclean lips because he has seen the King, the LORD of hosts.

The passage shows holiness through the encounter rather than giving a dictionary definition. God’s majesty exposes Isaiah’s uncleanness and calls forth reverence. The whole earth’s fullness of glory also prevents the scene from suggesting that God’s significance is confined to the temple Isaiah sees.

### Thine iniquity is taken away

A seraph takes a live coal from the altar, touches Isaiah’s mouth, and announces that his iniquity is taken away and his sin purged. Cleansing is provided; Isaiah does not first make himself clean enough to approach. His confession is followed by an action and a word addressed to the very lips he has named unclean.

Only then comes the question of who will go, and Isaiah’s answer that he will be sent. Read beyond that willing response. The commission speaks of hearing without understanding, judgment, and desolation, and the chapter ends with the image of the holy seed as the tree’s remaining substance. Being sent does not guarantee a readily receptive audience.

Holiness, cleansing, and a difficult commission belong in one account. Removing any of them changes the encounter into something easier and less searching than Isaiah describes.

**Consider:** What happens between Isaiah’s confession and his offer to go? How does the commission challenge a reading that stops at “send me”?

**Reflect:** Consider how acknowledging what is wrong and receiving mercy can precede service, rather than being distractions from it.

## 03 Merciful and gracious

**Main reading:** Exodus 34:1–9.

### Proclaimed the name of the LORD

Moses is told to prepare new stone tables like the first ones he broke and to come up the mountain. The LORD descends in the cloud and proclaims his name. The proclamation describes him as merciful and gracious, longsuffering, and abundant in goodness and truth.

The following words name keeping mercy and forgiving iniquity, transgression, and sin. These descriptions concern how God deals with wrongdoing and with people who need forgiveness. Mercy is not simply a pleasant impression Moses has formed. It is part of what the LORD declares about himself.

The KJV’s language of goodness and truth should remain visible when considering faithfulness. The proclamation joins generous mercy with a character upon which Moses may depend. It invites a response to the God speaking, not a comparison in which Moses chooses only the descriptions he finds agreeable.

### Pardon our iniquity and our sin

The same proclamation says that God will by no means clear the guilty and speaks of iniquity reaching through generations. Do not stop before these words in order to make mercy mean indifference to evil. Equally, do not let the warning erase the repeated language of mercy and forgiveness.

Moses bows and worships. He then asks the Lord to go among the people, acknowledges their stiffnecked character, and asks for pardon and for them to be taken as God’s inheritance. His prayer responds to the character just proclaimed. The confession of need accompanies the request for continued presence.

The passage does not explain every question about the relation of mercy, judgment, and consequences across generations. It does show Moses receiving the whole proclamation and appealing to God for forgiveness. Knowing this God leads him toward worship, confession, and intercession for the people.

**Consider:** Which words in the proclamation describe forgiveness, and which prevent it from being confused with treating guilt as insignificant? What does Moses ask in response?

**Reflect:** Consider whether your prayers make room for both honest confession and confidence in the mercy God declares.

## 04 He doth execute the judgment

**Main readings:** Deuteronomy 10:12–22; Psalm 96.

### The fatherless and widow

Deuteronomy asks what the LORD requires of Israel: reverence, walking in his ways, love, service, and keeping his commandments. The explanation speaks of the earth and heavens belonging to him, his love for the fathers, and the call for the people to cease being stiffnecked.

His greatness does not make him available to the highest bidder. He does not regard persons or take a reward. The next verse gives a concrete account of his justice:

> He doth execute the judgment of the fatherless and widow, and loveth the stranger, in giving him food and raiment.

Deuteronomy 10:18 · KJV

The command follows the description: Israel is to love the stranger, remembering its own experience as strangers in Egypt. God’s justice and love are reasons for how the people treat someone whose place among them might otherwise leave him vulnerable. The passage joins a confession of divine greatness to ordinary needs for food and clothing.

### He shall judge the world with righteousness

Psalm 96 summons the earth to sing and the nations to hear of God’s glory. It distinguishes the LORD who made the heavens from idols, calls for worship, and declares his reign. The creation’s joy then leads toward his coming to judge.

Judgment here is an occasion for rejoicing because it is righteous and true. The psalm does not imagine lasting peace as a world in which wrongdoing never matters. It looks to the just rule of the LORD.

Read these passages together without claiming that either supplies a complete account of every present injustice. Deuteronomy names the character Israel’s conduct should reflect; the psalm directs hope and praise toward God’s righteous judgment. Both challenge worship that has become indifferent to how people are treated.

**Consider:** How does Deuteronomy move from God’s greatness to the stranger’s needs? Why does the psalm’s announcement of judgment accompany joy?

**Reflect:** Consider one way your treatment of a person with little influence could reflect the impartiality and care described here.

## 05 O the depth of the riches

**Main reading:** Romans 11:25–36.

### Lest ye should be wise in your own conceits

Paul’s praise of divine wisdom concludes a discussion of Israel, the Gentiles, unbelief, calling, and mercy. He explicitly warns his readers against being wise in their own conceits. The doxology should not be separated from that call to humility.

The preceding verses speak of God’s covenant, his gifts and calling, and mercy received by people who had not believed. Paul is not praising an abstract intelligence unrelated to saving action. He marvels at the wisdom of the God whose ways with these people he has been describing.

> O the depth of the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God! how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out!

Romans 11:33 · KJV

The declaration acknowledges a depth beyond the reader’s mastery. It does not cancel the teaching Paul has just given. Instruction and wonder belong together: something has been made known, and the one making it known remains greater than the reader can exhaust.

### Of him, and through him, and to him

Paul asks who has known the Lord’s mind, served as his counsellor, or first given something that would place God in debt. The questions remove human claims to direct him or make him answerable as a debtor. The conclusion ascribes all things to him as source, means, and goal, and gives him glory.

This is a form of knowing marked by gratitude and worship. Paul reasons carefully, then recognises that he is not God’s adviser. The passage corrects both careless ignorance and the conceit that a reader has solved every question about God’s ways.

Let the setting govern the application. These verses do not make careful questions forbidden or provide an explanation for every individual tragedy. They teach humility before the wisdom revealed in God’s dealings and the mercy Paul proclaims.

**Consider:** What has Paul been discussing when he begins to praise God’s wisdom? What human claims do the three questions challenge?

**Reflect:** Consider a matter in which you need both more careful attention to what is written and less confidence that your understanding is complete.

## 06 He first loved us

**Main reading:** 1 John 4:7–21.

### In this was manifested the love of God

The passage begins by calling believers to love one another, because love is of God. It makes knowing God and loving inseparable, then gives content to the declaration that God is love. His love was manifested in sending his only begotten Son so that people might live through him.

The initiative belongs to God. The writer says that love is shown not in people first loving God, but in God loving them and sending his Son to be the propitiation for their sins. The passage identifies an act, a person sent, and the need of sinners. It does not leave love as a word into which readers may put any meaning they choose.

The conclusion follows: if God loved them in this way, they ought to love one another. The divine action is both the foundation of their life and the reason for their responsibility.

### That he who loveth God love his brother also

The passage speaks of the unseen God, the gift of the Spirit, testimony that the Father sent the Son, and confession of Jesus as the Son of God. Love is placed within this witness rather than detached from it.

It also connects perfected love with confidence in the day of judgment and speaks of love casting out fear. The nearby reference to judgment and torment helps identify the fear in view; the sentence should not become a rebuke of every startled, anxious, or grieving person.

> We love him, because he first loved us.

1 John 4:19 · KJV

The closing test is concrete. Someone who claims love for God while hating his brother contradicts that claim. The person seen cannot simply be neglected while devotion is directed toward the God unseen. The command joins love for God and love for the brother.

Knowing God therefore reaches beyond possessing correct descriptions. In this passage, receiving the witness to his love must affect how believers live with others. The claim to know him is examined by the life that accompanies it.

**Consider:** How does the sending of the Son explain “God is love”? Why does the passage conclude with the brother who can be seen?

**Reflect:** Consider a relationship in which a claim to love God needs to become a concrete act of love toward another person.

These passages give names, words, actions, and calls to respond. God makes himself known as holy, merciful, just, wise, and loving; the readings lead toward reverence, confession, trust, worship, and care for others. Keep those responses joined to the accounts from which they arise.
