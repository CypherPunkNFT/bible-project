**Scripture source.** Every Bible quotation in [content.md](content.md) is from the Berean Standard Bible, copied from
the site's own text in [data/text/bsb/](../../../../data/text/bsb/) and checked by script on 2026-11-06: each quoted

proves the words are the Berean's and sit in the verses cited; it does not prove the reading of them is right.

**Counts, and how they were made.** From the site's Berean text, by script, 2026-11-06, counting verses (not
occurrences) that contain the word, whole word, any case:
- "angel", "angels" or "archangel": 295 verses (109 Old Testament, 186 New Testament).
- "demon", "demons" or "demon-possessed": 73 verses (4 Old Testament, 69 New Testament).
- "Satan": 47 verses (14 Old Testament, 33 New Testament). "devil": 33 verses, all New Testament.
- "Gabriel": 4 verses (Daniel 8:16; 9:21; Luke 1:19, 26). "Michael": 15 verses, but most of the Old Testament ones are
  men of that name; the angel Michael is named only in Daniel 10:13, 21; 12:1; Jude 9; Revelation 12:7.
The draft uses none of these numbers; they are here for the design pass's figures bar, and each carries its counting
rule. A count of verses is not a count of passages about angels: "the angel of the LORD", the "host of heaven",
cherubim and seraphim and "sons of God" are not all caught by these words.

**Scope.** What Scripture says about the unseen world and where it stops, in seven parts: angels as creatures, not to be
worshipped (01); angels sent to serve, with guardian angels shown as an open question (02); the angel of the LORD, two
readings shown (03); Satan and the fallen angels, with three readings of where his fall is told (04); demons and the
authority of Jesus, with what the text does not give (05); Christ's victory at the cross over the powers (06); the
believer's stand, with the occult forbidden and Daniel 10 read with its limits (07). The way of reading taught is three
questions: what does the text actually say; where does it stop; where does it point. The signature move is the third
heading in every part, "What Scripture leaves unsaid", which carries Deuteronomy 29:29 through the whole study.

**Boundaries kept.** Study 18, The signs and works of Jesus, owns the man with the legion of spirits (Mark 5) and the
question "a demon or an illness?"; this study cites Matthew 4:24 for the same point in one sentence and links there.
Study 15, The kingdom he announced, owns Luke 11:20 ("the finger of God"); this study uses Matthew 12:28–29 (the strong
man bound) instead. Study 25, What the cross accomplishes, owns the victory over the powers as one of six pictures of
the cross; part 06 here reads it from the side of the powers and links there. Study 30, The hope to come, owns the final
judgment, hell and the millennium; part 06 names the end in two verses and sends the reader there. Study 50, Genesis
1–11, owns the "sons of God" of Genesis 6 and names this study; this study does not reopen Genesis 6. Study 49, Reading
Daniel and Revelation, owns how Daniel's and Revelation's visions work; this study uses Daniel 10 and Revelation 12 only
for what they say about angels and Satan.

**Length.** About 3,700 words, above the brief's guide of 2,000–2,600, in line with the recent drafts of the sequence
(Studies 48–50 run from about 4,200 to 5,500). Seven parts each carry an open question with named views; cutting one
would leave a common question unanswered. The design pass may split the three "views" lists into record sheets.

## Context, readings and limits

**2 Kings 6:15–17.** The story is a real event, not a parable; the draft uses it as a hook and draws only what the text
says: God opened the servant's eyes. It does not promise that believers will see angels, and the draft says so in the
closing ("You may never see an angel").

**Colossians 1:16; 2:15, 18.** "Thrones or dominions or rulers or authorities" (1:16) and "the powers and authorities"
(2:15) use the same family of words. Readers differ on whether Paul means only spiritual beings or also earthly
structures behind which they stand; the draft does not take up that question and treats them as unseen powers, as
2:15 and Ephesians 6:12 require at least. Colossians 2:18 is a hard verse in its details ("what he has seen"); the
draft uses only its plain warning against "the worship of angels".

**Hebrews 1:14 and the guardian angel question.** Three views are shown, each from a source read directly: the
Orthodox Longer Catechism, question 106 ("Without doubt"); Calvin, *Institutes* I.14.7 (uncertain, and of little
importance); Boyce, *Abstract of Systematic Theology*, chapter 18 (no scriptural authority). The Baptist Faith and
Message says nothing on it. Boyce also reports that Moses Stuart, in *Bibliotheca Sacra*, argued for guardian angels;
Stuart was not read and is not named in the draft.

**The angel of the LORD.** In Genesis 16, Exodus 3 and Judges 13 the angel speaks as God and is recognised as God. The
two readings shown are both old and both held by Baptists: Justin, *Dialogue with Trypho* 56 and Calvin, *Institutes*
I.13.10 (the Son before the incarnation); Augustine, *On the Trinity* III.11 (a created angel speaking in God's
person). The draft does not choose and says that the New Testament does not decide it. It does not say that Christ
"is" the angel of the LORD in the site's voice, because the New Testament does not draw that line (master prompt, Stage
2: a figure of Christ is claimed as such only where the New Testament draws it).

**Job 1–2 and Zechariah 3.** In both, the Hebrew has "the satan", the accuser, with the article. The draft follows the
Berean, which writes "Satan", and does not raise the article; the design pass may add a note if wanted. The point drawn
is the one the text makes twice: every limit is set by the LORD.

**Isaiah 14 and Ezekiel 28.** Both are addressed to human kings (Isaiah 14:4, "the king of Babylon"; Ezekiel 28:12,
"the king of Tyre"). Three readings are shown: Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine* III.37 (words spoken of the devil
"under the figure of the king of Babylon"); Jamieson, Fausset and Brown on Isaiah 14:12–15 (the Babylonian king
"primarily", foreshadowing Antichrist); and Boyce, who grounds Satan's sin of pride on 1 Timothy 3:6 without leaning on
either prophet. Boyce does not reject the Isaiah reading in the passage read; the draft does not say that he does. The
draft does not use the name "Lucifer", which is the Latin translation's word and is not in the Berean.

**2 Peter 2:4 and Jude 6.** Both say that angels sinned and are held for judgment. Whether these are the angels of
Genesis 6:1–4 is a question Study 50 shows; this study does not reopen it.

**Mark 1:23–34.** Why Jesus silenced the demons is not explained in the passage beyond "because they knew who He was"
(1:34). The draft asks the question and does not answer it for the reader.

**Can a believer be troubled by a demon from within?** The draft sets out the texts on each side (Colossians 1:13;
1 John 5:18; Ephesians 4:27) and does not choose. Acts 5:3 ("Satan has filled your heart") and Luke 22:3 ("Satan
entered Judas") were left out of the reader's text: whether Ananias was a believer is itself disputed, and Judas was
not one, so neither settles the question. No outside source on this question was read for this draft; see the open
questions.

**Colossians 2:13–15 with Revelation 12:10–11.** The draft's sentence "The accuser's weapon was our guilt; at the cross
the debt was cancelled" is the site's own reading, from the order of Colossians 2:13–15 (forgiveness, then triumph)
and Revelation 12:10–11 (the accuser thrown down, conquered "by the blood of the Lamb"). It is marked as meaning, not
as quotation.

**Daniel 10.** Calvin, *Institutes* I.14.7, reads the princes of Persia and Greece as angels appointed over kingdoms;
Boyce, *Abstract* chapter 18, doubts that guardian angels over nations can be proved from it, and quotes J. Pye Smith
that Michael is "probably the Messiah" (Smith was not read and is not named in the draft). The draft's sentence
"Scripture gives no command to map spirits or to speak to them" is an observation about what the text contains; it
names no movement. Daniel 10:2–3 (Daniel's fast) and 10:12 (his prayer was heard) are the textual basis.

**Deuteronomy 18:10–12.** Quoted for the list of forbidden practices. The Reflect question applies it to the reader
without naming any practice, to avoid putting an application in Scripture's mouth.

## Doctrine

- **Baptist Faith and Message 2000.** Article III names Satan as the tempter in the fall; the draft quotes it. Article
  X (last things) affirms hell as "the place of everlasting punishment"; the draft's part 06 states the devil's end from
  Matthew 25:41 and Revelation 20:10 and sends the details to Study 30. The confession does not set out a doctrine of
  angels; where it is silent (guardian angels; the angel of the LORD; where Satan's fall is told; whether a believer can
  be troubled by a demon from within; angels over nations) the draft shows the views with who holds them and does not
  choose.
- **In the site's own voice** the draft says: angels are created and are not to be worshipped or prayed to; they serve
  God for his people; Satan is a real, personal, fallen creature under God's limit; demons are real and subject to
  Christ; Christ disarmed the powers at the cross and they will be finally judged; believers resist by submitting to God
  and standing in his armour; occult practice is forbidden. Each is a plain statement of Scripture.
- **Other traditions.** The Orthodox Longer Catechism is quoted twice and labelled as Orthodox teaching. The nine
  orders of angels (from the *Celestial Hierarchy*, written under the name of Dionysius) are mentioned only as later
  writers' construction, with Calvin's judgment of it; Roman Catholic teaching on angels is not quoted in this draft.
- **Prayer.** The draft says angels are "never offered as someone to pray to or call on" and that "every prayer in
  Scripture goes to God". This is the Second London Confession's line (22.2) and the plain pattern of Scripture; it is
  where Baptists differ from traditions that ask angels to intercede.

## Open editorial questions

1. **Section.** The ranked list notes that no field fits angels well and offers a sixth, "The unseen world", as the
   alternative. This draft is filed in God and Christian Faith as the brief directs. The owner may move it.
2. **Can a believer be troubled by a demon from within?** The draft shows the texts and does not choose, but names no
   one who holds each view, because no outside source on this question was read. Before publication, either read and
   cite named works on each side (for example Clinton E. Arnold, *3 Crucial Questions about Spiritual Warfare*, 1997,
   for the view that believers can be strongly afflicted; and a Baptist systematic theology for the view that they
   cannot be possessed; neither was read for this draft), or keep it as a text-only question.
3. **The article in Job 1–2 and Zechariah 3.** Whether to tell the reader that the Hebrew says "the satan" (the accuser).
   It is accurate and helps explain the name; it needs a named lexicon source if added.
4. **Length.** About 3,700 words against a guide of 2,000–2,600. If it must be cut, part 03 (the angel of the LORD) is
   the most self-contained and could become a record sheet.
5. **Words like "principalities".** The Berean uses "rulers", "authorities", "powers" and once "principalities"
   (Romans 8:38). The draft quotes them as they come; a short word-list card may help the design pass.
6. **Pictures.** None is proposed in a content draft. Candidates for the design pass, for the owner to approve, none
   downloaded: an original line drawing of the hills "full of horses and chariots of fire" (2 Kings 6:17), drawn for
