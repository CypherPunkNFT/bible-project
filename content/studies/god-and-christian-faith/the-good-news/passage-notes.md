# Passage notes: The good news (54)

## Luke 15:1–32 (owned)

**Context.** Luke 15 follows Jesus' sayings on the cost of following Him (14:25–35). It answers the complaint of 15:1–2, so all three parables are addressed to the Pharisees and scribes, with tax collectors and sinners listening. The sheep (15:3–7) and coin (15:8–10) share a frame: something lost, a search, a find, a call to rejoice, and a saying about joy over one sinner who repents. The two-sons parable (15:11–32) lengthens this pattern and adds a second lost son who stays at home. It ends without saying how the older son responds. Luke 16 continues with parables about wealth.

**Main readings and who holds them.**
- *God's seeking and welcoming love, with the older son as Jesus' critics.* This is the mainstream reading, taken from 15:1–2. It is held across traditions; for example, Helmut Thielicke's sermons published in English as *The Waiting Father* centre the father.
- *Two lost sons.* Timothy Keller, *The Prodigal God* (2008), argues that the older brother is equally lost and that the parable is aimed at him. The study's closing question draws on this emphasis without quoting Keller.
- *Cultural reading.* Kenneth E. Bailey (*Poet and Peasant*; *The Cross and the Prodigal*) argued that the son's request and the father's running would have been shocking in a Middle Eastern village. [check: confirm which book and page before citing Bailey on the page; the study currently does not cite him.]
- *Allegorical/ethnic reading.* Some readers in the early church took the younger son as the Gentiles and the older as Israel. [check: named patristic source, if this is to be mentioned.] The study does not adopt this, and the site avoids any reading that sets the Jewish people as the resentful brother.
- The study flags the limit that the parable pictures welcome but not atonement. Some readers, from various traditions, have argued that the parable shows forgiveness without the cross; the study answers by linking Romans 3:25–26 and the cross study.

## Acts 2:22–24 and 2:36–41 (owned)

**Context.** These verses are Peter's Pentecost sermon (Acts 2:14–36) and the crowd's response. The opening events (2:1–21) belong to *The Holy Spirit* (23). Peter's reading of Psalms 16 and 110 (2:25–35) belongs to *The book of Psalms* (48); this study quotes 2:31–32 only. The setting is Shavuot, with Jewish pilgrims from many lands. Peter is a Jew addressing fellow Jews ("Men of Israel", "all Israel").

**Main readings and who holds them.**
- *2:23, "you … put Him to death."* This is read as Peter's address to the Jerusalem crowd present weeks after the crucifixion, joined with "the hands of the lawless" (Romans) and God's "set plan". Acts 4:27–28 widens the responsibility to Herod, Pilate, Gentiles and Israel. The study, following the plan, places guilt on all humanity (Isaiah 53:6) and never on the Jewish people as a people.
- *2:23, God's plan and human responsibility side by side.* Both are affirmed in the text. Calvinist and non-Calvinist Baptists explain the relation differently (article V). The study states both without choosing a system.
- *2:38, "for the forgiveness of your sins."* Baptists (article VII) read baptism as the believer's act of obedience, joined closely to faith but not its cause. Stone-Campbell churches (Churches of Christ) teach baptism as the point at which forgiveness is received [check: quote from one of their own documents before naming them on the page; currently not named]. Lutheran and Roman Catholic teaching holds that baptism conveys grace; the study quotes Luther's Small Catechism, in the wording already used on the site.
- *2:39, "you and your children."* Presbyterian and Reformed readers cite this for baptizing believers' infants (covenant promise). Baptists note that the verse continues "to all whom the Lord our God will call", and that 2:41 baptizes "those who embraced his message". The study does not argue this here; it belongs to the baptism study.
- *2:41.* The order is that people embraced the message and were then baptized. This is part of the Baptist case.

**Links.** *The Holy Spirit* (2:1–21); *The book of Psalms* (2:25–36); baptism study (quotes 2:38–41); *Who do you say that I am*; *What the cross accomplishes*; *Christ in the Old Testament* (Isaiah 53, Acts 8).
