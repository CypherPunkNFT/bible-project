# Covenants and promises

**Study 26 · God and Christian Faith · First content draft · 2026-10-13**

God makes promises to people in particular places and generations. Some concern the earth and its creatures; others concern a family, a nation, a royal house, or the forgiveness and renewal promised in a new covenant. Later Scripture returns to these words to explain God's faithfulness and the work of Christ.

Following these connections requires attention to what each passage says: who speaks, to whom, what is promised, what response is required, and whether a sign is given. A promise to Noah is not identical to a command at Sinai. The promise to David has its own setting before Luke speaks of Jesus receiving David's throne. Read the connections without losing the people and circumstances in which the promises were first heard.

## 01 My covenant with you

**Main reading:** Genesis 9:1–17.

### With every living creature

After the flood, God blesses Noah and his sons and tells them to be fruitful and multiply. The opening instructions address food, blood, and human life, grounding the seriousness of shedding human blood in God's making humanity in his image.

God then establishes his covenant with Noah, his descendants, and the living creatures that came out of the ark. Its reach extends beyond one family as a religious community. The stated promise concerns the preservation of life from another destroying flood:

> And I will establish my covenant with you; neither shall all flesh be cut off any more by the waters of a flood; neither shall there any more be a flood to destroy the earth.

Genesis 9:11 · KJV

The wording defines the promise. It is not a guarantee that no local flood, disaster, or death will ever occur. The covenant addresses the destruction just narrated and the future of the earth's living creatures.

### The token of the covenant

God appoints the bow in the cloud as the covenant's token. The passage repeatedly speaks of God remembering his covenant when the bow appears. Attention rests on his commitment to what he has said.

The sign does not create the promise through the observer's feelings. It points to God's declared faithfulness across generations. A reader can therefore distinguish the promise itself from personal circumstances that may change from one day to another.

This first reading also establishes a useful question for the others: how wide is the covenant's stated scope? Here the answer includes descendants and living creatures. Later passages name different recipients and responsibilities; they should be allowed to specify their own terms.

**Consider:** Who is included in this covenant? What precisely does God promise, and what does the sign recall?

**Reflect:** Consider how paying attention to a promise's wording can strengthen trust without turning it into a guarantee the passage does not give.

## 02 In thee shall all families be blessed

**Main readings:** Genesis 12:1–7; 15:1–21; 17:1–22.

### A land and a seed

God calls Abram to leave his country and kindred for a land he will show him. The promises include a great nation, blessing, and blessing reaching all families of the earth through him. On arriving in Canaan, Abram hears that the land will be given to his seed.

Genesis 15 brings Abram's childlessness into the conversation. God promises an heir from his own body and directs him to the stars as a picture of his descendants. Abram believes the LORD, and this is counted to him for righteousness.

The chapter then names a covenant and a land promise. It also speaks of descendants living as strangers, being afflicted, and returning after a period of delay. The promise is not presented as immediate possession without hardship. Abram hears about a future extending beyond his own lifetime.

### To be a God unto thee

Genesis 17 develops the covenant with Abraham and his descendants:

> And I will establish my covenant between me and thee and thy seed after thee in their generations for an everlasting covenant, to be a God unto thee, and to thy seed after thee.

Genesis 17:7 · KJV

The chapter includes a new name, nations and kings, the land of Canaan, and circumcision as a covenant token. It also requires covenant keeping and names the consequence of refusing the sign. God's initiative does not make the stated obligations disappear.

Sarah is named as the mother of the promised Isaac. Ishmael receives a promise of blessing and a great nation, while the covenant is specifically to be established with Isaac. Distinguishing those statements respects the passage's care in naming people and promises.

These chapters hold together family, land, blessing, faith, and relationship with God. Later writers draw on particular parts of this account; their arguments become clearer when these original features remain visible.

**Consider:** What does each of the three chapters add? Where does the narrative explicitly name a covenant, and where does it first announce promises?

**Reflect:** Consider what Abraham's waiting shows about receiving a promise whose fulfilment reaches beyond the present moment.

## 03 A kingdom of priests

**Main readings:** Exodus 19:1–8; 20:1–21; 24:1–11. **Wider setting:** Exodus 19–24.

### I brought you unto myself

At Sinai, God first recalls what he has done: he delivered Israel from Egypt and brought the people to himself. The instructions are addressed to a people already brought out of bondage.

God then sets obedience and covenant keeping before them, naming their calling as his peculiar treasure, a kingdom of priests, and a holy nation. The people answer that they will do all the LORD has spoken. The sequence matters: deliverance is remembered, then covenant responsibility is declared.

The commandments likewise begin with the identity of the LORD who brought them out of Egypt. They address worship, his name, sabbath, parents, human life, marriage, property, testimony, and desire. Covenant life reaches into relationships and ordinary conduct as well as the people's approach to God.

### The blood of the covenant

Exodus 24 describes Moses reporting the words and judgments, writing the words, and reading the book of the covenant before the people. Their response again promises obedience. Offerings and blood accompany this solemn undertaking:

> And Moses took the blood, and sprinkled it on the people, and said, Behold the blood of the covenant, which the LORD hath made with you concerning all these words.

Exodus 24:8 · KJV

The words, the people's response, and the blood belong together in the account. The chapter also describes Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu, and the elders going up, seeing the God of Israel, and eating and drinking.

Sinai should be read on its own terms before later contrasts are considered. Exodus does not present the people as rescuing themselves from Egypt through their obedience. It does present obedience as a serious responsibility within the relationship God establishes. Later biblical accounts of covenant failure depend on that responsibility being real.

**Consider:** What has God already done before giving the commandments? Which actions establish the covenant in Exodus 24?

**Reflect:** Consider how remembering deliverance might shape obedience differently from trying to make yourself your own deliverer.

## 04 Thy throne shall be established

**Main reading:** 2 Samuel 7:1–29. **Compare:** Psalm 89:3–4, 19–37; Luke 1:30–33.

### He will make thee an house

David wants to build a house for the ark of God. Through Nathan, the LORD recalls taking David from the sheepfold, accompanying him, and giving him rest. The speech turns the proposal around: the LORD will make David a house.

The promise concerns a descendant, a kingdom, and a throne. A son will build a house for God's name. The speech includes a father-and-son relationship, discipline for iniquity, and mercy that will not depart as it did from Saul.

> And thine house and thy kingdom shall be established for ever before thee: thy throne shall be established for ever.

2 Samuel 7:16 · KJV

The movement from David's building project to his enduring house concerns a royal line, not simply the construction of a building. David responds with wonder, remembrance of God's acts for Israel, and a prayer that God establish what he has spoken.

### The throne of his father David

Psalm 89 explicitly calls God's commitment to David a covenant. It remembers the oath and enduring throne while retaining discipline if David's children forsake God's law. The promise should not be confused with approval of every action by every king.

Luke's announcement to Mary then names David's throne in describing Jesus. He will be called the Son of the Highest, reign over the house of Jacob, and have a kingdom without end. The Gospel identifies Jesus' kingship through words already carrying a history of promise.

Keep the stages of that history visible. The warning about a royal son's iniquity belongs to the account of David's house; it should not be transferred mechanically into a claim that Jesus sinned. Luke's appeal to the throne connects Jesus to the promise without making every sentence about David's descendants an identical description of him.

**Consider:** How does the meaning of house develop in Nathan's speech? Which parts of the promise appear again in Luke?

**Reflect:** Consider how David's prayer responds to a promise already given rather than presenting an achievement God must reward.

## 05 I will make a new covenant

**Main readings:** Jeremiah 31:27–37; Luke 22:14–20; Hebrews 8:1–13.

### I will write it in their hearts

Jeremiah announces a new covenant with the house of Israel and the house of Judah. The contrast is with the covenant made when God brought their fathers out of Egypt, which they broke. The promise addresses that history of failure.

> But this shall be the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel; After those days, saith the LORD, I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts; and will be their God, and they shall be my people.

Jeremiah 31:33 · KJV

The next verse joins knowing the LORD with forgiveness and sins remembered no more. Renewal is described as God's law written within, not as the disappearance of all concern for his will. The surrounding verses also speak of Israel's continuing place before God, so the named recipients remain important to the reading.

### The mediator of a better covenant

At the supper, Jesus describes the cup as the new testament in his blood, shed for his disciples. Covenant language is thus joined to his approaching death. The meal recalls his self-giving rather than an agreement secured by the disciples' achievements.

Hebrews calls Jesus the mediator of a better covenant established on better promises and quotes Jeremiah's announcement at length. It then reasons from the word new that the first covenant has become old. The argument concerns Christ's ministry, forgiveness, and the promised relationship with God.

These passages create questions that Christian interpretations must address together: Jeremiah names Israel and Judah; Jesus speaks of his blood; Hebrews applies the promise to explain his present ministry. An account that omits any of these features will miss part of the connection the readings establish.

**Consider:** What failure does Jeremiah name, and what does God promise to do? How does Hebrews use the word new in its argument?

**Reflect:** Consider how forgiveness and inward obedience belong together in this promise.

## 06 Heirs according to the promise

**Main readings:** Galatians 3:6–29; 2 Corinthians 3:3–18; Romans 11:17–32.

### The law cannot disannul

Galatians returns to Abraham to explain the blessing of Gentiles through faith. Paul says that the law, coming later, cannot cancel the earlier promise. He identifies the promised seed with Christ and describes those belonging to Christ as Abraham's seed and heirs.

> And if ye be Christ’s, then are ye Abraham’s seed, and heirs according to the promise.

Galatians 3:29 · KJV

Paul also asks whether the law is against God's promises and rejects that conclusion. He explains its role in relation to transgressions and the coming of Christ. His argument distinguishes promise and law without treating God as having abandoned his earlier word.

Second Corinthians describes a ministry of the new testament and the Spirit's life-giving work, contrasting writing on stone with writing on hearts. Romans 11 warns Gentile believers against boasting over the branches and joins God's covenant with the removal of sins. Participation in blessing calls for humility toward those whose history carries the promises.

### Reading the promises together

Christian theological frameworks explain these connections differently. The Westminster Confession describes a covenant of works and a covenant of grace, with the latter administered differently under law and gospel while remaining one in substance. This is its broader synthesis of Scripture. See [chapter 7](https://opc.org/wcf.html).

Dallas Theological Seminary's dispensational statement distinguishes successive responsibilities under God's administration, while explicitly teaching salvation by grace through faith on the basis of Christ's death in every age. It also maintains a distinction between Israel and the church. See [articles V and XIII](https://www.dts.edu/why-dts/doctrinal-statement).

These are representative formulations, not the only Christian readings. They organise the connections differently; their names should not replace examining the passages. Begin with the promises and recipients the text names, then ask how each interpretation accounts for continuity, change, Christ, and the inclusion of Gentiles.

The readings give reasons for both confidence and care: God keeps his word, while readers must learn what he has actually promised. Following a promise through Scripture means attending to its first setting and to the later writer's argument, rather than claiming every promise as an immediate personal guarantee.

**Consider:** What does Paul say the law cannot do to the promise? How does Romans qualify the attitude Gentile believers should have?

**Reflect:** Choose one promise from this study. Name its first recipients, its stated content, and any later passage that draws on it before considering its application to you.
