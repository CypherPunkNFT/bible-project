# Grace faith and salvation

**Study 24 · God and Christian Faith · First content draft · 2026-10-13**

The New Testament speaks of salvation through God's mercy, Christ's death and resurrection, and the Spirit's renewing work. It calls people to repent and believe, excludes boasting, and describes a life in which faith works rather than remaining an empty claim.

Read the arguments before turning their words into a formula. Ephesians contrasts death with life and then speaks of good works. Romans asks how the ungodly are justified. Titus joins mercy and renewal to changed conduct. James examines a claimed faith without action. First John writes so believers may know the life God gives in his Son.

## 01 It is the gift of God

**Main reading:** Ephesians 2:1–10.

### But God, who is rich in mercy

Ephesians begins this passage with people dead in trespasses and sins, walking in disobedience and governed by desires. The description includes the writer and his community in its account of former life. It does not establish a class of outsiders whose need can be discussed from a position of personal innocence.

The turning point is God's mercy and love. God makes them alive with Christ, raises them with him, and speaks of the riches of grace shown in kindness through Christ Jesus. The initiative belongs to God within the very condition the opening verses describe.

> For by grace are ye saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God:

Ephesians 2:8 · KJV

The next verse explicitly excludes works as a ground for boasting. Salvation is not presented as wages God owes to people whose achievements have placed him in their debt. Faith appears within receiving this saving grace, not as a reason to congratulate oneself over another person.

### Created in Christ Jesus unto good works

Verse 10 follows the exclusion of boasting by calling believers God's workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works. The paragraph does not move from “not of works” to “works do not matter.” It distinguishes the gift that saves from the life for which people are made alive.

Notice the movement in walking. The opening describes a former walk in sins; the closing describes walking in good works God has prepared. Grace changes the setting and direction of life. The works are not smuggled back in as a price paid before mercy becomes available, but neither are they discarded from the passage's purpose.

**Consider:** Who acts at the paragraph's turning point? How does verse 10 complete the thought begun in verses 8–9?

**Reflect:** Consider whether you tend to turn good works into grounds for boasting or to use grace as a reason to neglect them. Read the whole paragraph against that tendency.

## 02 Repent ye, and believe the gospel

**Main readings:** Mark 1:14–15; Acts 20:17–24.

### The kingdom of God is at hand

Jesus' announcement in Galilee joins fulfilled time and the nearness of God's kingdom with the call to repent and believe the gospel. The response is not detached from the announcement: hearers are being addressed by the one proclaiming what God is doing.

Keep both imperatives. Repentance is not replaced by mere interest in religious information, and belief is not replaced by a programme of self-repair. The words call hearers toward the gospel Jesus proclaims. This brief announcement does not supply a detailed sequence by which every conversion must unfold.

It also does not provide an emotional threshold that every reader must reproduce. The direction of response matters more than a performance of the same outward intensity by everyone who hears.

### Repentance toward God, and faith toward our Lord Jesus Christ

In Acts 20, Paul recalls his ministry to the Ephesian elders: humility, tears, trials, public teaching, and teaching from house to house. He summarises its testimony:

> Testifying both to the Jews, and also to the Greeks, repentance toward God, and faith toward our Lord Jesus Christ.

Acts 20:21 · KJV

The same address describes his task as testifying to the gospel of God's grace. The call to repentance and faith therefore appears alongside grace in Paul's own account, rather than as a competing message. His testimony is directed to Jews and Greeks, not restricted to one group presumed naturally closer to salvation.

The passage gives repentance and faith their direction: toward God and toward the Lord Jesus Christ. They should not become an inward inspection detached from the one to whom the hearer is called. Read the response through the message and person to which it responds.

**Consider:** What announcement precedes Jesus' commands? How does Paul's reference to grace frame his description of repentance and faith?

**Reflect:** Consider whether your attention has become fixed on evaluating the intensity of your response while losing sight of the gospel to which you are responding.

## 03 Justified freely by his grace

**Main reading:** Romans 3:21–5:11.

### Where is boasting then?

Romans speaks of God's righteousness witnessed by the law and prophets, of all having sinned, and of being justified freely through the redemption in Christ Jesus. Christ's blood and God's righteousness belong to the explanation. God is described as just and as the justifier of the person believing in Jesus.

Paul asks where boasting is and answers that it is excluded. He then asks whether God belongs only to Jews, answering that he is God of Gentiles too. The argument concerns how sinners stand before God and the shared basis on which people are received, not simply a private feeling of relief.

Abraham's faith and David's words about forgiveness develop the account. Paul contrasts a worker's wage, reckoned as a debt, with righteousness counted to the one who believes in the God who justifies the ungodly. He also insists that Abraham's faith was reckoned before circumcision, so that Abraham's fatherhood includes uncircumcised believers.

### Peace with God

The Abraham argument leads toward those who believe in the God who raised Jesus from the dead. Paul then writes:

> Therefore being justified by faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ:

Romans 5:1 · KJV

The following verses include access, hope, tribulation, and God's love. Peace with God does not mean there are no difficulties left to endure. Paul grounds confidence in Christ dying for people described as without strength, ungodly, sinners, and enemies.

Christian formulations differ over how justification and inward renewal relate. The Westminster Confession distinguishes justification from inwardly making someone righteous and describes faith as its sole instrument, while insisting that this faith is accompanied by love and other graces. See [chapter 11](https://opc.org/wcf.html). The Catholic Catechism includes forgiveness, sanctification, and renewal within justification and connects it with faith and baptism, while grounding it in Christ's Passion and prior grace. See [paragraphs 1987–2001](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_one/chapter_three/article_2/grace_and_justification.index.html).

These are attributed formulations, not interchangeable definitions or an exhaustive survey. Neither should be caricatured as permission to remain unchanged or an ability to earn initial grace without God's action.

**Consider:** Why does Paul discuss both wages and circumcision? What grounds the confidence described in Romans 5?

**Reflect:** Consider whether your claim to stand before God rests in the mercy and work described here or in comparison with someone else's performance.

## 04 According to his mercy he saved us

**Main reading:** Titus 3:1–8.

### We ourselves also were sometimes foolish

Titus joins readiness for good work with gentleness toward other people. Its reason includes remembering the former life: foolishness, disobedience, deception, desires, malice, envy, and mutual hatred. That recollection challenges contempt in people who now know mercy.

The passage turns to the appearing of God's kindness and love:

> Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us, by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost;

Titus 3:5 · KJV

The next verses name the Spirit poured out through Jesus Christ, justification by grace, and becoming heirs according to the hope of eternal life. New life is described through God's saving action rather than a human record that made mercy deserved.

### Renewing of the Holy Ghost

Keep the washing and renewing language within the sentence. The passage is not describing only an external change of label with no relation to life. It names regeneration and the Spirit's renewal, and then directs those who have believed in God to maintain good works.

The relation of this washing language to baptism is an interpretive question requiring more than deleting the word or treating it as self-explanatory. The Catholic formulation linked above includes baptism in justification; this short reading retains Titus's wording without pretending to settle every sacramental question from the phrase alone.

What the paragraph does say clearly is that mercy, the Spirit, Jesus Christ, grace, inheritance, and good works belong together. Its opening and closing concern conduct toward others. Remembering salvation by mercy is meant to help form a gentle, useful life rather than a superior attitude toward those still described by the former pattern.

**Consider:** Why does the instruction to be gentle recall the readers' former conduct? What relationship does the paragraph establish between mercy and good works?

**Reflect:** Consider someone you are tempted to despise. How would remembering your own dependence on mercy affect your response?

## 05 That ye may know

**Main reading:** 1 John 5:1–13.

### This life is in his Son

First John connects believing that Jesus is the Christ with birth from God, love for God's children, and keeping his commandments. Faith is directed toward Jesus as the Son of God. The passage then speaks of God's testimony concerning him and of receiving that testimony.

Its central declaration is that God has given eternal life and that this life is in his Son. Assurance is therefore not directed toward an independent possession detached from Christ. The distinction is stated sharply: the person who has the Son has life; the person who does not have the Son does not have life.

Keep the focus of the testimony visible. The passage is not asking the reader to manufacture certainty about an unspecified religious experience. It concerns the Son whom God attests and the life given in him.

### Written unto you that believe

The writer identifies both the audience and the purpose:

> These things have I written unto you that believe on the name of the Son of God; that ye may know that ye have eternal life, and that ye may believe on the name of the Son of God.

1 John 5:13 · KJV

The purpose of knowing is real; the letter need not be read as though it were designed only to make its hearers uncertain. Yet the same reading includes love and commandments, so assurance should not be severed from everything the letter says about believing life.

Neither should those descriptions become a claim that momentary emotional confidence or flawless performance is the source of life. The source named is God's gift in his Son. Read the testimony, faith, love, and obedience together before drawing wider conclusions about perseverance or the possibility of falling away. Those doctrinal questions require the fuller arguments in which Christians address them.

**Consider:** What has God given, and where is that life found? How does the stated purpose guide the way you read the surrounding instructions?

**Reflect:** Consider how attending to the testimony concerning Christ differs from checking whether you feel equally certain every day.

## 06 Faith without works is dead

**Main reading:** James 2:14–26. **Compare:** Romans 4:1–8; Ephesians 2:8–10.

### What doth it profit?

James begins with someone saying he has faith while lacking works. His first example is a brother or sister without clothing and daily food. A farewell wishing warmth and satisfaction does not supply what the body needs. The example tests the claim by the actual neighbour left unaided.

James then speaks of showing faith through works and notes that even devils believe there is one God and tremble. An acknowledged proposition does not by itself establish the living response his argument seeks. The issue is not whether the claim sounds religiously correct, but what kind of faith it is.

Read the concrete need before rushing toward a slogan about the relation of faith and works. The paragraph begins with an urgent question about a profession that produces no help.

### Faith wrought with his works

James names Abraham offering Isaac, describes faith working with his actions and being made perfect through them, and cites the Scripture that Abraham believed God and it was counted as righteousness. He also names Rahab receiving and sending out the messengers. The conclusion compares faith without works to a body without spirit: dead.

Do not erase James's statement that a person is justified by works and not by faith only. Nor should reading it erase Paul's account of the ungodly justified by faith apart from works. The first task is to observe the arguments: Romans challenges boasting and the claim of wages owed; James challenges a profession left inactive. Both use Abraham, but attend to different actions and questions.

How their uses of justification relate is part of the doctrinal discussion, not something resolved by pretending one author used the other's exact sentence. Ephesians supplies a further connection: salvation is God's gift, and the people made alive are created for good works. A careful synthesis must account for gift, faith, and changed conduct together.

**Consider:** What kind of claim does James begin by testing? What does each author say about Abraham within his own argument?

**Reflect:** Identify one need that words of goodwill alone have not met. Consider what an active response of faith and love would require.

The readings direct attention to God's mercy, Christ's saving work, the Spirit's renewal, and faith's response. They exclude boasting without making conduct irrelevant, and give assurance without relocating life away from the Son. Keep the whole arguments in view as you study the language of salvation.
