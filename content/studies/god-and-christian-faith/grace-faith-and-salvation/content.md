# Saved by grace: how God forgives and changes us

Concepts: Gift · Verdict · New birth · Family · Growth

Motto: Dead, then alive. / Guilty, then declared right.

Lead: *Grace* is God's kindness given to people who have not earned it and could never repay it. *Salvation* is God's rescue: from the guilt of sin, from its hold on a life, and from his judgement, into his family for ever. This study walks six passages that show how God forgives and changes people. It teaches one habit for reading them: watch who is doing the verbs.

> But because of His great love for us, God, who is rich in mercy, made us alive with Christ even when we were dead in our trespasses. It is by grace you have been saved!

Ephesians 2:4–5 · BSB

## The habit: watch who is doing the verbs

Most sentences about salvation have two kinds of actor in them, God and us. Before you ask what a passage means, ask a plainer question: in each sentence, who is acting? Then ask what the other one does in reply.

Try it on the first passage. In Ephesians 2 the readers "used to walk" in sin and "lived" by their cravings (Ephesians 2:2–3). Then the subject changes. God "made us alive", "raised us up" and "seated us" (Ephesians 2:5–6). Only after that do the readers act again, and what they do are "good works, which God prepared in advance" (Ephesians 2:10).

This habit guards against two opposite mistakes. One is thinking we rescue ourselves and God only helps. The other is thinking that because God does the rescuing, the rescued person does nothing at all. Scripture's verbs rule out both.

## What this study covers

This is a selection, not a census. It follows the order of article IV (salvation) of the Baptist Faith and Message 2000, the statement of faith of the Southern Baptist Convention and the confession this site writes under. That article speaks of the new birth, repentance and faith, justification, sanctification and glorification, and each of those words is explained where it first appears. The study walks six passages: Ephesians 2:1–10, Luke 18:9–14, Titus 3:3–7, Romans 3:21–4:8, Galatians 4:4–7 and James 2:14–26. It quotes a few verses from other passages and links the studies that read them in full.

Some things are left to other studies. What Christ's death achieved belongs to *What the cross accomplishes*. Why every person needs rescue belongs to *Humanity, sin and renewal*. How God chooses and keeps his people belongs to *Chosen and kept*, and the raised body to *The hope to come*. Many readers of this site's Spanish and Portuguese pages were raised in the Roman Catholic Church. So in part 04 Catholic teaching on justification is set out in that church's own words and labelled as theirs. Other traditions are not surveyed here.

## 01 It is the gift of God

**Main readings:** Ephesians 2:1–10

Hook: Paul begins with a funeral and ends with a workshop.

Setting: Paul writes to believers in and around Ephesus, a mixed church of Jews and Gentiles (people who are not Jewish). He has just prayed that they would know "the surpassing greatness of His power" (Ephesians 1:19), and now he shows that power at work on them.

### Dead, and then alive

"And you were dead in your trespasses and sins" (Ephesians 2:1). Paul does not mean their hearts had stopped. He means they were cut off from the life of God, as unable to raise themselves toward him as a dead body is to sit up. A trespass is a step over a line God has drawn.

Then Paul includes himself, though he had been a devout Jew: "All of us also lived among them at one time", and "Like the rest, we were by nature children of wrath" (Ephesians 2:3). God's *wrath* is his settled, just opposition to evil. It is not a bad temper. Nobody in the room is let off the description, and the writer is not either.

Then the sentence turns on two words: "But ... God" (Ephesians 2:4). Watch the verbs. God "made us alive with Christ", God "raised us up with Christ and seated us with Him" (Ephesians 2:5–6). The readers are the objects of every one of these verbs. The reason given is God's "great love" and his being "rich in mercy" (Ephesians 2:4). *Mercy* is God holding back the punishment people deserve. Grace goes further and gives them what they could never deserve.

### Not from yourselves

> For it is by grace you have been saved through faith, and this not from yourselves; it is the gift of God, not by works, so that no one can boast.

Ephesians 2:8–9 · BSB

*Faith* here means trust: relying on God's promise and on Christ, rather than on yourself. Notice where Paul puts it. Salvation is "by grace", the cause, and "through faith", the way it is received. Faith is not a payment that buys the gift. It is the empty hand that takes it.

Readers have long asked what "this" refers to in "this not from yourselves": faith alone, or the whole of being saved by grace through faith. In the Greek, "this" does not match the word for faith in form, so many read it as the whole of salvation. The verse's point stands either way: no part of it starts with us, "so that no one can boast".

### Made for good works

> For we are God’s workmanship, created in Christ Jesus to do good works, which God prepared in advance as our way of life.

Ephesians 2:10 · BSB

The paragraph that began with a walk in sin (Ephesians 2:2) ends with a way of life in good works. The order matters. Good works do not come before grace as its price. They come after it as its purpose. "Not by works" (Ephesians 2:9) and "to do good works" (Ephesians 2:10) stand one verse apart, and Paul sees no conflict between them.

What the passage does not do: it does not explain how God's choosing and human trust fit together, which belongs to *Chosen and kept*. It does not make works optional. And it does not give anyone a reason to look down on people still "dead in trespasses", because the writer counts himself among them.

**Consider:** In verses 4 to 7, who is the subject of each verb? How does verse 10 finish the thought that verses 8 and 9 began?

**Reflect:** Which mistake are you closer to: treating your good works as the reason God accepts you, or treating grace as a reason not to bother with good works?

## 02 Two men went up to pray: repentance and faith

**Main readings:** Luke 18:9–14

Hook: One man thanks God for himself. The other asks God for mercy. Only one goes home forgiven.

### The call

Jesus began preaching with a call:

> “The time is fulfilled,” He said, “and the kingdom of God is near. Repent and believe in the gospel!”

Mark 1:15 · BSB

Read more in *The kingdom he announced*.

The *gospel* is the good news: God's kingdom has come near in Jesus, who died for sins and rose again, and God forgives everyone who trusts him. To *repent* is to turn. It means a change of mind that becomes a change of direction, away from sin and toward God. To *believe* is to trust. The Baptist Faith and Message (article IV) says "Repentance and faith are inseparable experiences of grace". You do not do one now and the other later. Turning from sin and trusting Christ are the same movement seen from two sides. Luke 18 shows that movement in a person.

### Two men in the temple

Luke tells us whom the story was for: "some who trusted in their own righteousness and viewed others with contempt" (Luke 18:9). *Righteousness* means being right in God's sight.

First, the setting in its own Jewish terms. The temple in Jerusalem was the centre of Israel's worship, and people went up to pray there, often at the hours of the morning and afternoon sacrifices. The Pharisees were a respected movement among the Jewish people, devoted to keeping the law of Moses carefully in daily life. The law commanded one yearly fast, on the Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur), so fasting "twice a week" (Luke 18:12) went well beyond what it required. A tithe is a tenth of one's goods given to God. A tax collector gathered taxes for the ruling powers and was widely despised for working with them and for taking more than was owed.

So this is not a story of Jews against Christians. Jesus, both men and the first hearers were all Jewish. It is a story about two kinds of prayer, and either one can be prayed in any church today.

Watch the verbs in the Pharisee's prayer: "I am not like the other men", "I fast", "I pay" (Luke 18:11–12). He is the subject of every sentence, and God is only the audience. Then the other man:

> But the tax collector stood at a distance, unwilling even to lift up his eyes to heaven. Instead, he beat his breast and said, ‘God, have mercy on me, a sinner!’

Luke 18:13 · BSB

He brings nothing, and he asks God to act. The word Luke uses for "have mercy" is related to the Greek words for atonement, the covering of sin by sacrifice. Then comes Jesus' verdict: "this man, rather than the Pharisee, went home justified" (Luke 18:14). *Justified* means declared to be in the right with God. Part 04 explains the word more fully. Here, notice only how the man received it: by asking.

What the parable does not teach: it does not say the tax collector's beating his breast earned his pardon. It does not condemn fasting or giving, which are good things. And it gives no reason to despise Pharisees. A reader who prays "God, I thank You that I am not like this Pharisee" has turned straight back into the man the story warns about.

[care] Some readers feel too guilty to pray the tax collector's prayer, because they are sure they have gone too far. Look at who went home justified: the man who called himself "a sinner" and had nothing to add. **Reflect:** When you pray, do you tell God what you have done, or ask him for what only he can do?

## 03 The washing of new birth

**Main readings:** Titus 3:3–7

Hook: Paul describes his own past in the same list he uses for the people he warns against.

### What we were

Paul left Titus on the island of Crete to put its young churches in order (Titus 1:5). Just before this passage he tells believers to be gentle and courteous to everyone (Titus 3:1–2), and then he gives the reason: "For at one time we too were foolish, disobedient, misled, and enslaved to all sorts of desires and pleasures—living in malice and envy, being hated and hating one another" (Titus 3:3). Believers have no grounds to sneer at outsiders, because they remember where they came from.

### What God did

> But when the kindness of God our Savior and His love for mankind appeared, He saved us, not by the righteous deeds we had done, but according to His mercy, through the washing of new birth and renewal by the Holy Spirit.

Titus 3:4–5 · BSB

Watch the verbs again. God's kindness "appeared", "He saved us", and he "poured out" the Spirit (Titus 3:6). The readers' "righteous deeds" are named only in order to be ruled out. One sentence holds the whole of God: God our Savior, the Holy Spirit, and "Jesus Christ our Savior" (Titus 3:6). Salvation is the work of Father, Son and Holy Spirit together.

*New birth*, or *regeneration*, means God giving a person a new life from within. Jesus spoke of it to Nicodemus (John 3). Read more in *Encounters with Jesus*. John's Gospel puts it in two verses:

> But to all who did receive Him, to those who believed in His name, He gave the right to become children of God— children born not of blood, nor of the desire or will of man, but born of God.

John 1:12–13 · BSB

Read more in *Who do you say that I am*.

The Baptist Faith and Message (article IV) calls regeneration "a work of God's grace whereby believers become new creatures in Christ Jesus", "a change of heart wrought by the Holy Spirit". Titus then draws the line through to the end: "having been justified by His grace, we would become heirs with the hope of eternal life" (Titus 3:7). New birth, a new standing and a future inheritance are held in one sentence.

### Is the washing baptism?

Readers ask whether "the washing of new birth" means baptism in water. Traditions answer differently.

[other] The Roman Catholic Church, together with the Orthodox, Lutheran and some other churches, teaches that baptism is a *sacrament*, a sign through which God gives the grace it signifies. In this verse they read baptism as the means of new birth. The Catechism of the Catholic Church says that justification "is conferred in Baptism, the sacrament of faith" (paragraph 1992). That is their teaching, and it is shown here as theirs.

[read] Baptists read the washing as the Spirit's own cleansing of the heart, which baptism then pictures. The verse's own words point that way: it names the Holy Spirit as the one who renews, and it has just ruled out anything "we had done". It also echoes the prophet's promise of clean water and a new spirit (Ezekiel 36:25–27). The Baptist Faith and Message calls baptism an *ordinance*, an act Christ commanded his church to keep, and describes it as "an act of obedience symbolizing the believer's faith" (article VII, baptism and the Lord's Supper). Baptism matters, and Christ commanded it, but it follows new birth and does not cause it. Read more in *Baptism and the Lord's Supper* [check: final title of the baptism study].

What the passage does not do: it does not say what new birth feels like, or require one kind of experience. Some believers remember the hour. Others only know that they once did not trust Christ and now they do.

**Consider:** Which verbs in verses 4 to 6 belong to God? Why does Paul remind his readers of verse 3 before telling them how they were saved?

**Reflect:** Think of someone you find hard to be gentle with. How does verse 3 change the way you see them?

## 04 Declared right: justification by faith alone

**Main readings:** Romans 3:21–4:8

Hook: A judge declares the guilty innocent, and he stays just in doing it.

*Justification* is a courtroom word. To justify someone is to declare them in the right: the judge's verdict, not a change of character. *Justification by faith alone* is the teaching that God gives this verdict to sinners because of Christ, received by trusting him and not earned by anything they do.

### But now

Romans 1:18–3:20 has brought everyone, Jew and Gentile, into the dock. Read more in *Humanity, sin and renewal*. Then Paul writes "But now" (Romans 3:21). God's righteousness, his own rightness and the right standing he gives, "has been revealed", "apart from the law", yet "attested by the Law and the Prophets" (Romans 3:21). It is not a new idea, because Israel's Scriptures had pointed to it.

> for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God, and are justified freely by His grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus.

Romans 3:23–24 · BSB

"Freely" means as a gift, without cost to the one receiving it. *Redemption* means setting a captive free by paying a price. The price was not small: God "presented Him as the atoning sacrifice through faith in His blood" (Romans 3:25). An *atoning sacrifice* is one that deals with sin and turns away God's just anger against it. Read more in *What the cross accomplishes*.

Then comes the sentence that answers the hardest question: God did this "so as to be just and to justify the one who has faith in Jesus" (Romans 3:26). God does not pretend sin does not matter. He deals with it in Christ, and so he can declare the guilty right without becoming unjust himself.

### No boasting, one God

"Where, then, is boasting? It is excluded" (Romans 3:27). "For we maintain that a man is justified by faith apart from works of the law" (Romans 3:28).

Paul's next argument rests on the confession every Jewish child learned, the Shema, which begins "Hear, O Israel: The LORD our God, the LORD is One" (Deuteronomy 6:4) and is still prayed morning and evening. Because "there is only one God", he will justify Jew and Gentile alike, "by faith" and "through that same faith" (Romans 3:30). Nor does this cancel God's law: "we uphold the law" (Romans 3:31). The law was always good. It was never the way a sinner could earn a verdict.

### Abraham and David

Paul then turns to Israel's own father, Abraham, and to its king, David. Scripture is read by Scripture here. Paul quotes Genesis 15:6, "Abraham believed God, and it was credited to him as righteousness" (Romans 4:3). To be *credited* is a bookkeeping word: something is entered to your account.

> Now the wages of the worker are not credited as a gift, but as an obligation. However, to the one who does not work, but believes in Him who justifies the wicked, his faith is credited as righteousness.

Romans 4:4–5 · BSB

Look at the last phrase: God "justifies the wicked". He does not wait until people become good and then announce that they are. He declares the guilty right, for Christ's sake, when they trust him. David's psalm says the same in other words: "Blessed is the man whose sin the Lord will never count against him" (Romans 4:8, quoting Psalm 32:2).

The words "faith alone" are not in Romans. They are the shorthand of the sixteenth-century Reformers for what Romans 3:28 and 4:5 say: justified "apart from works", by faith "to the one who does not work". The Baptist Faith and Message calls justification "God's gracious and full acquittal upon principles of His righteousness of all sinners who repent and believe in Christ" (article IV). The Baptist Catechism, approved in 1693 and printed in 1695, answers the question "What is justification?" by calling it an act of God's free grace "wherein he pardoneth all our sins, and accepteth us as righteous in his sight, only for the righteousness of Christ imputed to us, and received by faith alone" [check: the question number and exact wording in the site's creeds collection]. The verdict brings peace:

> Therefore, since we have been justified through faith, we have peace with God through our Lord Jesus Christ,

Romans 5:1 · BSB

Read more in *Faith under pressure*.

### What the Roman Catholic Church teaches, in its own words

[other] The Council of Trent, a council of the Roman Catholic Church, set out its teaching on justification in a decree of its sixth session in 1547. Chapter 7 says that justification "is not remission of sins merely, but also the sanctification and renewal of the inward man", and that its instrumental cause is "the sacrament of baptism, which is the sacrament of faith". Chapter 8 explains in what sense Catholics say we are justified by faith: "faith is the beginning of human salvation, the foundation, and the root of all Justification". It also explains in what sense we are justified freely: "none of those things which precede justification—whether faith or works—merit the grace itself of justification" [check: wording against the English translation held in the site's creeds collection, J. Waterworth, 1848].

[other] The Catechism of the Catholic Church, published in 1992 with the English edition in 1994, teaches the same in paragraphs 1987–1995. Justification "is not only the remission of sins, but also the sanctification and renewal of the interior man" (1989). It "has been merited for us by the Passion of Christ" and "is conferred in Baptism, the sacrament of faith" (1992). And it "establishes cooperation between God's grace and man's freedom" (1993) [check: wording of paragraphs 1989, 1992 and 1993 against the Vatican's English text].

Many readers learned this teaching as children. It deserves to be heard fairly. It does not say that people earn their first acceptance with God. It says grace comes first and that Christ's suffering merits it. It also calls people to a changed life, as this study does.

[read] Where Baptists differ is in what the word *justify* means and how the verdict is received. Baptists hold, from these verses, that justification is God's declaration, not a process of inner renewal. The verbs in Romans 4 are declaring verbs: credited, forgiven, covered, not counted (Romans 4:3–8). And the one God justifies is "the wicked" (Romans 4:5), not someone already made righteous inside. Inner renewal is just as real, but Scripture gives it other names: new birth (part 03) and growing in holiness (part 07). It always comes with justification and never becomes its ground. The verdict rests on Christ alone and is received by faith alone, before baptism and apart from it, as the tax collector received it in the temple (Luke 18:14). That is why Baptists can say a believer has "peace with God" now (Romans 5:1) and need not wait to see whether they have done enough.

A piece of history: in 1999, at Augsburg, the Lutheran World Federation and the Roman Catholic Church signed the *Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification*. It stated a shared understanding of basic truths about justification and left some differences standing. It was an agreement between those two bodies. Baptists did not take part in it, and it does not speak for them.

**Consider:** In Romans 4:4–8, what words does Paul use for how God treats the believer? Whom does Romans 4:5 say God justifies?

**Reflect:** If someone asked you tonight why God should accept you, what would your first sentence be, and whose verbs would be in it?

## 05 No longer a slave, but a son: adoption into God's family

**Main readings:** Galatians 4:4–7

Hook: A judge can acquit you. Only a father can bring you home.

*Adoption* is God taking those he has justified into his own family as his children and heirs. Justification answers the question "Am I guilty?" Adoption answers the question "Do I belong?"

### God sent his Son, then his Spirit

Paul writes to churches in Galatia where Gentile believers were being told they must also be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to belong fully to God's people. His answer is a short history, and God is the subject of the verbs:

> But when the time had fully come, God sent His Son, born of a woman, born under the law, to redeem those under the law, that we might receive our adoption as sons.

Galatians 4:4–5 · BSB

"Born under the law": Jesus was a Jew. He was circumcised, kept the festivals and lived inside the covenant God made with Israel at Sinai. A *covenant* is a binding promise that sets up a relationship. He came under the law to "redeem", to buy free, those under it.

Then the second sending: "God sent the Spirit of His Son into our hearts, crying out, “Abba, Father!”" (Galatians 4:6). *Abba* is the Aramaic word for father, used in Jewish homes, and the word Jesus himself used when he prayed in Gethsemane (Mark 14:36). The Spirit gives believers the same word for God that the Son used. Read more in *The Holy Spirit*.

### Sons and heirs

> So you are no longer a slave, but a son; and since you are a son, you are also an heir through God.

Galatians 4:7 · BSB

In that world it was sons who inherited. Paul gives every believer, man or woman, the heir's place, for "there is neither male nor female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28). The Baptist Faith and Message says justification "brings the believer unto a relationship of peace and favor with God" (article IV). The Second London Baptist Confession of 1677 gives adoption a chapter of its own (chapter 12).

[care] For some readers the word "father" brings pain. That might be a father who left, who hurt them, or whom they never knew. Here the Father is known by what he does: he sends his Son to free you and his Spirit to bring you home. Your own father is not the measure of him. If someone in your home is harming you now, getting to safety and telling someone you trust is right, and nothing in this passage asks you to stay.

What the passage does not do: it does not make God everyone's Father in this sense whether or not they trust Christ. "To all who did receive Him" (John 1:12) is the door. It also does not promise that belonging to God's family will make earthly life easy.

**Consider:** What two things does God send in verses 4 to 6, and what does each do? What is the difference between a slave and an heir in verse 7?

**Reflect:** Do you come to God more like a servant hoping to be paid, or a child expecting to be heard?

## 06 Faith that is alive: James and Paul

**Main readings:** James 2:14–26

Hook: A hungry neighbour hears a blessing and gets no bread.

### A faith that only talks

James writes to believers scattered abroad (James 1:1), and he begins with a question: "What good is it, my brothers, if someone claims to have faith, but has no deeds? Can such faith save him?" (James 2:14). Note the word "claims". James is not testing faith. He is testing a claim to have it.

His example is a person, not a theory. A brother or sister without clothes or food is told "Go in peace; stay warm and well fed", and nothing is given (James 2:15–16). "So too, faith by itself, if it does not result in action, is dead" (James 2:17). Then a harder example still: "You believe that God is one. Good for you! Even the demons believe that—and shudder" (James 2:19). Agreeing that something is true is not the trust that saves. The demons agree, and they are not saved.

### Abraham, Rahab, and "not by faith alone"

> Was not our father Abraham justified by what he did when he offered his son Isaac on the altar? You see that his faith was working with his actions, and his faith was perfected by what he did.

James 2:21–22 · BSB

Then comes the sentence that has troubled many readers: "As you can see, a man is justified by his deeds and not by faith alone" (James 2:24). Rahab follows, the Canaanite woman of Jericho who hid Israel's spies (Joshua 2). She is "justified by her actions when she welcomed the spies" (James 2:25). The closing picture: "As the body without the spirit is dead, so faith without deeds is dead" (James 2:26).

### Does James contradict Paul?

This is a real question and deserves a careful answer. Both writers quote the same verse about Abraham, and they seem to reach opposite conclusions. The confession this site writes under holds that Scripture is "truth, without any mixture of error" (article I, the Scriptures), so this site reads the two together. Three things in the text show how.

First, they ask different questions. Paul asks how a guilty person comes to stand right before God, and answers: not by wages (Romans 4:4–5). James asks whether a faith that produces nothing is real, and answers: it is dead (James 2:17).

Second, James uses "faith" here for the faith someone "claims" (James 2:14), the kind that demons share (James 2:19). Paul uses it for trust in "Him who justifies the wicked" (Romans 4:5).

Third, look at when the events happened. Paul quotes Genesis 15:6, where Abraham believed God's promise and was counted righteous. James points to Genesis 22, the offering of Isaac, many years later, and says that by that act "the Scripture was fulfilled" (James 2:23). The deed did not earn the earlier verdict. It showed the verdict was true. In James, "justified" means shown to be in the right. In Romans 4, it means declared to be in the right.

[source:calvin-antidote] John Calvin, answering the Council of Trent in 1547, put it in a line that has been repeated ever since: it is faith alone that justifies, and yet the faith that justifies is not alone [check: exact wording and location in Calvin's *Acts of the Council of Trent with the Antidote*, on the sixth session].

[other] The Council of Trent itself, in chapter 10 of its sixth session, cites James 2:24 to teach that the justified grow in justification through good works [check: chapter and wording]. That is Catholic teaching, shown here as theirs. Baptists read James 2:24, for the reasons above, as speaking of faith shown to be real, and not of a verdict increased or earned.

What the passage does not do: it does not say deeds earn salvation. It does not let anyone judge another person's standing from one failure. And it does not tell a struggling believer that their weakness proves they were never saved. James is aiming at people who are comfortable doing nothing, not at people who are trying and falling.

**Consider:** What does someone "claim" in verse 14? Which event in Abraham's life does Paul point to, and which does James point to?

**Reflect:** Name one need near you that you have answered only with kind words. What would a living faith do about it this week?

## 07 Growing holy, made perfect: sanctification and glorification

**Main readings:** Ephesians 2:10 and Titus 3:3–7 again, with Philippians 2:12–13, Romans 6:1–2 and Romans 8:29–30 quoted

Hook: The verdict is given once. The growing takes a lifetime.

### Growing holy

*Sanctification* means being made holy: set apart for God, and changed little by little to be like Christ. The Baptist Faith and Message calls it "the experience, beginning in regeneration", by which the believer is "enabled to progress toward moral and spiritual maturity through the presence and power of the Holy Spirit dwelling in him" (article IV).

Here the habit of watching the verbs matters most, because there are two verbs in one breath:

> Therefore, my beloved, just as you have always obeyed, not only in my presence, but now even more in my absence, continue to work out your salvation with fear and trembling. For it is God who works in you to will and to act on behalf of His good purpose.

Philippians 2:12–13 · BSB

Read more in *A new creation*.

The believer works, really and with effort. God works in the believer, underneath that effort and before it. Paul does not say "work for your salvation", as if it had to be earned. He says "work out", as you live out something already given.

### "Can I live as I like?"

If grace is free, why not keep sinning? Paul heard that question too:

> What then shall we say? Shall we continue in sin so that grace may increase? Certainly not! How can we who died to sin live in it any longer?

Romans 6:1–2 · BSB

Read more in *A new creation*.

The grace that forgives is the same grace that raises a dead person to a new life (Ephesians 2:5). Someone who wants the forgiveness and refuses the new life has not understood what was offered. This site's apologetics answer [If salvation is a gift, why do good works matter?](/apologetics/study/grace) takes up the wider objection that free grace makes morality pointless.

### "Can I lose it?"

The confession this site writes under says plainly that those God has accepted in Christ "will never fall away from the state of grace" (article V, God's purpose of grace). It also says believers may fall into sin, grieve the Spirit and bring hurt on themselves. Both are true. Other Christians, in the Wesleyan and Arminian traditions, teach that a true believer can finally fall away. That view is theirs, and it is not this site's. Read more in *Chosen and kept*.

[care] If you are anxious every day about whether your faith is real enough, go back to where Scripture points you. Look away from the strength of your feelings and toward "Him who justifies the wicked" (Romans 4:5). Constant fear of this kind can sometimes be part of an anxiety illness. A doctor or counsellor, alongside a pastor, can help, and seeking that help is no failure of faith.

### Made perfect at the end

*Glorification* is the end of salvation: believers made completely free from sin and raised to share Christ's glory. The confession calls it "the culmination of salvation" and "the final blessed and abiding state of the redeemed" (article IV). Paul draws the whole line in one sentence, and God is the subject of every verb:

> For those God foreknew, He also predestined to be conformed to the image of His Son, so that He would be the firstborn among many brothers. And those He predestined, He also called; those He called, He also justified; those He justified, He also glorified.

Romans 8:29–30 · BSB

Read more in *Chosen and kept*.

Baptists who hold the Calvinist reading and Baptists who do not both affirm article V. They differ over what "foreknew" and "predestined" mean in detail, and this study does not choose between them. All of them read the sentence as an unbroken chain: no one God justifies drops out before "glorified". What that glory means for the body, raised and made new when Christ returns, is the subject of *The hope to come*.

**Consider:** In Philippians 2:12–13, what does the believer do, and what does God do? In Romans 8:29–30, how many verbs belong to God?

**Reflect:** Where in your life right now do you need to "work out" what God has already worked in?

## Close: watch who is doing the verbs

Way: In every passage on salvation, ask first who is acting. You have seen God make the dead alive (Ephesians 2:5), a tax collector go home justified because he asked (Luke 18:13–14), God save "according to His mercy" (Titus 3:5), God justify the wicked (Romans 4:5), God send his Son and his Spirit (Galatians 4:4–6), and God glorify those he justified (Romans 8:30). Then ask what the person does in reply: turns, trusts, cries "Abba, Father!", feeds the hungry neighbour, works out what God works in. God's verbs come first, and ours follow.

Invite: If you have never trusted Christ, you do not have to make yourself better first. The tax collector did not. The good news is that "the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Romans 6:23). Turn from your sin and trust Christ as Lord, because, in the confession's words, "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord". You can say it to God in your own words, as the tax collector did. Then tell a Christian you trust, find a church that preaches this gospel, and ask to be baptized. Read more in *The good news*.

Question: When you think about where you stand with God, whose verbs come first: yours, or his?
