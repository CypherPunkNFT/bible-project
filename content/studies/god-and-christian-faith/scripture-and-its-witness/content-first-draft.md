# Scripture and its witness

**Study 27 · God and Christian Faith · First content draft · 2026-10-13**

The biblical writers describe Scripture as given by God, profitable for teaching and correction, and a source of patience, comfort, and hope. Jesus opens the Scriptures to explain his suffering and resurrection. The apostles return to earlier writings as they proclaim Christ and teach communities how to live.

Those claims invite attentive reading. Who is speaking, what does the passage address, and how does its argument unfold? Questions about which books belong to Scripture, how their wording has been transmitted, and how they are translated are related but distinct. A statement about inspiration is not, by itself, a list of books or a history of their manuscripts.

## 01 Given by inspiration of God

**Main readings:** 2 Timothy 3:10–17; 2 Peter 1:16–21.

### The holy scriptures

Second Timothy urges Timothy to continue in what he has learned, remembering those from whom he learned it. The appeal comes amid warnings about deception and recollections of teaching, conduct, persecution, and endurance. Scripture belongs within that call to remain faithful.

Timothy has known the holy Scriptures from childhood. They are described as able to make him wise unto salvation through faith in Christ Jesus. The passage then states:

> All scripture is given by inspiration of God, and is profitable for doctrine, for reproof, for correction, for instruction in righteousness:

2 Timothy 3:16 · KJV

The sentence joins origin and usefulness. It does not stop at identifying Scripture as inspired; it goes on to name what Scripture does in forming the reader. The surrounding reference to salvation through faith in Christ also gives direction to the wisdom being described.

Timothy's childhood acquaintance with sacred writings should not be imagined as possession of a completed modern New Testament. The passage speaks of writings he already knew and their relation to Christ, without supplying a table of contents for every writing later received by the churches.

### Moved by the Holy Ghost

Second Peter places the prophetic word alongside testimony to Christ's majesty. The writer recalls the voice heard on the holy mountain and urges readers to attend to prophecy as to a light in a dark place.

The concluding explanation says that prophecy did not come by human will, but that holy men spoke as they were moved by the Holy Ghost. Human speakers are named, while the source of their speaking is attributed to God's Spirit. The passage does not describe the process as merely the speakers inventing religious ideas.

Its statement about private interpretation must be read with the following explanation about prophecy's origin. It should not become a detached slogan settling every question about individual reading or church authority. The paragraph directs attention to the prophetic word and the Spirit who moved its speakers.

**Consider:** How do origin and purpose belong together in 2 Timothy? What does 2 Peter contrast with the Spirit's work?

**Reflect:** Consider whether you approach Scripture mainly to collect information or also to receive the teaching and correction it describes.

## 02 Throughly furnished unto all good works

**Main reading:** 2 Timothy 3:14–4:5.

### Doctrine and correction

The uses listed in 2 Timothy are practical as well as instructional. Doctrine teaches; reproof confronts what is wrong; correction addresses the need to be set right; instruction in righteousness concerns the life that should follow. The sentence gives its intended result:

> That the man of God may be perfect, throughly furnished unto all good works.

2 Timothy 3:17 · KJV

The emphasis is on being equipped for good work. Scripture is not presented as a collection of impressive sentences to display without being changed by them. The reader's understanding, judgments, and conduct all come within its stated usefulness.

Correction also requires a willingness to be corrected. A person who uses Scripture only against other people may avoid the very reproof the passage says it provides. The immediate address is to Timothy, whose own endurance and ministry are under consideration.

### With all longsuffering and doctrine

The next chapter charges Timothy to preach the word, reprove, rebuke, and exhort with longsuffering and teaching. The task is serious, but its manner includes patience. Authority does not make impatience or careless accusation part of the commission.

The warning about hearers seeking teachers to suit their desires explains why faithfulness can be difficult. Timothy is called to remain watchful, endure afflictions, and fulfil his ministry. The answer to resistance is sustained, truthful service.

These verses connect Scripture's usefulness with the work of communicating it. Teaching should make the passage understandable; correction should address what it actually corrects; exhortation should help the hearer respond. Quoting a verse without establishing its relevance does not automatically fulfil those purposes.

**Consider:** What result does verse 17 name? How does the charge in chapter 4 shape both the content and manner of teaching?

**Reflect:** Identify something a passage has corrected in your own thinking. Consider how to explain that correction patiently rather than using it to establish superiority.

## 03 Search the scriptures

**Main readings:** Acts 17:10–12; Matthew 4:1–11.

### Whether those things were so

Acts describes Paul and Silas entering the synagogue at Berea. The hearers receive the message with readiness and examine it:

> These were more noble than those in Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so.

Acts 17:11 · KJV

Receptiveness and examination appear together. The hearers are neither praised for dismissing the message before listening nor described as accepting it without inquiry. Their searching concerns whether the things proclaimed agree with the Scriptures they read.

The account leads to many believing. It portrays examination as part of their response to the message. For a reader, this invites a concrete practice: read the cited passage, follow its surrounding argument, and ask how it supports the claim being made.

### It is written again

Matthew's temptation account shows why merely including a quotation is not enough. Jesus answers temptation with Scripture, but the devil also quotes scriptural words in urging him to throw himself down from the temple. Jesus responds with another passage forbidding putting God to the test.

The dispute concerns what a passage warrants. A promise of divine care is being used to demand an act of presumption. Jesus' answer refuses that use of the promise. Recognising words as scriptural does not require accepting every action someone attaches to them.

Before applying a text, identify its speaker, hearers, subject, and place in the paragraph or story. Ask whether it is a command, promise, prayer, report, or someone else's disputed claim. Then examine the proposed application. These questions serve the reading rather than replacing trust with suspicion.

**Consider:** What two qualities does Acts commend in the Bereans? Why does the quotation in Matthew 4:6 not justify the action proposed?

**Reflect:** Revisit a verse you often use. Read the surrounding paragraph and check whether your usual application preserves its purpose.

## 04 The things concerning himself

**Main reading:** Luke 24:25–49.

### Beginning at Moses and all the prophets

On the road, the risen Jesus challenges the disciples' slowness to believe what the prophets have spoken. He connects the Messiah's suffering with his entering glory, then explains the Scriptures:

> And beginning at Moses and all the prophets, he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself.

Luke 24:27 · KJV

The disciples later remember their hearts burning as he opened the Scriptures. Their understanding changes through his explanation of the events they had struggled to comprehend. The cross and resurrection are placed within the scriptural witness rather than treated as events disconnected from it.

Luke does not provide the full contents of that conversation. The verse establishes the scope and subject of Jesus' exposition, but it does not identify every passage he used. A proposed list of those passages remains a reconstruction unless another text explicitly supplies the connection.

### That they might understand

Later in the chapter, Jesus names the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms as writings concerning him. He opens the disciples' understanding and speaks of suffering, resurrection, and the proclamation of repentance and remission of sins among all nations.

Understanding therefore leads toward witness. The disciples are named as witnesses and told to wait for the promised power. The scriptural explanation belongs to their preparation to proclaim what has happened in Jesus.

Reading Scripture in relation to Christ need not erase the earlier passages' own settings. Luke's account invites readers to follow the connections Jesus and the apostles establish. It does not require inventing a hidden reference to Jesus in every object or incidental detail of every story.

**Consider:** What events does Jesus connect with the Scriptures? What task follows the opening of the disciples' understanding?

**Reflect:** Consider how a passage's connection to Christ can deepen your reading while preserving the people and circumstances it first describes.

## 05 Written for our learning

**Main reading:** Romans 15:1–13. **Compare:** Psalm 69:7–12.

### Patience and comfort

Romans calls the strong to bear with the weak and seek their neighbour's good. Paul brings Christ's refusal to please himself into the argument and cites the words about reproaches falling on the speaker. Those words appear in Psalm 69, within the sufferer's account of shame and hostility.

Paul then explains the continuing usefulness of earlier writings:

> For whatsoever things were written aforetime were written for our learning, that we through patience and comfort of the scriptures might have hope.

Romans 15:4 · KJV

The statement appears within an appeal about life together. Learning from Scripture includes patience, comfort, and hope that shape relationships. The passage moves toward a prayer for shared-mindedness and the united glorifying of God.

### Receive ye one another

Paul urges the readers to receive one another as Christ received them. He then connects Christ with confirming the promises to the fathers and Gentiles glorifying God for mercy. A series of earlier scriptural quotations supports the inclusion of Gentiles in praise and hope.

The quotations form an argument. They are not ornaments added after the exhortation. Paul uses them to show why a community containing different people should receive one another and glorify God together.

When following this use of Scripture, read in both directions: examine the earlier passage, then the later writer's use of it. Ask what is quoted and what work the quotation does in the new argument. This helps distinguish an explicit apostolic connection from a resemblance a modern reader happens to notice.

**Consider:** What relationship problem surrounds verse 4? How do the later quotations support Paul's call to receive one another?

**Reflect:** Consider whether your reading is producing the patience toward others that Paul connects with Scripture's instruction.

## 06 The other scriptures

**Main readings:** 2 Peter 3:14–18; Colossians 4:16; 1 Thessalonians 5:27.

### Read among you

Colossians directs the recipients to have its letter read in Laodicea and to read the letter from Laodicea. First Thessalonians charges that its letter be read to all the brethren. These instructions show letters intended for communal hearing and, in Colossians, circulation between churches.

Second Peter speaks of Paul's letters and acknowledges that some things in them are hard to understand. It also warns of distortion:

> As also in all his epistles, speaking in them of these things; in which are some things hard to be understood, which they that are unlearned and unstable wrest, as they do also the other scriptures, unto their own destruction.

2 Peter 3:16 · KJV

The comparison places Paul's writings alongside other Scriptures while warning that readers can misuse them. Difficulty is acknowledged rather than denied. The response urged is watchfulness and growth in grace and knowledge.

These passages give evidence of reading, circulation, and recognition within the New Testament itself. They do not name a complete collection of Paul's letters or provide a complete canon list.

### Which writings belong

Canon concerns the writings received as Scripture. Christian traditions differ over that collection. The Westminster Confession lists the books of its Old and New Testaments and excludes the books it calls Apocrypha from the canon. See [chapter 1.2–3](https://opc.org/wcf.html). The Catholic Catechism lists 46 Old Testament and 27 New Testament books, including Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and 1 and 2 Maccabees, and attributes the Church's discernment of the canon to apostolic Tradition. See [paragraph 120](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_one/chapter_two/article_3/iv_the_canon_of_scripture.html).

These statements identify two received lists and their claims; they are not a complete history of how the lists developed. Inspiration concerns the writings' relation to God. Canon concerns which writings belong. Manuscript study examines their transmitted wording; translation concerns expressing that wording in another language. A question in one area needs evidence suited to it.

The readings also bring the inquiry back to its purpose. Scripture is to be understood, faithfully taught, and lived. Attention to its authority should deepen care with its meaning and readiness to receive its correction.

**Consider:** What do the letter-reading instructions and 2 Peter establish? What further evidence would be needed to explain the development of a complete canon?

**Reflect:** Name the question you want to investigate next: meaning, inspiration, canon, transmitted wording, or translation. Consider which sources would actually answer it.
