# What the Bible says about itself

Concepts: Breathed out · True · Enough · Clear · About Christ · Opened by the Spirit

Motto: A lamp. A dark place. / Read by its own light.

Lead: The Bible makes claims about itself. It says it comes from God, that it is true, that it is enough, and that it can be understood. It says it is about Jesus, and that God's Spirit helps people read it. This study walks the passages where those claims are made, faces the fair objection that this is "quoting the book to prove the book", and teaches one habit: when a verse is quoted, read the paragraph around it and ask what the verse is being used to do.

> All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for instruction, for conviction, for correction, and for training in righteousness, so that the man of God may be complete, fully equipped for every good work.

2 Timothy 3:16–17 · BSB

Frame: In one afternoon in the wilderness, two speakers used the same words: "It is written." One was Jesus. The other was the devil (Matthew 4:6–7). So knowing that a sentence comes from the Bible does not settle what it means or what it allows. **Scripture** is the Bible's own word for its writings. It simply means "what is written". In this study you will watch the people inside the Bible handle Scripture: Paul writing to a younger friend, Peter remembering a mountain, Jesus answering a tempter, and two grieving travellers on a road. Each time, ask the same question. What is this verse being used to do? This site writes in agreement with the Baptist Faith and Message 2000, the statement of faith of the Southern Baptist Convention. Its article I (the Scriptures) says the Bible "has God for its author, salvation for its end, and truth, without any mixture of error, for its matter". The parts below show where that sentence comes from in Scripture. If it sounds like going in a circle to prove the Bible from the Bible, that is a fair objection. It has its own answer near the end.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. Four passages are walked here, because each is a place where Scripture speaks about Scripture at length: 2 Timothy 3:14–17, 2 Peter 1:16–21, Matthew 4:1–11, and Luke 24:13–35 with 24:44–49. A few other verses are quoted and linked to the studies that walk them. Three related questions are not answered here, only pointed to: which books belong in the Bible (the canon), how the words were copied over the centuries (the manuscripts), and how they are put into other languages. A claim that Scripture comes from God is not, by itself, a list of books or a history of copies. Each of those questions needs its own kind of evidence. For how to read a single passage well, see *Reading the Bible well: where to start and how to keep going*.

## 01 Breathed out by God: what "inspired" means

**Main readings:** 2 Timothy 3:14–17

Hook: An old man in prison writes to a younger friend about the books his grandmother read him.

### What Timothy already had

Second Timothy is a letter from the apostle Paul to Timothy, his younger co-worker. An **apostle** is one sent out by Jesus with his authority to teach. Paul writes near the end of his life, in chains, and the chapter is full of warnings about people who will lead others astray. Against that, Paul tells Timothy to stay put: "continue in the things you have learned and firmly believed, since you know from whom you have learned them" (2 Timothy 3:14). Earlier in the letter Paul names those teachers as Timothy's grandmother Lois and his mother Eunice (2 Timothy 1:5).

Then Paul says what Timothy learned from: "From infancy you have known the Holy Scriptures" (2 Timothy 3:15). For a boy in a Jewish household of that time, those were the Hebrew Scriptures, what Christians call the Old Testament. Paul says these writings "are able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus" (2 Timothy 3:15). **Salvation** means rescue: being brought back to God, forgiven, and saved from the judgment that sin deserves. So the old writings are not only history to Paul. They lead a reader to trust Christ.

### God-breathed

> All Scripture is God-breathed and is useful for instruction, for conviction, for correction, and for training in righteousness,

2 Timothy 3:16 · BSB

**Inspiration** is the church's word for what this verse describes. In everyday English, "inspired" means a gifted person had a good idea. Here it means more. The Greek word the Berean translates "God-breathed" pictures Scripture as breathed out by God, the way a speaker's words come out on the breath. B. B. Warfield, the Princeton theologian, argued at length that the word means "breathed out by God", and not that God breathed life into a human book after it was written.

[source:calvin-2tim] John Calvin, commenting on this verse, calls this "a principle which distinguishes our religion from all others, that we know that God hath spoken to us" [check: exact wording in the Calvin Translation Society edition of the commentary on 2 Timothy].

Paul does not stop at where Scripture comes from. He goes straight on to what it does: instruction (teaching what is true), conviction (showing what is wrong), correction (setting it right), and training in **righteousness**, which means right living before God. The goal follows: "so that the man of God may be complete, fully equipped for every good work" (2 Timothy 3:17). The words were breathed out so that they would change the person who reads them.

### What the verse does not say

The verse does not explain how God breathed out Scripture. Elsewhere, as Part 02 shows, the human writers are fully present, with their own words and styles. It also gives no list of books. Timothy's childhood Scriptures were the Hebrew writings. The New Testament had not yet been gathered. Part 04 shows that the apostles already counted some Christian writings as Scripture, but this verse is not a table of contents. "The man of God" first means Timothy, a minister of the church. Applying the verse to every believer is fair, because the uses it names are for everyone, but that is this site's application.

[care] Correction is one of Scripture's four uses, and it is aimed first at the reader. A person who only ever uses the Bible to correct other people has skipped the part where it corrects them.

**Consider:** What did Timothy learn, from whom, and what does Paul say those writings could do? What four uses does verse 16 name, and what result does verse 17 give?

**Reflect:** Do you come to the Bible mainly to collect information, or also to be taught, shown your wrong, set right and trained? Which of the four do you most avoid?

## 02 Not clever stories: the Bible is true

**Main readings:** 2 Peter 1:16–21. **Quoted:** John 10:35; Matthew 5:17–18; Psalm 19:7–11

Hook: Peter was there. But he points his readers past his own memory.

### We were eyewitnesses

Peter writes to churches that are being told the Christian message is a myth. He answers first with what he saw:

> For we did not follow cleverly devised fables when we made known to you the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ, but we were eyewitnesses of His majesty.

2 Peter 1:16 · BSB

He means the day on a mountain when Jesus' appearance was changed before Peter, James and John, an event Christians call the Transfiguration (Matthew 17:1–8 tells it). Peter remembers the sound: "This is My beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased" (2 Peter 1:17). "And we ourselves heard this voice from heaven when we were with Him on the holy mountain" (2 Peter 1:18). The Christian claim, from its first writers, is a claim about things that happened in public, in history.

### A lamp in a dark place

Then Peter turns to the older Scriptures: "We also have the word of the prophets as confirmed beyond doubt" (2 Peter 1:19). A **prophet** was someone who spoke a message from God. Readers take Peter's sentence in two ways. Some hear him saying that what he saw on the mountain confirmed what the prophets had said. Others hear him saying that the prophets' written word is even firmer ground than his own experience. [check: name a commentator for each reading, for example Thomas Schreiner, *1, 2 Peter, Jude* (2003), and Richard Bauckham, *Jude, 2 Peter* (1983), and confirm which reading each holds.] Either way, Peter tells his readers to "pay attention to it, as to a lamp shining in a dark place, until the day dawns and the morning star rises in your hearts" (2 Peter 1:19).

> Above all, you must understand that no prophecy of Scripture comes from one’s own interpretation. For no such prophecy was ever brought forth by the will of man, but men spoke from God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit.

2 Peter 1:20–21 · BSB

Verse 20 is also read two ways. The best-supported reading takes it as being about where prophecy came from. The prophets did not make up their own explanation of what they saw. The reason is the word "For" that opens verse 21: the next sentence explains verse 20, and it is about the origin of prophecy, not about its readers. Others read verse 20 as being about how readers interpret Scripture, and take it to mean that the Bible is read within the church and not by each person alone. Part 04 returns to that question.

Verse 21 holds two things together. "Men spoke": real people with their own voices. "From God as they were carried along by the Holy Spirit": the **Holy Spirit** is God himself, the third person of the one God who is Father, Son and Spirit. The word for "carried along" is used in Acts 27 of a ship driven by the wind [check: that the same Greek verb appears in Acts 27:15 and 27:17]. The sailors were really sailing, and the wind decided where the ship went.

### Scripture cannot be broken

If God is the one who breathed Scripture out, and God does not lie (Titus 1:2 says so), then what he breathed out is true. Jesus spoke this way. Arguing with opponents, he rested his whole case on one word from a psalm, and added:

> If he called them gods to whom the word of God came—and the Scripture cannot be broken—

John 10:35 · BSB

In the Sermon on the Mount he went down to the smallest marks of the written page:

> Do not think that I have come to abolish the Law or the Prophets. I have not come to abolish them, but to fulfill them. For I tell you truly, until heaven and earth pass away, not a single jot, not a stroke of a pen, will disappear from the Law until everything is accomplished.

Matthew 5:17–18 · BSB

Read more in *Following Jesus and the Sermon on the Mount*.

Long before, a psalm had sung the same confidence:

> The Law of the LORD is perfect, reviving the soul; the testimony of the LORD is trustworthy, making wise the simple. The precepts of the LORD are right, bringing joy to the heart; the commandments of the LORD are radiant, giving light to the eyes. The fear of the LORD is pure, enduring forever; the judgments of the LORD are true, being altogether righteous.

Psalm 19:7–9 · BSB

Read more in *The book of Psalms*.

Notice that the psalm does not treat truth as cold. The same words are "more precious than gold" and "sweeter than honey" (Psalm 19:10).

### What truthfulness means, and what it does not

The Baptist Faith and Message says Scripture is "truth, without any mixture of error", and "totally true and trustworthy". The word Christians often use for this is **inerrancy**: the Bible does not err in anything it affirms. The Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978), signed by evangelical scholars and pastors from many churches, is the best-known modern statement of it. This site holds that view.

Truthfulness does not mean every sentence is the same kind of writing. A psalm is poetry. A parable is a story told to make a point. A writer may give a round number or describe the sun "rising" as everyone sees it. A true book uses all the ordinary ways people speak truly. It does not mean every passage is easy either. Some passages seem to disagree, and careful readers have worked on them for centuries. Read more in the site's apologetics answer [What should we do with apparent Bible contradictions?](/apologetics/study/contradictions).

[other] Some Christians and churches hold that the Bible is fully reliable in what it teaches about faith and how to live, but not necessarily in every historical or scientific detail. No document stating that view has been quoted for this page, and it is not the view of the confession this site writes under.

**Consider:** What does Peter set against "cleverly devised fables"? In 2 Peter 1:21, who speaks, and what carries them?

**Reflect:** Is there a part of the Bible you quietly treat as less true than the rest? What would change if you read it as true, and read it carefully for what kind of writing it is?

## 03 Every word from the mouth of God: Scripture is enough

**Main readings:** Matthew 4:1–11. **Quoted:** Deuteronomy 8:3

Hook: Forty days without food, and Jesus answers hunger with a sentence.

### Hungry in the wilderness

> Then Jesus was led by the Spirit into the wilderness to be tempted by the devil. After fasting forty days and forty nights, He was hungry.

Matthew 4:1–2 · BSB

To be **tempted** is to be pulled toward doing wrong. The tempter starts with the most reasonable request imaginable: "If You are the Son of God, tell these stones to become bread" (Matthew 4:3). Jesus is starving. He has the power. Why not?

> But Jesus answered, “It is written: ‘Man shall not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of God.’”

Matthew 4:4 · BSB

Jesus is quoting Deuteronomy, which in Jewish tradition is the last of the five books of the Torah. The **Torah**, often translated "the Law", means God's teaching. Deuteronomy is Moses' farewell to Israel at the edge of the promised land, and the line Jesus quotes looks back on forty years in the desert:

> He humbled you, and in your hunger He gave you manna to eat, which neither you nor your fathers had known, so that you might understand that man does not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of the LORD.

Deuteronomy 8:3 · BSB

Israel was hungry in the wilderness for forty years, and Jesus for forty days. Israel had to learn that life comes from God's word. Jesus lives it. All three of his answers come from Deuteronomy (6:16 and 6:13 are the other two).

### Worship and serve him only

The last offer is the largest: "all the kingdoms of the world and their glory", for one act of worship (Matthew 4:8–9). Jesus answers:

> “Away from Me, Satan!” Jesus declared. “For it is written: ‘Worship the Lord your God and serve Him only.’” Then the devil left Him, and angels came and ministered to Him.

Matthew 4:10–11 · BSB

Notice what Jesus does not do. He does not argue with the devil, perform a sign, or reach for a new revelation. In each test, the words God had already given are enough.

### What "enough" means

The church calls this the **sufficiency** of Scripture: the Bible gives everything we need to know God, to be saved, and to live in a way that pleases him. Paul said the same in other words, "fully equipped for every good work" (2 Timothy 3:17). The Second London Baptist Confession (1677, reissued 1689), an older Baptist confession, opens by calling Scripture "the only sufficient, certain, and infallible rule of all saving Knowledge, Faith, and Obedience" [check: spelling and punctuation against the 1677 text in the site's creeds collection]. The Baptist Faith and Message calls it "the supreme standard by which all human conduct, creeds, and religious opinions should be tried".

[other] The Roman Catholic Church teaches otherwise. The Catechism of the Catholic Church holds that the Church "does not derive her certainty about all revealed truths from the holy Scriptures alone", and that "both Scripture and Tradition must be accepted and honored with equal sentiments of devotion and reverence" [check: wording and paragraph number, believed to be 82]. Baptists answer that Jesus himself measured the traditions of his day by Scripture, and that 2 Timothy 3:17 calls Scripture enough to equip a person "for every good work".

Sufficiency has limits, and they are worth naming. The Bible is enough for what it was given for. It is not a textbook of medicine or engineering, and it does not forbid learning from doctors, counsellors or scientists. The Baptist Faith and Message counts all sound learning as part of the Christian heritage (article XII, education). And Jesus' answers are not magic words. He quoted Scripture because he believed it and obeyed it, and the devil, as the next part shows, could quote it too.

**Consider:** Which book does Jesus quote each time, and what experience of Israel's does the first quotation recall? What does Jesus refuse to do in each test?

**Reflect:** When you are pulled toward something wrong, what do you reach for first? Is there one sentence of Scripture you know well enough to stand on?

## 04 It is also written: clear, and easily twisted

**Main readings:** Matthew 4:5–7. **Quoted:** Psalm 119:105; Acts 17:11; 2 Peter 3:15–16

Hook: The devil can quote the Bible word for word. Jesus answers with more of it.

### A true verse, a false use

> Then the devil took Him to the holy city and set Him on the pinnacle of the temple. “If You are the Son of God,” he said, “throw Yourself down. For it is written: ‘He will command His angels concerning You, and they will lift You up in their hands, so that You will not strike Your foot against a stone.’”

Matthew 4:5–6 · BSB

The devil is quoting Psalm 91, and quoting it accurately. The psalm really does promise God's care. What is false is the use: a promise of protection turned into a demand that God put on a show. Jesus does not deny the psalm. He sets beside it what Scripture says elsewhere:

> Jesus replied, “It is also written: ‘Do not put the Lord your God to the test.’”

Matthew 4:7 · BSB

This is the habit this study teaches, done by Jesus himself. A verse is read with its paragraph and with the rest of Scripture. Recognising the words as biblical is not the same as accepting what someone wants them to do.

### A lamp to my feet

The church calls this the **clarity** of Scripture: what we need to know to be saved and to follow God can be understood by ordinary readers, without a special key. A psalm puts it in one line:

> Your word is a lamp to my feet and a light to my path.

Psalm 119:105 · BSB

Read more in *The book of Psalms*.

Clarity does not mean everything is easy. Peter says so plainly about Paul's letters:

> Consider also that our Lord’s patience brings salvation, just as our beloved brother Paul also wrote you with the wisdom God gave him. He writes this way in all his letters, speaking in them about such matters. Some parts of his letters are hard to understand, which ignorant and unstable people distort, as they do the rest of the Scriptures, to their own destruction.

2 Peter 3:15–16 · BSB

Read more in *The hope to come*.

Two things stand out. Peter admits difficulty and does not pretend it away. And he counts Paul's letters with "the rest of the Scriptures". Within the New Testament itself, Christian writings are already being placed beside the Hebrew Scriptures.

[source:wcf] The Westminster Confession, a Presbyterian and Reformed statement, says it well in chapter 1, section 7: "All things in Scripture are not alike plain in themselves, nor alike clear unto all: yet those things which are necessary to be known, believed, and observed for salvation are so clearly propounded and opened in some place of Scripture or other, that not only the learned, but the unlearned, in a due use of the ordinary means, may attain unto a sufficient understanding of them." The Second London Baptist Confession takes over the same paragraph [check: confirm wording of chapter 1, section 7 in the 1677 text].

### Ordinary people checking

When Paul preached in the town of Berea, his hearers did something remarkable:

> Now the Bereans were more noble-minded than the Thessalonians, for they received the message with great eagerness and examined the Scriptures every day to see if these teachings were true.

Acts 17:11 · BSB

Read more in *Learning to discern*.

They were eager and they checked, both at once. Ordinary townspeople held an apostle's preaching up to the Scriptures they could read for themselves, and Luke calls them "noble-minded" for it.

[other] The Roman Catholic Church teaches that "the task of giving an authentic interpretation of the Word of God, whether in its written form or in the form of Tradition, has been entrusted to the living teaching office of the Church alone" [check: wording, and paragraph number, believed to be 85, of the Catechism of the Catholic Church]. Baptists hold that every believer may read Scripture and is answerable to God for how they read it. They also read it together, in a congregation, under pastors who teach, and with the warning of 2 Peter 3:16 in mind. Reading for yourself is not the same as reading by yourself.

[care] The devil quoting a psalm is a warning for anyone who has had the Bible used against them. If someone has used verses to control you, frighten you, or keep you quiet about being harmed, the quoting does not make the demand right. Tell someone you trust. A pastor who reads the whole passage with you is a good place to start, and where a crime has been committed, it belongs with the authorities.

**Consider:** What is true about the devil's quotation, and what is false about its use? What two things does Peter admit about Paul's letters?

**Reflect:** Think of a verse you often quote. Read the paragraph around it today. Does your usual use of it fit what the paragraph is doing?

## 05 About Himself: Christ at the centre of Scripture

**Main readings:** Luke 24:13–35

Hook: Two people walk away from Jerusalem with the right facts and no hope.

### We were hoping

On the Sunday after Jesus' death, two of his followers walk to a village called Emmaus, "about seven miles from Jerusalem" (Luke 24:13). A stranger joins them. "But their eyes were kept from recognizing Him" (Luke 24:16). When he asks what they are discussing, "They stood still, with sadness on their faces" (Luke 24:17).

One of them, Cleopas, tells the story: Jesus of Nazareth, "a prophet, powerful in speech and action"; his death; the women at the empty tomb and the angels' message (Luke 24:18–24). Then the saddest line in the chapter:

> But we were hoping He was the One who would redeem Israel. And besides all this, it is the third day since these things took place.

Luke 24:21 · BSB

They have every fact. They even have the report that he is alive. What they lack is the frame that makes the facts make sense.

[care] Cleopas says "Our chief priests and rulers delivered Him up" (Luke 24:20). The speaker is himself a Jew, speaking of particular leaders in one city in one week. The New Testament never lays the guilt for Jesus' death on the Jewish people. Jesus said he laid down his life of his own accord (John 10:18), and Christians confess that he died for the sins of people of every nation, their own included.

### Beginning with Moses

> Then Jesus said to them, “O foolish ones, how slow are your hearts to believe all that the prophets have spoken! Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer these things and then to enter His glory?” And beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, He explained to them what was written in all the Scriptures about Himself.

Luke 24:25–27 · BSB

**Christ** is not Jesus' surname. It is a title, the Greek form of the Hebrew "Messiah", meaning "anointed one": the king God had promised. Jesus' rebuke is not that they failed to see a miracle. It is that they had not read their Scriptures through to the end. Suffering and then glory: that, he says, was the shape all along.

Start with how Jewish readers describe these writings. They are Israel's own Scriptures, the Tanakh, read in synagogues for centuries: the Torah ("Moses" in Luke's phrase), the Prophets, and the Writings. Jewish tradition reads them as God's teaching and promises to Israel, and does not read them as pointing to Jesus. Christians read the same books with this conversation on the road as their key. Jesus himself says that "all the Scriptures" speak "about Himself". The Baptist Faith and Message puts it this way: all Scripture is "a testimony to Christ, who is Himself the focus of divine revelation".

### Burning hearts, broken bread

The two travellers urge the stranger to stay. At the table "He took bread, spoke a blessing and broke it, and gave it to them. Then their eyes were opened and they recognized Jesus—and He disappeared from their sight" (Luke 24:30–31). Only then do they understand what they had felt:

> They asked each other, “Were not our hearts burning within us as He spoke with us on the road and opened the Scriptures to us?”

Luke 24:32 · BSB

Seven miles at night did not stop them. "They got up that very hour and returned to Jerusalem", and found the others already saying, "The Lord has indeed risen and has appeared to Simon!" (Luke 24:33–34).

### What Luke does not tell us

Luke does not list the passages Jesus explained on the road, and any list a reader makes is a reconstruction. He also does not tell us to find a hidden picture of Jesus in every object and detail of the Old Testament. Reading Scripture with Christ at the centre does not erase the people and places of each passage's own story. It means following the lines that Jesus and his apostles draw, and seeing where the whole story is heading. Read more in *Christ in the Old Testament*. For the empty tomb and the risen Jesus' appearance to the disciples earlier in this chapter, read more in *The cross, the empty tomb and the throne: what Jesus' death and rising did*.

**Consider:** What did the two travellers know, and what were they missing? What shape does Jesus say the Scriptures have in verse 26?

**Reflect:** When you read the Old Testament, do you read it as a set of separate lessons, or as a story going somewhere? Where does it lead you?

## 06 He opened their minds: the Holy Spirit's help

**Main readings:** Luke 24:44–49. **Quoted:** Romans 15:4

Hook: They had heard every word before. This time they understood.

### The Law, the Prophets and the Psalms

Back in Jerusalem, the risen Jesus stands among the disciples and reminds them of what he had already told them:

> Jesus said to them, “These are the words I spoke to you while I was still with you: Everything must be fulfilled that is written about Me in the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms.” Then He opened their minds to understand the Scriptures.

Luke 24:44–45 · BSB

The three names follow the three parts of the Hebrew Scriptures as Jewish readers arrange them: the Torah, the Prophets, and the Writings, with Psalms as the best-known book of the third part. Jesus claims the whole of it. Then Luke adds a sentence that matters for every reader since: "He opened their minds". They had the words already. Understanding came as a gift.

### The message and the witnesses

What they now understand is a message:

> And He told them, “This is what is written: The Christ will suffer and rise from the dead on the third day, and in His name repentance and forgiveness of sins will be proclaimed to all nations, beginning in Jerusalem. You are witnesses of these things.

Luke 24:46–48 · BSB

To **repent** is to turn: to turn away from sin and turn to God. **Forgiveness** is God's cancelling of the debt our wrongs have run up. This is the heart of the **gospel**, which means "good news": that the Christ suffered and rose, and that anyone, from any nation, may turn and be forgiven in his name. Reading Scripture, in Luke's telling, does not end in knowing more. It ends in telling others. Read more in *Witness and the nations: telling others about Jesus*.

> And behold, I am sending the promise of My Father upon you. But remain in the city until you have been clothed with power from on high.

Luke 24:49 · BSB

"The promise of My Father" is the Holy Spirit, poured out on the disciples a few weeks later at the feast of Pentecost. Read more in *The Holy Spirit*.

### The Spirit who wrote it helps us read it

Put Luke 24 beside 2 Peter 1 and a pattern appears. The same Spirit who "carried along" the writers (2 Peter 1:21) is the one who opens readers' minds. The church's word for this second work is **illumination**, which means lighting up. The Baptist Faith and Message, in article II on God the Holy Spirit, says he "inspired holy men of old to write the Scriptures", and "through illumination He enables men to understand truth".

Paul shows what that understanding is for:

> For everything that was written in the past was written for our instruction, so that through endurance and the encouragement of the Scriptures, we might have hope.

Romans 15:4 · BSB

Read more in *Faith in daily life*.

Endurance, encouragement and hope: the old writings were given to keep people going.

### What the Spirit's help is not

The Spirit's help does not replace careful reading. The Bereans prayed and they checked. It does not add new Scripture. It opens what is already written. And not every strong feeling while reading is the Spirit's voice; a feeling is tested against what the text actually says. Read more in *Learning to discern*. Praying before you read, and asking God to open your mind as Jesus opened theirs, is this site's suggestion and not a command from the passage. But it is a prayer the passage gives you good reason to pray.

**Consider:** What did the disciples already have, and what did Jesus give them in verse 45? What message, and what task, follow from their understanding?

**Reflect:** Before you next open the Bible, will you ask God to open your mind? What would you be hoping to understand?

## Question circle: "You are quoting the book to prove the book." Isn't that circular?

Text: Luke 24:44

[read] It is a fair objection, and it deserves a fair answer. If the only reason to trust the Bible were that the Bible says so, that would be going in a circle. But that is not the whole argument. The usual Christian answer goes in steps.

[read] First, set aside for a moment any claim that the Bible is from God. Read the four Gospels simply as ancient sources about a man from Galilee in the first century. Historians do this with any ancient writing. They ask how early it is, how well it was copied, whether it rests on eyewitnesses, and whether it fits what else is known. The Gospels have more surviving copies, made closer to their writing, than almost any other ancient book. See the Academic studies *The surviving witnesses* and *Manuscripts*. Richard Bauckham's *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses* (2006) argues that the Gospels rest on eyewitness testimony. Peter, as Part 02 showed, claimed exactly that.

[read] Second, ask what those sources report about Jesus' death and what followed. The tomb was found empty. Many people, some of them sceptics, said they saw him alive. His followers changed suddenly and stayed changed under threat of death. Scholars who have weighed this evidence include N. T. Wright, *The Resurrection of the Son of God* (2003), and Gary Habermas and Michael Licona, *The Case for the Resurrection of Jesus* (2004). This page names those books and does not quote them. Read more in *The cross, the empty tomb and the throne: what Jesus' death and rising did*, and in the site's apologetics answer [Does the resurrection really matter?](/apologetics/study/resurrection).

[read] Third, if Jesus did rise from the dead, then his view of Scripture carries more weight than anyone else's. And his view is plain in these same sources: "the Scripture cannot be broken" (John 10:35); "not a single jot, not a stroke of a pen" will pass away (Matthew 5:18); "everything must be fulfilled that is written about Me" (Luke 24:44). Christians trust the Bible because they trust the one who rose, and he trusted it.

[read] Two honest notes. Every final authority is in some way tested by itself: you cannot prove reason without using reason. And this argument removes the vicious circle, but it does not replace faith. The disciples on the road had the evidence and still needed their minds opened. A person can follow every step above and still need to come to Christ.

## Question canon: Which books belong in the Bible?

Text: 2 Peter 3:16

[read] The **canon** is the list of writings received as Scripture. The word means a measuring rule. Baptists, with other Protestant churches, receive 66 books: 39 in the Old Testament and 27 in the New. Part 04 showed the beginnings of that recognition inside the New Testament itself, where Peter counts Paul's letters with "the rest of the Scriptures" (2 Peter 3:16).

[source:wcf] The Westminster Confession, chapter 1, sections 2 and 3, lists the same books and says the books "commonly called Apocrypha" are "no part of the canon of the Scripture" [check: wording against the confession's text].

[other] The Catechism of the Catholic Church, paragraph 120, lists 46 Old Testament books, including Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, and 1 and 2 Maccabees, with the same 27 in the New. ## Question copies: Hasn't the text changed over the centuries?

Text: Matthew 5:18

[read] No original page of any book of the Bible survives. What survives is thousands of copies, and they differ in small ways, mostly in spelling and word order. Comparing them lets scholars recover the wording with great confidence. The Chicago Statement affirms inerrancy for the original writings and treats the copies as faithful to them to the degree they agree. The Baptist Faith and Message affirms the Bible's truthfulness without addressing copies. Read more in the Academic studies *The surviving witnesses* and *Manuscripts*, and in the site's apologetics answer [Has the Bible been changed beyond recognition?](/apologetics/study/manuscripts).

## Close: Reading with the Bible's own habits

Way: Here is the way of reading to carry to the next passage. When a verse is quoted, by a preacher, by a friend, by yourself, or by one biblical writer quoting another, read the paragraph around it. Ask what the verse is being used to do. Then do what Jesus did in the wilderness: set it beside what is "also written". Read it as God-breathed, true, and enough. Expect it to be clear on what matters most and hard in places. Look for where it points, which is to Christ. And ask the Spirit who carried its writers along to open your mind.

Close: The Bible says these things about itself. Its own people show you how to take them: Timothy staying put in what he learned, Peter pointing past his own memory, Jesus standing on Deuteronomy while starving, and two travellers whose hearts burned as the Scriptures were opened.

Invite: If you have not yet trusted Christ, the Bible's own purpose for you is in 2 Timothy 3:15. It is able to make you "wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus". The message Jesus gave on the last page of Luke is for you: the Christ suffered and rose, and "repentance and forgiveness of sins" are offered in his name to all nations. Turn from your sin and trust him. Read more in *The good news: how a person is made right with God*.
