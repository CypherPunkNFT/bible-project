## Context, readings and limits

1. **Leviticus 1:1 after Exodus 40:35.** The link between the closed tent at the end of Exodus and God calling from the
   tent at the start of Leviticus is an observation from the two verses side by side; no source is cited for it.
2. **The offerings.** The five names follow the Berean (burnt, grain, peace, sin, guilt; 7:37 adds the ordination
   offering). Some scholars prefer "purification offering" for the sin offering, because it is also brought where no sin
   is in view (12:6–8, after childbirth); the draft keeps the Berean's word and does not raise the debate. Luke 2:24: the
   Berean footnote gives Leviticus 12:8 as the source. The draft says the sin offering was for sins done "unintentionally"
   (4:2), the guilt offering for wrongs confessed and repaid (6:1–7, which includes deliberate wrongs such as lying about
   a deposit), and that the defiant sinner had no offering (Numbers 15:30). The Day of Atonement's "all their sins"
   (16:21) is left to part 04.
3. **Hebrews 10:4 and Leviticus 4:35 together.** The draft holds both: forgiveness was real under the old covenant, and
   it rested on what Christ would do (compare Romans 3:25's "the sins committed beforehand"). This is the common
   evangelical reading; Boyce (below) speaks of the sacrifices as setting forth "substitution, imputation and
   sacrifice". The draft does not quote Boyce; he is a candidate for a Baptist voice in revision.
4. **Nadab and Abihu.** Leviticus gives only "contrary to His command" (10:1). Matthew Henry's two suggestions (wine,
   10:9; common fire instead of altar fire) are given as his, labelled as guesses. Henry also argues they had no orders
   to burn incense that day at all. Jewish interpretations (for example in the rabbinic midrash) were not read and are
   not cited.
5. **Clean and unclean.** The draft gives only the reasons Leviticus itself gives (15:31; 20:24–26) and says other
   proposed reasons (health; wholeness and order) are not in the text. **No named holder is given for those other
   reasons.** Candidates, located by name only and not read: Maimonides, *Guide for the Perplexed* 3.48 (health);
   Mary Douglas, *Purity and Danger* (1966) (order and wholeness); Gordon J. Wenham, *The Book of Leviticus* (NICOT,
   Eerdmans, 1979), who discusses these views. Name and read one for each view before publishing, or keep the sentence
   unattributed as now. Mark 7:19: the draft follows the Berean main text ("Thus all foods are clean") and reports the
   footnote's alternative. Acts 10:14: the Berean notes "impure" is literally "common", matching Leviticus 10:10's "holy
   and common"; not used in the text, could be added. Acts 15:29 (abstain from blood) is not discussed; it bears on
   Leviticus 17:10–14 and could be added in revision.
6. **Bonar.** Andrew Bonar's reading of leprosy as a figure of sin is quoted as an example of an older typological
   reading, labelled as his. The draft adds that the New Testament itself does not draw this figure. That is accurate
   (no New Testament text calls skin disease a picture of sin) but a reader could take it as a rebuke of Bonar; soften
   if the owner prefers.
7. **The Day of Atonement.** Azazel: the Berean footnote reads "Literally the other to Azazel"; the three readings (the
   departing goat, a place, a wilderness power) are given **without named holders**. Candidates, located by name only
   and not read: Wenham's commentary (above); the book of 1 Enoch (chapters 8–10), where Azazel is a fallen angel, which
   shows the third reading is ancient. Romans 3:25: the Greek *hilastērion* was checked in the local Greek texts:
   Tischendorf's New Testament for Romans 3:25 and Hebrews 9:5 ([grc-tisch](../../../../../sources/ebible/grc-tisch/)),
   and the Greek Old Testament as printed by Brenton for Leviticus 16:13–15 ([grcbrent](../../../../../sources/ebible/grcbrent/)).
   That interpreters differ on "mercy seat" versus "atoning sacrifice" is stated **without named holders**; add one on
   each side (candidates, not read: Douglas J. Moo, *The Epistle to the Romans*, NICNT, Eerdmans, 1996; Thomas R.
   Schreiner, *Romans*, BECNT, Baker, 1998, a Southern Baptist seminary professor).
8. **Holiness.** 1 Peter 1:16 is quoted with the Berean footnote's sources (Leviticus 11:44–45; 19:2). The claim that
   19:18 is the Leviticus verse the New Testament quotes most is supported by the quotations listed (Matthew 5:43;
   19:19; 22:39; Mark 12:31, 33; Luke 10:27; Romans 13:9; Galatians 5:14; James 2:8); the draft no longer states it as a
   count. Luke 10: the draft paraphrases Jesus' turn of the question and quotes only 10:29 and 10:37.
9. **The feasts.** The New Testament links named: Passover (1 Corinthians 5:7), firstfruits (1 Corinthians 15:20),
   Weeks/Pentecost (Acts 2:1, Berean footnote), the Day of Atonement (Hebrews 9). John 7:37 is placed at Tabernacles
   by John 7:2. Edersheim's account of the water rite is cited as background, not proof; Edersheim himself says the rite
   is described in later Jewish sources, and the Mishnah (Sukkah 4:9) was **not read**. The view that the autumn feasts
   are a timetable of Christ's return is described without a named holder, deliberately, and is said not to be a link
   the New Testament makes.
10. **The Jubilee.** The Hebrew word for "liberty" (Strong's number H1865) was checked in the local Westminster
    Leningrad Codex text at Leviticus 25:10 and Isaiah 61:1 ([hboWLC](../../../../../sources/ebible/hboWLC/)). "Scripture
    never records a Jubilee being kept" is an argument from silence and is stated as such; Jeremiah 34 concerns the
    seventh-year release of Hebrew slaves (Exodus 21:2; Deuteronomy 15:12), not the Jubilee itself, and 2 Chronicles
    36:21 with Leviticus 26:34–35 concerns sabbath years. The draft words this as "release broken", not "Jubilee
    broken". Whether Jesus' Nazareth sermon is a Jubilee proclamation is an echo through Isaiah, labelled so.
11. **Leviticus today.** Part 08 shows the threefold division (Calvin; the 1689 Confession) and the
    fulfilled-as-a-whole reading (progressive covenantalists and others; dispensationalists noted, with Study 26 for
    their map) without choosing, and names theonomy only to identify it. **"Baptists hold both"** rests on the 1689
    Confession on one side and, on the other, on Scheiderer's description of progressive covenantalists as fellow
    Baptists. The best-known progressive covenantal work, Peter J. Gentry and Stephen J. Wellum, *Kingdom through
    Covenant* (Crossway, 2012; 2nd ed. 2018), by two professors at the Southern Baptist Theological Seminary, is named by
    Scheiderer and was **not read** here; read its chapter on the law before publishing. Theonomy is named without a
    holder; the standard statement is Greg L. Bahnsen, *Theonomy in Christian Ethics* (1977), **not read**. The
    dispensational sentence has no named holder; add one or link only to Study 26.

## Doctrine

- **Baptist Faith and Message 2000, Article II:** Christ "honored the divine law by His personal obedience" and made
  provision for redemption by "His substitutionary death". Parts 01, 04 and 08 rest on this, argued first from Hebrews
  9–10 and Romans 3:25.
- **Article IV (salvation):** no one is justified by keeping the law; the offerings' forgiveness is presented as real and
  as resting on Christ's once-for-all sacrifice.
- **Article VIII (the Lord's Day):** quoted in part 06. How the fourth commandment relates to the first day is a point
  where Baptists differ (the 1689 Confession's chapter 22 holds a Christian Sabbath; progressive covenantalists do not,
  per Scheiderer). Part 08 names the Sabbath as the place the two readings "arrive differently" and does not choose.
- **Where Baptists differ:** how the parts of the law apply today (part 08). The confession does not address the
  threefold division. Both readings are shown with a named source on the confessional side and a named description of
  the other; the other side's own statement is still to be read (note 11).
- **Other traditions:** theonomy is identified, not argued. No Jewish interpretation is given in the site's voice; where
  the draft mentions later Jewish practice (the water rite), it is cited through Edersheim and marked as background.
