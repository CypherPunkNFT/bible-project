# Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice

**Study 47 · God and Christian Faith · First content draft · 2026-11-06**

Exodus ends with a closed door: "Moses was unable to enter the Tent of Meeting because the cloud had settled on it, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle" (Exodus 40:35 · BSB). Leviticus begins on the other side of that door.

> Then the LORD called to Moses and spoke to him from the Tent of Meeting, saying,

Leviticus 1:1 · BSB

God has come to live among His people. How can they live with Him? Leviticus answers in order: offerings (chapters 1–7), priests (8–10), clean and unclean (11–15), the Day of Atonement (16), and a call to holiness (17–27) that includes the feasts (23) and the Jubilee (25). It ends where it was heading all along: "I will make My dwelling place among you" (26:11 · BSB).

This study reads the book in that order. (Study 26, Covenants and promises, already covers the covenant at Sinai and the law and the promise.) Carry three questions through every part:

1. **What did it mean in Israel?** A real family brought a real animal to a real tent. Start there.
2. **What does the New Testament do with it?** Some things it says are fulfilled in Christ, some set aside, and some it quotes as still binding.
3. **What does it ask of me?** Leviticus calls a redeemed people to be holy, and Peter repeats the call (1 Peter 1:15–16).

## 01 The offerings: coming near with something in your hands

**Main readings:** Leviticus 1:1–9; 4:27–35; 5:5–13; 6:1–7; Hebrews 10:1–14.

Why put your hand on an animal's head before it dies? Because the worshipper was saying: this is for me.

### In Israel

Chapters 1 to 7 set out five offerings: the **burnt offering**, burned whole; the **grain offering**, seasoned with "the salt of the covenant" (2:13); the **peace offering**, whose meat the worshipper ate (7:15); the **sin offering**; and the **guilt offering**. The worshipper "is to lay his hand on the head of the burnt offering, so it can be accepted on his behalf to make atonement for him" (1:4 · BSB). The sin offering ends with a promise: "the priest will make atonement for him for the sin he has committed, and he will be forgiven" (4:35 · BSB). The guilt offering added confession (5:5) and repair: "He must make restitution in full, add a fifth of the value, and pay it to the owner" (6:5 · BSB).

Look at who could come. A man who could not afford a lamb brought two birds; one who could not afford birds brought "a tenth of an ephah of fine flour" (5:11 · BSB). No one was too poor to be forgiven. When Mary and Joseph presented Jesus, they brought "a pair of turtledoves or two young pigeons" (Luke 2:24 · BSB), the offering Leviticus allows a mother who cannot afford a lamb (12:8).

### How the New Testament reads it

Hebrews says why the offerings kept coming: the law "can never, by the same sacrifices offered year after year, make perfect those who draw near to worship" (Hebrews 10:1 · BSB). Then the contrast:

> But when this Priest had offered for all time one sacrifice for sins, He sat down at the right hand of God.

Hebrews 10:12 · BSB

The language of offering stays: believers offer their bodies "as living sacrifices" (Romans 12:1 · BSB), and praise and good deeds, "for with such sacrifices God is pleased" (Hebrews 13:16 · BSB).

### Where the reading stops

Leviticus never treats the offerings as a way to buy God off. The sin offering was for sins done "unintentionally" (4:2), and the guilt offering for wrongs confessed and repaid (6:1–7); the one who "sins defiantly" had no offering to bring (Numbers 15:30 · BSB). Hebrews says animal blood could not "take away sins" (10:4); Leviticus says the worshipper "will be forgiven" (4:35). Both are true: the forgiveness was real, given through the means God appointed, and it pointed beyond itself (Study 25, What the cross accomplishes, follows it to the cross).

**Consider:** What did the worshipper do with his hand (1:4), his mouth (5:5) and his money (6:5)?

**Reflect:** The guilt offering paid back what was taken, plus a fifth. Is there a wrong of yours that confession alone has not yet put right?

## 02 The priests: fire that accepts, fire that consumes

**Main readings:** Leviticus 8:1–13; 9:22–24; 10:1–11; Hebrews 7:11–12; 1 Peter 2:5, 9.

On the same day, fire came out from the LORD twice.

### In Israel

Aaron and his sons are ordained (chapter 8). On the eighth day Aaron offers and blesses the people; "Fire came out from the presence of the LORD and consumed the burnt offering," and the people "shouted for joy and fell facedown" (9:24 · BSB). Then, with no break in the story:

> Now Aaron's sons Nadab and Abihu took their censers, put fire in them and added incense, and offered unauthorized fire before the LORD, contrary to His command. So fire came out from the presence of the LORD and consumed them, and they died in the presence of the LORD.

Leviticus 10:1–2 · BSB

Moses gives God's word: "To those who come near Me I will show My holiness" (10:3 · BSB). Aaron is silent. The priests must "distinguish between the holy and the common, between the clean and the unclean" (10:10 · BSB).

### How the New Testament reads it

Hebrews argues that Aaron's line could not bring "perfection", so God raised up another priest, and "when the priesthood is changed, the law must be changed as well" (Hebrews 7:11–12 · BSB). Peter gives the priests' language to the whole church: "a holy priesthood, offering spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ" (1 Peter 2:5 · BSB). Study 41, Christ in the Old Testament, follows Christ as the priest.

### Where the reading stops

What made the fire "unauthorized"? Leviticus says only "contrary to His command" (10:1). Right after the story, God forbids priests wine when they enter the tent (10:9), and the commentator Matthew Henry suggests, "Probably they had seen the ill effect of it in Nadab and Abihu." He also suggests it was common fire, not fire from the altar. The text confirms neither guess; what it says is enough: God is approached as He has said. Hebrews keeps the warning for believers who now come near freely: "worship God acceptably with reverence and awe. 'For our God is a consuming fire'" (Hebrews 12:28–29 · BSB).

**Consider:** What did the same fire do in 9:24 and in 10:2? What made the difference?

**Reflect:** Every believer is now a priest (1 Peter 2:5). Where is your worship shaped more by your own ideas than by what God has said?

## 03 Clean and unclean: what may come near

**Main readings:** Leviticus 11:44–47; 13:45–46; 15:31; 20:24–26; Mark 1:40–42; 7:18–19; Acts 10:9–35.

A man with a skin disease had to cover his mouth and call out "Unclean, unclean!" (13:45 · BSB). One day a man like that knelt in front of Jesus.

### In Israel

Chapters 11 to 15 cover food, childbirth, skin disease, mildew and discharges. Unclean is not the same as sinful: a new mother was unclean for a time (chapter 12), yet bearing a child is no sin. To be unclean was to be unfit, for a time, to come near the tabernacle: "so that they do not die by defiling My tabernacle, which is among them" (15:31 · BSB). The food laws carry a second reason:

> You are therefore to distinguish between clean and unclean animals and birds... You are to be holy to Me because I, the LORD, am holy, and I have set you apart from the nations to be My own.

Leviticus 20:25–26 · BSB

Every meal reminded Israel whose people they were.

### How the New Testament reads it

The leper says, "If You are willing, You can make me clean," and Jesus "reached out His hand and touched the man" (Mark 1:40–41 · BSB). Touching the unclean made a person unclean; here cleanness flows the other way. Then He sends him to the priest with "the offering Moses prescribed" (Luke 5:14 · BSB), keeping the law while showing Himself greater than it.

Later Jesus says food "does not enter his heart, but it goes into the stomach," and Mark comments: "(Thus all foods are clean.)" (Mark 7:19 · BSB). The Berean's footnote gives another way to take the Greek ("thereby expelling all foods"); the main text is followed here. Peter needed a vision to learn it: "Do not call anything impure that God has made clean" (Acts 10:15 · BSB). He draws the conclusion about people: "God has shown me that I should not call any man impure or unclean" (10:28 · BSB). The line that kept Israel apart from the nations is crossed by the gospel (Ephesians 2:14).

### Where the reading stops

Leviticus gives two reasons for these laws: God's dwelling among His people (15:31) and Israel's separation from the nations (20:24–26). Others have been proposed, such as health or a picture of wholeness; Leviticus does not give them, and none should be taught as the meaning. Some older commentators read the chapters as pictures of sin; the Scottish minister Andrew Bonar wrote that "Jehovah opens up sin under the figure of leprosy." The New Testament does not explain the skin laws as a figure; it shows Jesus making the unclean clean.

**Consider:** In Mark 1:41, who would normally become unclean by the touch? What happens instead?

**Reflect:** Peter learned not to call any person unclean. Is there a person, or a kind of person, you keep at a distance?

## 04 The Day of Atonement: two goats and a curtain

**Main readings:** Leviticus 16; Romans 3:25; Hebrews 9:7–14; 10:19–22; 13:11–13.

Leviticus 16 opens by remembering the two dead priests (16:1). Aaron may not come behind the curtain whenever he likes, "or else he will die" (16:2 · BSB). Once a year, he may.

### In Israel

On the tenth day of the seventh month the high priest cast lots over two goats, "one for the LORD and the other for the scapegoat" (16:8 · BSB). The first was killed, and its blood sprinkled "against the mercy seat" behind the veil (16:15). Then the second:

> Then he is to lay both hands on the head of the live goat and confess over it all the iniquities and rebellious acts of the Israelites in regard to all their sins.

Leviticus 16:21 · BSB

One goat dies; the other carries sin "into a solitary place" (16:22). The promise: "you will be clean from all your sins before the LORD" (16:30 · BSB).

### How the New Testament reads it

The high priest went in "only once a year, and never without blood" (Hebrews 9:7 · BSB); Christ "entered the Most Holy Place once for all by His own blood, thus securing eternal redemption" (9:12 · BSB). The bodies burned "outside the camp" (Leviticus 16:27) become a picture of the cross: "Jesus also suffered outside the city gate" (Hebrews 13:12 · BSB). And the curtain now has a way through it: "the new and living way opened for us through the curtain of His body" (10:20 · BSB).

Paul reaches for the same day. God presented Christ "as the atoning sacrifice through faith in His blood" (Romans 3:25 · BSB); the Berean's footnote offers "as a propitiation". The Greek word, *hilastērion*, is the one Hebrews 9:5 uses for the mercy seat, and the Greek translation of Leviticus 16 uses it too.

### Where the reading stops

Two questions stay open. "Scapegoat" is, as the Berean's footnote says, literally "to Azazel", a word found only in this chapter. Readers take it as the goat that goes away, a remote place, or the name of an evil power of the wilderness; whichever is right, the text is plain that the goat carries Israel's sins away. And interpreters differ on whether Romans 3:25 means the mercy seat itself or atoning sacrifice in general. Both say the same of Christ: His blood does what that day pictured. The Baptist Faith and Message: "in His substitutionary death on the cross He made provision for the redemption of men from sin" (Article II).

**Consider:** What happens to the first goat, and to the second? What does each show about sin?

**Reflect:** "Let us draw near with a sincere heart in full assurance of faith" (Hebrews 10:22 · BSB). What keeps you standing outside?

## 05 Be holy: the neighbour, the poor and the foreigner

**Main readings:** Leviticus 19:1–37; 20:7–8, 26; 1 Peter 1:14–19; Mark 12:28–31; Luke 10:25–37.

"Be holy" sounds like a command for the temple. Leviticus 19 works it out in a field, a courtroom and a neighbour's house.

### In Israel

Chapters 17 to 27 call the whole people to holiness, and the reason is God Himself:

> Speak to the whole congregation of Israel and tell them: Be holy because I, the LORD your God, am holy.

Leviticus 19:2 · BSB

Then holiness comes down to earth. Leave the edges of your field "for the poor and the foreigner" (19:10 · BSB). Pay the hired hand before morning; do not trip the blind; judge fairly (19:13–15). Then: "Do not seek revenge or bear a grudge against any of your people, but love your neighbor as yourself. I am the LORD" (19:18 · BSB). The same love reaches the outsider: "love him as yourself, for you were foreigners in the land of Egypt" (19:34 · BSB).

Notice what sits between them: "you shall not wear clothing made of two kinds of material" (19:19 · BSB). Leviticus does not file its commands in separate drawers; holiness meant being set apart to God in everything. Part 08 asks how Christians tell these commands apart.

### How the New Testament reads it

Peter quotes the refrain to scattered Christians in Asia Minor: "just as He who called you is holy, so be holy in all you do, for it is written: 'Be holy, because I am holy'" (1 Peter 1:15–16 · BSB). Jesus names love for God as the first commandment, then "The second is this: 'Love your neighbor as yourself'" (Mark 12:31 · BSB). Paul says "The entire law is fulfilled in a single decree" (Galatians 5:14 · BSB); James calls it "the royal law" (James 2:8 · BSB). Asked "And who is my neighbor?" (Luke 10:29 · BSB), Jesus tells of a Samaritan, and the neighbour is "The one who showed him mercy" (10:37 · BSB).

### Where the reading stops

Holiness in Leviticus begins with what God has done: "I am the LORD who sanctifies you" (20:8 · BSB). Peter keeps the same order: his readers were redeemed "with the precious blood of Christ" (1 Peter 1:19 · BSB) before he calls them to be holy. Holiness is the life of people God has already claimed, not the price of being claimed.

**Consider:** List the people Leviticus 19:9–18 tells Israel to protect. What does that say holiness looks like?

**Reflect:** Who is the person you have been hoping is not your neighbour?

## 06 The feasts: a year shaped by God

**Main readings:** Leviticus 23; 1 Corinthians 5:7; 15:20; Acts 2:1; John 7:37–39; Colossians 2:16–17.

Before any feast, Leviticus 23 names the week: "the seventh day is a Sabbath of complete rest" (23:3 · BSB).

### In Israel

Then come "My appointed feasts" (23:2 · BSB). In spring, **Passover** and **Unleavened Bread** (23:5–8), the **firstfruits** of harvest (23:10–11) and, fifty days on, the feast of **Weeks** (23:15–21). In autumn, the day of **trumpet blasts** (23:24), the **Day of Atonement** (23:27) and **Tabernacles**, a week living in booths, "so that your descendants may know that I made the Israelites dwell in booths when I brought them out of the land of Egypt" (23:43 · BSB).

### How the New Testament reads it

"Christ, our Passover lamb, has been sacrificed" (1 Corinthians 5:7 · BSB). Christ raised is "the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Corinthians 15:20 · BSB). The Spirit comes "when the day of Pentecost came" (Acts 2:1 · BSB), the feast of Weeks, as the Berean's footnote explains. At Tabernacles Jesus cries, "If anyone is thirsty, let him come to Me and drink" (John 7:37 · BSB). Alfred Edersheim's *The Temple: Its Ministry and Services* (1874) describes a later temple rite at that feast, water from the Pool of Siloam poured at the altar. Leviticus does not command it and John does not mention it: background, not proof. Paul speaks of all such days together: "These are a shadow of the things to come, but the body that casts it belongs to Christ" (Colossians 2:17 · BSB).

### Where the reading stops

The New Testament draws lines from Passover, firstfruits, Weeks and the Day of Atonement to Christ. It draws none from the day of trumpets. Some teachers read the autumn feasts as a timetable of Christ's return; that is their interpretation, not a link the New Testament makes. For the day of rest, the Baptist Faith and Message speaks of the first day: "The first day of the week is the Lord's Day... It commemorates the resurrection of Christ from the dead," kept by "the Christian's conscience under the Lordship of Jesus Christ" (Article VIII).

**Consider:** Which feasts does the New Testament connect with Christ, and which does it leave unconnected?

**Reflect:** Israel's year was built around remembering what God had done. What in your week is built around remembering Him?

## 07 The Jubilee: the land is Mine

**Main readings:** Leviticus 25:8–55; Isaiah 61:1–2; Luke 4:16–21.

Every fiftieth year a horn sounded across the land, and families went home.

### In Israel

The Jubilee was announced on the Day of Atonement (25:9), forgiveness and freedom together: "proclaim liberty in the land for all its inhabitants. It shall be your Jubilee, when each of you is to return to his property and to his clan" (25:10 · BSB). Land sold under debt went back to its family; Israelites who had sold themselves into service went free. The reason is God's ownership: "The land must not be sold permanently, because it is Mine" (25:23 · BSB); "the Israelites are My servants... whom I brought out of the land of Egypt" (25:55 · BSB).

### How the New Testament reads it

At Nazareth Jesus reads Isaiah 61, "He has sent Me to proclaim liberty to the captives... to proclaim the year of the Lord's favor," and says, "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:18–21 · BSB). Isaiah's Hebrew word for "liberty" is the word Leviticus 25:10 uses for the Jubilee, and many readers hear the Jubilee in Isaiah's "year of the LORD's favor". Luke does not name the Jubilee: the link is an echo through Isaiah, not a quotation.

### Where the reading stops

Scripture never records a Jubilee being kept. It records release broken: in Jeremiah's day Jerusalem freed its slaves, then "later they changed their minds and took back" those they had freed (Jeremiah 34:11 · BSB). Leviticus warned that a land denied its rest would have it in exile, "the rest it did not receive during the Sabbaths when you lived in it" (26:35 · BSB), and the Chronicler says that is what happened (2 Chronicles 36:21). The Jubilee shows what God wanted for His people's life together, and what they did not do.

**Consider:** What two reasons do Leviticus 25:23 and 25:55 give for the Jubilee?

**Reflect:** "The land... is Mine." What do you treat as finally yours that God says is His?

## 08 Leviticus today: how Christians sort the commands

**Main readings:** Leviticus 19:18–19; Matthew 5:17–18; Romans 13:8–10; Hebrews 8:13; 1 Corinthians 9:20–21.

Leviticus 19 puts "love your neighbor" and the rule about mixed cloth one verse apart. Christians keep the first and wear blended fabric without guilt. On what grounds?

### Where Christians agree

No one is made right with God by keeping Leviticus, and the sacrifices have ended: "by a single offering He has made perfect for all time those who are being sanctified" (Hebrews 10:14 · BSB). Jesus came not "to abolish the Law or the Prophets... but to fulfill them" (Matthew 5:17 · BSB). Paul still speaks of the law as holy and good (Romans 7:12). The Baptist Faith and Message says Christ "honored the divine law by His personal obedience" (Article II). The question is how to get from there to the rest of the book.

Baptists answer in three families. Each is set out below with Baptists who hold it, so you can see that all three are at home in Baptist churches.

### Three kinds of law

The best-known answer divides the law into three. John Calvin called it "the well-known division which distributes the whole law of God, as promulgated by Moses, into the moral, the ceremonial, and the judicial law" (*Institutes* 4.20.14). The Second London Baptist Confession (1677, reissued 1689) holds it: the **ceremonial** laws, "prefiguring Christ", are "abrogated and taken away"; the **judicial** laws "expired together with the state of that people", "their general equity onely, being of moral use"; the **moral** law, given at Sinai in the Ten Commandments, "doth for ever bind all" (chapter 19). On this reading 19:18 is moral and binds today, 19:19 is ceremonial and fulfilled, and Israel's penalties ended with Israel's state, though the justice in them still teaches.

**Baptists who hold it.** This is the older Baptist confessional line. The Baptist Catechism (approved 1693, printed 1695) teaches that the moral law is summed up in the Ten Commandments [check: the question number and exact wording in the Baptist Catechism]. Today it is held by Baptists who keep the 1689 confession, often called confessional or Reformed Baptists. Richard Barcellos defends it against the other readings in *In Defense of the Decalogue* [check: publisher and year, believed to be 2001].

### The law of Moses ended as a whole: the dispensational reading

A **dispensational** reading sees the Bible's story as a series of stages ("dispensations") in God's dealings with people, and keeps God's promises to Israel distinct from His purposes for the church. On this reading the law of Moses was given to Israel as one unit, and it ended as one unit when Christ came. Christians are not under Moses at all. The commandments that appear again in the New Testament bind Christians because Christ and His apostles give them, not because Moses did. Paul is the example they point to:

> The commandments "Do not commit adultery," "Do not murder," "Do not steal," "Do not covet," and any other commandments, are summed up in this one decree: "Love your neighbor as yourself."

Romans 13:9 · BSB

So 19:18 binds a Christian because Jesus and Paul repeat it; 19:19 does not, because no one in the New Testament takes it up. The Sabbath command is the one of the Ten that the New Testament does not repeat as a command, and this reading does not bind it on Christians.

**Baptists who hold it.** This view is widely held in independent and fundamentalist Baptist churches, and it reached many Southern Baptist congregations through the notes of the Scofield Reference Bible (1909). A dispensational statement of the law question is Wayne G. Strickland's essay in *Five Views on Law and Gospel* (Zondervan, 1996) [check: Strickland's church affiliation; if he is not a Baptist, replace with a named Baptist dispensational writer and the book where he states this view]. Read more in *Covenants and promises*, which maps the dispensational view of the covenants (a **covenant** is a binding agreement God makes with His people).

### Fulfilled in Christ: progressive covenantalism and new covenant theology

Other Baptists also read the law of Moses as one covenant that has ended, but they read the Bible's covenants as one storyline that reaches its goal in Christ, rather than keeping Israel and the church on separate tracks. Hebrews says that in speaking of a new covenant God has made the first one obsolete (Hebrews 8:13). Paul says he is not under the law, yet he is under the law of Christ (1 Corinthians 9:20–21). On this reading a command from Leviticus binds Christians as the New Testament takes it up through Christ, as it does 19:18 and does not do with the food laws.

**Baptists who hold it.** The name **progressive covenantalism** (each covenant unfolding in turn until all are fulfilled in the new covenant) comes from Peter Gentry and Stephen Wellum, both teachers at the Southern Baptist Theological Seminary, in *Kingdom through Covenant* (Crossway, 2012). Thomas Schreiner, of the same seminary, works the law question through in *40 Questions About Christians and Biblical Law* (Kregel, 2010). A close relative is **new covenant theology**, set out by the Baptists Tom Wells and Fred Zaspel in *New Covenant Theology* (2002) [check: publisher].

Among Baptists, the confessional heirs of 1689 and the progressive covenantalists differ here; a 2023 article by the confessional Baptist Daniel Scheiderer notes that progressive covenantalists do not treat the Ten Commandments as the summary of the moral law, and that the two part most visibly over the Sabbath [check: title and place of publication of Scheiderer's 2023 article, and that it says this].

### A further question: Israel's penalties

A further question is whether nations should adopt Israel's civil penalties. Calvin rejected those who "deny that any commonwealth is rightly framed which neglects the law of Moses" (*Institutes* 4.20.14). A modern Reformed movement called theonomy argues that they should; it is named here so you will recognise it. None of the three Baptist families above holds it.

### Where this study stands

The Baptist Faith and Message does not choose among these readings, and neither does this study; Baptists hold all three. They differ more in how they reach a command than in where they arrive, though the Sabbath is one place they arrive differently. The confession does settle that the first day of the week is the Lord's Day, for worship, commemorating the resurrection (Article VIII). All three keep 19:18, none offers a sacrifice, and all read Leviticus as God's word, pointing to Christ.

How each family reads the Ten Commandments themselves, one by one, belongs to another study. Read more in *The Ten Commandments*.

**Consider:** Take Leviticus 19:18 and 19:19. How would each of the three views explain why a Christian keeps one and not the other?

**Reflect:** It is easy to argue about mixed cloth and forget the neighbour. Which takes more of your energy?

## Read Leviticus again

Leviticus closes its promises with God among His people: "I will walk among you and be your God, and you will be My people" (26:12 · BSB). Paul gives that promise to a church: "For we are the temple of the living God. As God has said: 'I will dwell with them and walk among them, and I will be their God, and they will be My people'" (2 Corinthians 6:16 · BSB). The book that seems farthest from Christian life describes it: a holy God, a sacrifice that opens the way, a people set apart to love Him and their neighbour.

**Your question:** God says to you, through Leviticus and through Peter, "Be holy, because I am holy." Where this week, in a field, a courtroom or a neighbour's house, is He asking you to live as someone He has already set apart?

Read the passage again before you answer.
