# The church and its calling

Concepts: One word · Four sizes · Two ordinances
Motto: Many stones. / One house.
Lead: The New Testament's letters were read aloud to rooms full of people, and most of what they say about the church is said to all of them at once. So this study teaches one habit. Before you ask what a line says to you, ask who was in the room.

> As you come to Him, the living stone, rejected by men but chosen and precious in God's sight, you also, like living stones, are being built into a spiritual house to be a holy priesthood, offering spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ.

1 Peter 2:4–5 · BSB

Selection: This page takes six readings from the study's written draft, every use of one English word, and every baptism Acts tells. It counts the word as the Berean prints it. It does not survey every tradition's teaching about the church, and where it names another tradition it says whose words they are. How the gifts of the Spirit work today belongs to the planned study of the Holy Spirit.

WordLead: Every time the Berean New Testament prints the word church or churches, one point of light. The rings answer one question: how big is the church this verse is talking about?
WordReads: Most of the time it is a people you could visit. A few meet in one house. Most are named by a town, or counted as many churches in many towns. A smaller number of uses reach past every town to all who belong to Christ, and most of those are in Ephesians and Colossians. Neither size cancels the other. This is what Baptists confess in one article, which calls a church an "autonomous local congregation of baptized believers", then says the New Testament also speaks of the church as the Body of Christ, including "all of the redeemed of all the ages".
WordCare: Which ring a verse sits in is this study's judgment, and a hollow point marks a close call. The script proves only that every use is here, that a use on the house ring is a church meeting at a house, and that a use on the many-places ring is the plural or on a short named list. It counts one English word. The Berean also writes "assembly" in Acts 7:38 and "congregation" in Hebrews 12:23, and those are not counted. No Greek was compared.

ReadingsLead: Each reading opens with who was in the room, in the passage's own words. Then what it says, what this study takes it to mean, and a question left for you. Choose a picture to read it.

WaterLead: Acts tells of ten baptisms, counting each time it says that a person or a group was baptized in Jesus' name and not counting commands, John's baptism or a retelling. Read down the water. On the left, what the passage says came first. On the right, the baptism.
WaterReads: In nine of the ten, Acts names hearing, believing, praying or receiving the Spirit before the water, of those it says were baptized. For Lydia's household Acts says nothing about faith or age. It names only her opened heart. For the jailer's household and for Crispus' it says they believed. No infant is named, and none is ruled out. This pattern is one reason Baptists baptize those who have believed.
WaterCare: Baptism follows faith, and it does not prove it. Simon "believed and was baptized" in Acts 8:13, and is told "your heart is not right before God" in Acts 8:21. The Berean has no verse between Acts 8:36 and Acts 8:38, so that passage is shown in two parts. Choosing which words count as what came first is this study's judgment.

NamesLead: Two verses in Peter's first letter describe a scattered people in six phrases: names, a task, and a change of standing. Each has an older sound. Choose one to see an older verse beside it.
NamesReads: These were said of Israel first. Peter gives them to people he has just called "exiles of the Dispersion" in 1 Peter 1:1, people who were once not a people at all in 1 Peter 2:10. Scripture is reading Scripture here, and a reader who knows Exodus and Hosea hears much more in those verses than one who does not.
NamesCare: Peter gives no chapter and verse for these. The pairings are this study's, and the page says of each how close the wording is, in the Berean's English. How Israel and the church relate is a question on which Baptists themselves differ, and this page does not choose. The Covenants and promises mock-up shows several Baptist maps of it.

RoomLead: Each of these three lines is often read as if it were written to one person alone. Try the habit on each one: who was in the room? Then open the answer.

QuestionsLead: Six things readers ask, each opening on the side. Each gives the passage, then this study's reading in the site's own voice, then what others have taught, labelled as theirs.

## 01 One body, many parts

Slug: body
Read: 1 Corinthians 12:12–31; Ephesians 4:1–16
Ask: Which of the two mistakes is yours?
Hook: One part says it does not belong. Another says it needs no one. Paul answers both.

### Each of you is a member

Paul compares the church to a body whose different parts belong together. A foot does not stop belonging because it is not a hand. An ear does not become unnecessary because it is not an eye. Being different does not cancel membership.

The same picture then turns on the part that claims to need no one. "The eye cannot say to the hand, I do not need you" in 1 Corinthians 12:21. The parts that seem weaker are called "indispensable" in 1 Corinthians 12:22. God composed the body so "that its members should have mutual concern for one another" in 1 Corinthians 12:25.

> Now you are the body of Christ, and each of you is a member of it.

1 Corinthians 12:27 · BSB

[read] Belonging has consequences. "If one part suffers, every part suffers with it" in 1 Corinthians 12:26. So this passage gives the prominent member no leave to dismiss anyone, and gives the unseen member no reason to conclude there is no place for them.

### To build up the body

Ephesians opens its appeal for unity with humility, gentleness and patience. Then it lists what is already one: "one body and one Spirit", one hope, Lord, faith and baptism, in Ephesians 4:4–5.

Christ gives some "to be pastors and teachers" in Ephesians 4:11, and the next verse says what for: "to equip the saints for works of ministry and to build up the body of Christ", in Ephesians 4:12.

[read] The picture closes on "the work of each individual part" in Ephesians 4:16. Teachers are given so that the rest can do the work. The church's ministry is larger than what its most visible people do.

Consider: Which two mistaken attitudes does Paul's picture of the body correct? What does Ephesians say the gifts of Christ are for?
Reflect: Think of someone in your church whose work no one notices. What would change if you treated that work as indispensable?

## 02 Members of God's household

Slug: house
Read: Ephesians 2:11–22; 1 Peter 2:4–10
Ask: Whose house is it, and how did you get in?
Hook: They had been strangers at the door. Now the letter calls them family.

### No longer strangers

Ephesians speaks to Gentiles who were once "alienated from the commonwealth of Israel" in Ephesians 2:12. They "have been brought near through the blood of Christ" in Ephesians 2:13. Christ tore down the wall of hostility and brought both into one body through the cross.

> Therefore you are no longer strangers and foreigners, but fellow citizens with the saints and members of God's household,

Ephesians 2:19 · BSB

[read] The movement is from outside to inside, and no one in the room earned the door. These people are not guests, tolerated by those with a better claim. The picture then becomes a building, "with Christ Jesus Himself as the cornerstone" in Ephesians 2:20, and it is still growing into "a holy temple in the Lord" in Ephesians 2:21. The building is the people.

### A spiritual house

First Peter uses the same picture. Christ is "the living stone", and those who come to Him are "like living stones" in 1 Peter 2:4–5. Christ stays at the centre: He is the stone people rejected, and God chose.

[read] Peter's names for this people lead straight to a purpose, "to proclaim the virtues of Him who called you" in 1 Peter 2:9. Mercy is the reason for those names, as 1 Peter 2:10 says, so they give no one grounds to look down on anyone. The section on six phrases, further down this page, traces where they came from.

Consider: What has changed between the start and the end of Ephesians 2? What purpose does Peter attach to the names he gives?
Reflect: When you say my church, do you first picture a building and a schedule, or the people Christ has brought in with you?

## 03 Everything in order

Slug: order
Read: 1 Corinthians 14:1–19; 1 Corinthians 14:26–40; 1 Corinthians 11:17–22
Ask: Who in the room can follow what is happening?
Hook: A trumpet that makes a muffled sound calls no one to anything.

### So that the church may be edified

Paul weighs speech, prayer and song in the gathering by one test: are others understanding and being built up? He uses a trumpet to make the point. "If the trumpet sounds a muffled call, who will prepare for battle?" in 1 Corinthians 14:8.

In the church he would rather speak "five coherent words to instruct others" than ten thousand they cannot follow, in 1 Corinthians 14:19. He also thinks of the visitor. An unbeliever who comes in and understands may end up "proclaiming, God is truly among you!" in 1 Corinthians 14:25.

[read] One person's experience is not the measure of the gathering. The good of the people hearing is part of what makes a gift rightly used.

[open] Whether tongues and prophecy continue today is a question on which Baptists differ, because the Baptist Faith and Message does not settle it. This page does not choose. The planned study of the Holy Spirit takes it up.

### In a proper and orderly manner

The chapter gives plain directions: speakers take turns, someone interprets, and others "weigh carefully what is said" in 1 Corinthians 14:29.

> But everything must be done in a proper and orderly manner.

1 Corinthians 14:40 · BSB

[read] Order here serves people. The chapter does not give a service schedule for every later congregation. It does say that enthusiasm is no reason to ignore whether the gathering helps anyone. The verses on women's speech in this chapter are taken up in the question on who may be a pastor, further down.

Three chapters earlier Paul adds a sharper test: "your gatherings do more harm than good" in 1 Corinthians 11:17. Being in one room does not by itself mean a church is honouring its calling.

Consider: Why does Paul keep asking whether others understand? What does order accomplish in his argument?
Reflect: Think of something in your church's gathering that a first-time visitor could not follow. What could you explain or change so they could?

## 04 Be shepherds of God's flock

Slug: flock
Read: 1 Timothy 3:1–15; 1 Peter 5:1–4; Acts 20:17–28
Ask: Whose flock is it?
Hook: The flock is never the shepherd's own. Every passage says so.

### How can he care for the church of God?

First Timothy describes an overseer almost entirely by character: "temperate, self-controlled, respectable, hospitable, able to teach" and "not violent but gentle", in 1 Timothy 3:2–3. Violence, greed and pride are named as disqualifying. They are not treated as the cost of strong leadership.

Deacons must be "dignified" and "must first be tested" in 1 Timothy 3:8–10. The letter says why all this matters: so that "each one" knows how to live "in God's household, which is the church of the living God", in 1 Timothy 3:15.

### Examples to the flock

Peter writes as "a fellow elder" in 1 Peter 5:1 and says how the work is done.

> not lording it over those entrusted to you, but being examples to the flock.

1 Peter 5:3 · BSB

[read] Three words in the New Testament name this work, and two passages put them together. Paul sends for "the elders of the church" in Acts 20:17 and tells them the Holy Spirit made them "overseers" and to "be shepherds" in Acts 20:28. Peter tells "the elders among you" to "be shepherds of God's flock" in 1 Peter 5:1–2. This is why Baptists speak of one office, pastor, elder and overseer, with deacons beside it.

[source:barnes] Albert Barnes, a Presbyterian, draws the same conclusion from Acts 20:28: elder and bishop were names "given to the same class of officers". His church and ours arrange things differently after that.

[read] The flock belongs to God, and Christ is "the Chief Shepherd" in 1 Peter 5:4. A leader's authority is real, and these verses rule out domination as the way to use it.

[care] No title excuses mistreating people. If a leader has harmed you, telling someone you trust is not disloyalty to Christ, and where a crime has been committed, it belongs with the authorities.

Consider: Which qualifications here are about character and which about ability? How does knowing the flock is God's change how it is led?
Reflect: How would you recognise patient, trustworthy service in your church, without confusing it with being well known?

## 05 Water and bread

Slug: water
Read: Matthew 28:16–20; 1 Corinthians 11:17–34
Ask: What are these two things for?
Hook: Water once, for each believer. Bread again and again, together.

### Baptizing them

The risen Jesus sends the eleven to "make disciples of all nations, baptizing them" and "teaching them to obey", in Matthew 28:19–20. Baptism sits inside a commission, with teaching after it and His presence promised over it.

Paul says what it pictures: "We were therefore buried with Him through baptism into death", so that "we too may walk in newness of life", in Romans 6:4.

[read] Baptists hold that Christian baptism is "the immersion of a believer in water". It is "an act of obedience symbolizing the believer's faith", and that picture of burial and rising is one reason for going under the water. It does not save. It says in public what has already happened. The section on ten baptisms, further down this page, sets out what Acts tells, which is part of where this reading comes from.

### You proclaim the Lord's death

Paul recalls Jesus' words over the bread and the cup in order to correct a divided meal. "While one remains hungry, another gets drunk" in 1 Corinthians 11:21. Those with nothing are humiliated.

> For as often as you eat this bread and drink this cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until He comes.

1 Corinthians 11:26 · BSB

[read] Baptists call the Supper "a symbolic act of obedience", by which members of the church "memorialize the death of the Redeemer and anticipate His second coming". It looks back and it looks forward. Paul's warning about eating "in an unworthy manner" in 1 Corinthians 11:27 has to be read with the conduct he is confronting. The meal is not a test of whether you feel deserving. It is a meal of people who deserved nothing, and Paul's remedy is "wait for one another" in 1 Corinthians 11:33.

Consider: What goes with baptism in Jesus' commission? What was the Corinthians' conduct doing to the meaning of the meal?
Reflect: How should remembering what Christ gave up change the way you welcome the person next to you at the table?

## 06 Proclaim the virtues

Slug: sent
Read: 1 Peter 2:9–12; Acts 1:6–8
Ask: Who is all this for?
Hook: They asked Him when. He told them where.

### Out of darkness

Peter's names for God's people lead to a task, and the task begins with what God has done: He "called you out of darkness into His marvelous light" in 1 Peter 2:9.

He then calls them "foreigners and exiles" and tells them to live with such honour among their neighbours that even those who slander them "may see your good deeds and glorify God" in 1 Peter 2:11–12.

[read] Speaking and living go together here. Good conduct does not replace telling people the gospel, and telling them does not make conduct irrelevant. Peter does not promise that doing right will stop the slander.

### You will be My witnesses

The disciples ask whether the kingdom will now be restored. Jesus sets aside the timetable and gives them a task.

> But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit comes upon you, and you will be My witnesses in Jerusalem, and in all Judea and Samaria, and to the ends of the earth.

Acts 1:8 · BSB

[read] The promise was first spoken to those disciples, on that day, and the book of Acts is the story of it coming true. The church since has rightly taken the task as its own, with Matthew 28:19. So a church's calling is larger than keeping its own gatherings going. Baptists confess that every believer and every church is to "endeavor to make disciples of all nations". The Bearing witness mock-up shows what this looks like in ordinary speech.

Consider: How does Peter join who they are, what they say and how they live? What does Jesus give the disciples in place of a timetable?
Reflect: What is one way your church's life together could reach someone outside its familiar circle?

## Question alone: Can I follow Christ without a church?

Text: Hebrews 10:24–25

[read] Faith in Christ is personal, and no one is saved by belonging. Baptists hold "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord", so a believer far from any church, or one in prison or too ill to go, is not cut off from Christ. But the New Testament has no picture of a Christian who chooses to stay apart. "Let us not neglect meeting together" is in Hebrews 10:25, and the body picture rules out saying "I do not need you", in 1 Corinthians 12:21.

[care] Some people stay away because a church hurt them. That is a wound, and it is a different thing from indifference. Leaving a church that has harmed you is not leaving Christ. When you are ready, you could look for a church whose leaders fit what Peter describes, "examples to the flock" in 1 Peter 5:3.

[source:lbc] The Second London Baptist Confession of 1677, chapter 26, puts it this way: "all Believers are bound to joyn themselves to particular Churches, when and where they have opportunity so to do".

## Question who: Who should be baptized, and how?

Text: Acts 2:41

[read] This site holds with the Baptist Faith and Message that baptism is "the immersion of a believer in water". The reasons are the pattern in Acts, the command in Matthew 28:19 that puts making disciples first, and the picture of burial and rising in Romans 6:4. Philip and the eunuch "went down into the water", and "came up out of the water", in Acts 8:38–39.

[source:nh] Older Baptists said the same. The New Hampshire Confession of 1833 calls baptism "the immersion in water of a believer".

[source:wcf] The Westminster Confession, chapter 28, teaches otherwise, as a Presbyterian and Reformed confession: "the infants of one or both believing parents are to be baptized", and "Dipping of the person into the water is not necessary". It reasons from God's covenant with believers and their children.

[source:barnes] Barnes, a Presbyterian commentator, finds in Lydia's household "a strong presumptive proof that this was an instance of household or infant baptism" on Acts 16:15.

[source:jfb] Jamieson, Fausset and Brown, who are not Baptists either, state it differently on the same verse. They presume children were there, then say that whether they were "is not explicitly stated", and that "the question of infant baptism must be determined on other grounds".

[read] Baptists answer that the jailer's household "rejoiced that they had come to believe in God" in Acts 16:34, and that Crispus' "whole household believed in the Lord" in Acts 18:8. Lydia's verse says nothing either way. No household passage names an infant, so these verses cannot settle the question for either side. Baptists rest it on the command and the pattern.

## Question saves: Does baptism save?

Text: 1 Peter 3:21

[read] Baptists answer no. Salvation is by grace through faith in Christ, as Ephesians 2:8 says, and the thief on the cross was promised Paradise in Luke 23:43, where Luke records no baptism. The hard verses say what they say. Peter writes "this water symbolizes the baptism that now saves you also", and goes straight on: "not the removal of dirt from the body, but the pledge of a clear conscience toward God", in 1 Peter 3:21. Baptists read that verse as saying the water pictures salvation, and that the pledge and the resurrection are where it lies.

[read] Peter says "Repent and be baptized, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins" in Acts 2:38, and Ananias says "be baptized, and wash your sins away, calling on His name" in Acts 22:16. Baptists read these beside Acts 10:43–47, where the Spirit falls on believers before any water, and take them as speaking of faith and its first public act together, since Acts shows baptism following belief closely. Others read them as giving the water a part in forgiveness. Baptists call baptism "an act of obedience symbolizing the believer's faith".

[source:luther] Luther's Small Catechism, a Lutheran teaching, says of baptism that "It worketh forgiveness of sins" and goes on to speak of those who believe it. It is shown here as theirs.

## Question supper: What happens at the Lord's table?

Text: 1 Corinthians 11:23–26

[read] This site holds with Baptists that the Supper is "a symbolic act of obedience". Jesus says "do this in remembrance of Me" in 1 Corinthians 11:24, and the bread is still called "this bread" after He has said it is His body, in 1 Corinthians 11:26. Baptists read His words over the bread as a picture that means what it says, as they read other sayings of His. Some Baptists, reading "a participation in the blood of Christ" in 1 Corinthians 10:16, speak of the Lord's nearness to those who come in faith, and others hold to remembrance alone. Article 7 says the Supper memorializes His death and anticipates His return, and no more. Neither does this page.

[source:trent] The Council of Trent, a Roman Catholic council, teaches that after the consecration Christ "is truly, really, and substantially contained" under the appearances of bread and wine.

[source:luther] Luther's Small Catechism, a Lutheran teaching, calls it "the true body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ, under the bread and wine".

[source:wcf] The Westminster Confession, chapter 29, Presbyterian and Reformed, says worthy receivers "spiritually, receive and feed upon Christ crucified", and that His body and blood are "not corporally or carnally in, with, or under the bread and wine; yet as really, but spiritually, present to the faith of believers".

[source:barnes] What about "without recognizing the body" in 1 Corinthians 11:29? Barnes reads it as failing to tell the Supper apart from any meal, "between the bread which is used on this occasion, and common and ordinary food".

[read] This study also hears the church in that word, because of 1 Corinthians 10:17 and of the hungry being shamed in that very chapter. That is this study's reading, no writer holding it was read for this page, and both may be true together.

## Question pastors: Who may be a pastor?

Text: 1 Timothy 2:12

[read] This site holds with the Baptist Faith and Message: "While both men and women are gifted for service in the church, the office of pastor/elder/overseer is limited to men as qualified by Scripture". The grounds are in 1 Timothy 2:12 and in the overseer as "the husband of but one wife" in 1 Timothy 3:2.

[read] The same New Testament honours women's service plainly. Phoebe is "a servant of the church in Cenchrea" in Romans 16:1. Priscilla, with Aquila, "explained to him the way of God more accurately" in Acts 18:26. Women pray and prophesy in 1 Corinthians 11:5. Paul's words are plain, "Women are to be silent in the churches" in 1 Corinthians 14:34, and how they sit beside 1 Corinthians 11:5 is taken up below.

[source:barnes] Barnes, who is not a Baptist, takes 1 Corinthians 14:34 strictly: "This rule is positive: explicit and universal". On 1 Corinthians 11:5 he answers that women who prophesied were inspired, so "their example is no warrant now" for public teaching without inspiration.

[open] How exactly to fit 1 Corinthians 14:34–35 with 1 Corinthians 11:5 is a question careful readers answer in more than one way, some taking it of the weighing of prophecy just before it in 1 Corinthians 14:29. This page does not settle it. Other churches, some of them called Baptist, ordain women. Their reasons are not surveyed here.

## Question govern: Who decides things in a church?

Text: Matthew 18:15–17

[read] The Baptist answer is the congregation, under Christ. Article 6 of the Baptist Faith and Message says each congregation "operates under the Lordship of Christ through democratic processes". The reason is what the churches in the New Testament did. Jesus says "tell it to the church" in Matthew 18:17. The whole group chose the seven in Acts 6:5. The decision in Acts 15:22 was made by "the apostles and elders, with the whole church". Corinth acted when "assembled" in 1 Corinthians 5:4, and a penalty there was "imposed on him by the majority" in 2 Corinthians 2:6.

[read] That is no licence for a church without leaders, because Hebrews 13:17 tells believers to obey them. Congregations and elders are both in these texts. Baptist churches arrange the two in more than one way, and the confession leaves room for this.

[source:wcf] The Westminster Confession, chapter 31, Presbyterian and Reformed, gives this work to assemblies of ministers: "It belongeth to synods and councils" to settle controversies. It adds that these "may err, and many have erred".

[source:lbc] The Second London Baptist Confession, chapter 26, is older Baptist teaching, in which those who join a church are "under the Censures and Government thereof, according to the Rule of Christ".

## Close: Learning to read together

Close: You have read one word in all its sizes, six readings, ten baptisms and six old phrases.
Way: Here is the way of reading to carry to the next passage. Find who was in the room, usually in the letter's first lines. Read the word you as all of them, unless the passage says otherwise. Then ask what this means for us, before you ask what it means for me. Faith is still your own, and no one believes for you. But most of these lines were never meant to be heard alone.
Invite: If you have not yet trusted Christ, none of this begins with joining anything. It begins with Him. Turn from your sin and trust Him as Lord, because "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord". Then find a church, and ask to be baptized.
Question: Who in your church would be poorer if you stopped coming, and whose part are you still treating as one you can do without?
Again: Read the passage again before you answer.
