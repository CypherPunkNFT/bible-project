# The church and its calling

**Study 28 · God and Christian Faith · First content draft · 2026-10-13**

The New Testament calls the church Christ's body, God's household, and a spiritual house. These descriptions concern people brought together through Christ, given gifts for service, and called to worship, learn, and bear witness. They explain both belonging and responsibility.

The readings move from the community's identity to its shared life: gathering for edification, receiving care from leaders, practising baptism and the Lord's Supper, and proclaiming Christ. Christian traditions organise some of these practices differently. Begin with what the passages say, then distinguish their words from the fuller arrangements traditions build from them.

## 01 One body and many members

**Main readings:** 1 Corinthians 12:12–31; Ephesians 4:1–16.

### Members in particular

Paul compares the church to a body whose different members belong together. A foot does not cease to belong because it is not a hand; an ear does not become unnecessary because it is not an eye. Difference of function does not remove membership.

The comparison also challenges the member who claims not to need another. Those who seem weaker are necessary, and those thought less honourable receive particular care. The body is arranged so that its members care for one another rather than compete to prove their independence.

> Now ye are the body of Christ, and members in particular.

1 Corinthians 12:27 · KJV

The surrounding verses describe shared suffering and rejoicing. Belonging has consequences when another person is hurt or honoured. The passage gives neither the prominent member permission to dismiss others nor the less visible member a reason to conclude that there is no place for them.

### The edifying of the body of Christ

Ephesians begins its appeal for unity with humility, gentleness, patience, and love. One body, Spirit, hope, Lord, faith, baptism, and God and Father form the setting for the gifts Christ gives.

The named ministries serve the equipping of the saints and the building up of the body. Their purpose includes maturity, knowledge of the Son of God, and stability against deceptive teaching. Growth is directed toward Christ, who is the head.

The closing picture includes every part contributing to the body's growth in love. The community's work is therefore larger than the activity of its most visible teachers. Teaching serves a people who are themselves being equipped to minister.

**Consider:** Which two mistaken attitudes does Paul's body illustration challenge? What outcome does Ephesians name for the gifts Christ gives?

**Reflect:** Consider a contribution that receives little attention in your community. How might recognising its value change your understanding of the whole body's work?

## 02 Of the household of God

**Main readings:** Ephesians 2:11–22; 1 Peter 2:4–10.

### No more strangers and foreigners

Ephesians addresses Gentiles who were once far off and says that they have been brought near by Christ's blood. Christ is their peace, making both one and reconciling both to God through the cross. The passage names shared access by one Spirit to the Father.

> Now therefore ye are no more strangers and foreigners, but fellowcitizens with the saints, and of the household of God;

Ephesians 2:19 · KJV

The movement is from alienation to belonging. The people addressed do not remain guests whose standing depends on being tolerated by a more entitled group. They are described as fellow citizens and members of God's household.

The image then becomes a building founded on the apostles and prophets, with Christ as the chief cornerstone. The whole grows as a holy temple and a dwelling of God through the Spirit. The building language describes the people being joined together in Christ.

### A spiritual house

First Peter likewise speaks of coming to Christ as a living stone and being built as living stones into a spiritual house. The people are called a holy priesthood offering spiritual sacrifices acceptable to God through Jesus Christ.

Christ's position remains central: he is chosen and precious, though rejected by people. The community's identity is received in relation to him. Peter's language of a chosen generation, royal priesthood, holy nation, and God's own people leads directly to declaring the praises of the one who called them into light.

The next verse recalls mercy: those once not a people are now God's people. Their shared identity provides a reason for gratitude and witness. It does not make them the source of their own standing or an excuse for contempt toward others.

**Consider:** What changes between the beginning and end of Ephesians 2? What purpose accompanies the descriptions of God's people in 1 Peter?

**Reflect:** Consider whether your understanding of church begins with a place or programme, or with the people Christ brings together for God.

## 03 Let all things be done unto edifying

**Main readings:** 1 Corinthians 14:1–19, 26–40. **Compare:** 1 Corinthians 11:17–22.

### That the church may receive edifying

Paul discusses speech, prayer, song, and spiritual gifts by asking whether others understand and are built up. His illustrations include musical instruments and a trumpet whose signal must be recognisable. Sound alone does not communicate what the hearer needs.

He applies the same concern to prayer and thanksgiving: someone who does not understand cannot meaningfully join in the response. In the gathering, he prefers a few understandable words that teach others to many words they cannot understand.

The question is communal. A participant's experience is not the only measure of what should happen when the church assembles. The good of those hearing and learning matters to the use of a gift.

### Decently and in order

The chapter gives directions about speakers taking turns, interpretation, and weighing what is said. It limits speech in particular situations so that the gathering can serve learning and comfort rather than confusion.

> Let all things be done decently and in order.

1 Corinthians 14:40 · KJV

Order here serves the community's edification. The paragraph does not supply a complete service schedule for every later congregation. It does establish that spiritual claims and enthusiasm are not a reason to disregard whether the gathering helps other people.

The chapter also contains disputed instructions about women's speech. Those require reading alongside other relevant passages and distinguishing the kinds of speech under discussion; they should not be silently resolved by a general statement that everyone contributes. The present focus is the repeated test of intelligibility, judgment, and edification.

First Corinthians 11 adds another necessary test: a gathering can make matters worse when divisions and humiliation govern it. Assembling in one place is not, by itself, evidence that the community is honouring its calling.

**Consider:** Why does Paul repeatedly ask whether others understand? What does order accomplish within his argument?

**Reflect:** Consider a part of a gathering that newcomers find difficult to understand. What explanation or change could help them participate meaningfully?

## 04 Feed the flock of God

**Main readings:** 1 Timothy 3:1–16; 1 Peter 5:1–4.

### Take care of the church of God

First Timothy describes the work of a bishop through qualifications that include sobriety, hospitality, ability to teach, patience, and a good reputation. Violence, greed, and pride are treated as dangers, not as acceptable costs of forceful leadership.

The passage connects household conduct with care for the church. It also requires that deacons be tested and trustworthy, holding the faith with a pure conscience. The concern reaches beyond public speaking to the person's conduct and reliability.

These instructions serve the stated purpose of knowing how to behave in God's house, described as the church of the living God and the pillar and ground of the truth. A community's teaching and the character of those entrusted with responsibility belong together.

### Ensamples to the flock

Peter addresses elders as a fellow elder and directs them to feed God's flock willingly, without dishonest gain. He then specifies the manner of their oversight:

> Neither as being lords over God’s heritage, but being ensamples to the flock.

1 Peter 5:3 · KJV

The flock belongs to God, and the leaders serve under the chief Shepherd. Their responsibility is real, but the passage excludes domination as its proper expression. Example is part of the work itself.

Christian arrangements for church government differ. The Baptist Faith and Message describes autonomous congregations governed through democratic processes under Christ. The Westminster Confession provides for synods and councils to address church government, controversies, and complaints, while acknowledging that councils can err. See [Baptist Faith and Message VI](https://bfm.sbc.net/bfm2000/) and [Westminster chapter 31](https://opc.org/wcf.html).

These two arrangements do not exhaust Christian practice. Whatever structure is being examined, the readings give concrete questions about trustworthy conduct, care, teaching, and the refusal to dominate.

**Consider:** Which qualifications concern character rather than public ability? How does belonging to God shape the way the flock should be led?

**Reflect:** Consider how a community can recognise patient, trustworthy service rather than confusing prominence with qualification.

## 05 Baptizing and remembering

**Main readings:** Matthew 28:16–20; 1 Corinthians 11:17–34.

### In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost

The risen Jesus commissions the eleven to teach all nations, baptizing in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, and teaching obedience to his commands. Baptism belongs within a commission grounded in his authority and accompanied by his presence.

Christian interpretations differ about its recipients and meaning. The Baptist Faith and Message specifies a believer's immersion as an act of obedience symbolising faith in Christ. Westminster includes professing believers and the infants of believing parents, describing baptism as a covenant sign and seal. See [Baptist Faith and Message VII](https://bfm.sbc.net/bfm2000/) and [Westminster chapter 28](https://opc.org/wcf.html).

Those positions involve wider biblical arguments than the wording of this commission alone. Here the command itself joins baptism to teaching and a life of obedience to Christ, rather than leaving it as an isolated act with no continuing formation.

### Ye do shew the Lord's death

Paul recalls Jesus' words over the bread and cup in correcting the Corinthians' divided meal. Some have plenty while others go hungry; those who have nothing are shamed. Against that conduct he sets the meaning of the shared practice:

> For as often as ye eat this bread, and drink this cup, ye do shew the Lord’s death till he come.

1 Corinthians 11:26 · KJV

The passage includes remembrance, proclamation, self-examination, judgment, and waiting for one another. Its warning about unworthy participation must be read with the behaviour Paul confronts, not reduced to a vague demand to feel personally deserving of grace.

The Baptist statement describes the Supper as a symbolic memorial and anticipation of Christ's return. Westminster also speaks of believers spiritually receiving and feeding on Christ by faith. See [article VII](https://bfm.sbc.net/bfm2000/) and [chapter 29.7](https://opc.org/wcf.html). These are two formulations, not a complete comparison of sacramental teaching. Paul's immediate correction requires the community to honour the meal's meaning in how it treats those gathered.

**Consider:** What accompanies baptism in Matthew's commission? What conduct makes the Corinthian meal contradict its stated meaning?

**Reflect:** Consider how remembering Christ's self-giving should affect the welcome and care shown at a shared table.

## 06 Shew forth his praises

**Main readings:** 1 Peter 2:9–12; Matthew 28:16–20; Acts 1:6–8.

### Out of darkness into his marvellous light

Peter's descriptions of God's people lead to a purpose: declaring the praises of the one who called them out of darkness. Their witness begins with what God has done and the mercy they have received.

He then calls them strangers and pilgrims and urges them to abstain from desires that war against the soul. Their conduct among Gentiles is to be honourable, so that even those speaking against them may see their good works and glorify God.

The people who speak of God's mercy are also called to live in a way consistent with it. Good conduct does not replace proclamation, and proclamation does not make conduct irrelevant. Peter places both within the community's calling.

### Ye shall be witnesses unto me

In Acts, the disciples ask about the restoration of the kingdom to Israel. Jesus directs them away from knowing the times fixed by the Father and toward the power and task they will receive:

> But ye shall receive power, after that the Holy Ghost is come upon you: and ye shall be witnesses unto me both in Jerusalem, and in all Judæa, and in Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth.

Acts 1:8 · KJV

The promise is first addressed to those disciples in that setting. It establishes the outward movement of witness that Acts goes on to narrate. Matthew's commission likewise joins the nations, teaching, baptism, obedience, and Christ's presence.

The church's calling therefore extends beyond maintaining its own gatherings. The body is built up in Christ and sent to speak of him. Its shared life should make room for teaching, worship, care, and witness as connected responsibilities of a people who have received mercy.

**Consider:** How does Peter connect identity, speech, and conduct? What task does Jesus give the disciples instead of a timetable?

**Reflect:** Consider one way your community's shared life can support faithful witness beyond its familiar circle.
