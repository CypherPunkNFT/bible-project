# Chosen and kept: can I be sure?

Concepts: Chosen · Kept · Known

Motto: Two hands. One grip. / No one snatches.

Lead: Some people come to these chapters curious. Many more come afraid: afraid they were never chosen, afraid they have drifted too far, afraid of one sentence Jesus spoke about a sin that will never be forgiven. This study walks the passages that answer those fears. It also teaches one habit: before you ask whether a verse is about you, ask what it was written to do.

> I have written these things to you who believe in the name of the Son of God, so that you may know that you have eternal life.

1 John 5:13 · BSB

**The habit.** Every passage in this study was written for a purpose, and the purposes differ. Some were written to **praise**: Paul sings about being chosen before he explains anything. Some were written to **comfort**: Jesus speaks about His sheep to people who need to know they are safe. Some were written to **warn**: Hebrews shakes readers who are drifting. Some were written to **answer an objection**: Paul argues with a voice that says God is unfair. A song is not a sorting machine. A warning is not a verdict. A promise is not a riddle. Read each one as what it is.

**A few words first.** *Chosen* is the Bible's word for what Christians call **election**: God's choosing of people to belong to Him. *Kept* is about **perseverance**: the teaching that those who truly belong to Christ will continue in faith to the end, because God holds them. *Sure* is about **assurance**: a believer's settled confidence that they belong to God. Other church words are explained where they first come up.

**This is a selection, not a census.** The study walks eight passages: Ephesians 1:3–14, Romans 8:28–39, Romans 9:6–24, John 6:35–44, John 10:27–30, Hebrews 6:4–12, 1 John 5:1–13 and Mark 3:28–30. They were chosen because each is a main place Scripture speaks about being chosen, being kept or being sure, and because readers bring their hardest questions to them. The study does not walk every verse on these subjects, and it does not survey every Christian tradition. Where Baptists themselves differ, as they do on the details of election, it shows the views and names people who hold them. Where the site's confession speaks, as it does on the believer being kept, the study says so plainly.

The site writes in agreement with the Baptist Faith and Message 2000, the statement of faith of the Southern Baptist Convention. Its article V (God's purpose of grace) covers this whole study. A confession is a church's written summary of what it believes from Scripture. Baptists hold that it serves Scripture and never stands over it.

## 01 Chosen in Christ: a song before it is an argument

**Main readings:** Ephesians 1:3–14

*Hook:* Paul does not open with a debate about choosing. He opens with a song of praise.

In Greek, Paul's original language, Ephesians 1:3–14 runs as one long sentence of praise. It opens with "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ" (Ephesians 1:3), and three times it comes back to the same refrain, "to the praise of His glory" (Ephesians 1:6, 12, 14). This is worship. Read it first as worship, before you turn it into a puzzle.

> For He chose us in Him before the foundation of the world to be holy and blameless in His presence. In love He predestined us for adoption as His sons through Jesus Christ, according to the good pleasure of His will,

Ephesians 1:4–5 · BSB

Look at what the sentence says.

- **When:** "before the foundation of the world". God's choosing was not a reaction to how anyone turned out.
- **Where:** "in Him", in Christ. That phrase, or one like it, comes again and again in these verses. Nobody is chosen apart from Jesus.
- **What for:** "to be holy and blameless". Election aims at a changed life. It is never a licence to live carelessly.
- **Why:** "in love" and "according to the good pleasure of His will". *Predestined* means decided beforehand. *Adoption* means being taken into God's family as His own children.

The passage goes on to **grace**, God's kindness given to people who have not earned it and cannot repay it. God has "freely given" grace "in the Beloved One" (Ephesians 1:6). In Christ "we have redemption through His blood, the forgiveness of our trespasses" (Ephesians 1:7). *Redemption* means being set free at a price. *Trespasses* are sins, wrongs done against God.

Then the sentence reaches its readers:

> And in Him, having heard and believed the word of truth—the gospel of your salvation—you were sealed with the promised Holy Spirit, who is the pledge of our inheritance until the redemption of those who are God’s possession, to the praise of His glory.

Ephesians 1:13–14 · BSB

The **gospel** is the good news about Jesus: who He is, what He did in His death and rising, and what God freely gives to those who trust Him. The Ephesians "heard and believed" it. Then they were "sealed". A seal marked something as belonging to its owner. A *pledge* is a first payment that guarantees the rest is coming. The Father chooses, the Son redeems, and the Spirit seals and guarantees. All three are at work in one sentence.

**What it does not say.** The passage never tells anyone to work out whether they are chosen before they may believe. It was written to people who had already "heard and believed", so that they would praise God for a salvation that began before they did. Baptists read the words "chose us in Him" in two main ways. Some read them as God's choosing of particular people. Others read them as God's choosing of a people in Christ, which a person joins by faith. Both readings are set out in part 03.

[care] If you are worried that you may not be chosen, notice where this passage sends you. It does not send you to search for a hidden list. It sends you to Christ, "in Him", and to the gospel the Ephesians heard and believed. That gospel is offered to you too.

**Consider:** What purpose does verse 4 give for God's choosing? What did the Ephesians do in verse 13 before they were sealed?

**Reflect:** When you think about being chosen, does it lead you to praise, as it led Paul, or to anxiety? What would change if you read it first as a song?

## 02 A chain with no broken link

**Main readings:** Romans 8:28–39

*Hook:* Five links, from before time to glory. Then Paul asks who could break them.

Romans 8 is Paul's long account of life in the Spirit. Its first half belongs to *The Holy Spirit* study, and its middle, on creation groaning and waiting, belongs to *The hope to come*. Here Paul gathers everything into a single chain:

> For those God foreknew, He also predestined to be conformed to the image of His Son, so that He would be the firstborn among many brothers. And those He predestined, He also called; those He called, He also justified; those He justified, He also glorified.

Romans 8:29–30 · BSB

There are five links: foreknew, predestined, called, justified, glorified. To be **justified** is to be declared right with God, forgiven and accepted, because of Christ and not because of our own record. To be **glorified** is to be made finally complete and free of sin when Christ returns. Paul writes even that last link in the past tense: "He also glorified." It has not happened yet, but in God's purpose it is as good as done. Nobody who enters at the first link falls out before the last.

Verse 28 is often quoted on its own: "God works all things together for the good of those who love Him." Look at what it promises. It does not say all things *are* good. A few verses later Paul lists trouble, persecution, famine and the sword (Romans 8:35). The promise is that God works in all of these. The "good" is named in the next verse: to be "conformed to the image of His Son".

**What "foreknew" means.** Baptists read this word in two ways. Many Calvinist Baptists read it as God setting His love on people beforehand, as Scripture sometimes uses "know" for a close relationship. Many non-Calvinist Baptists read it as God knowing in advance who would believe. Both readings agree with article II of the confession, which holds that God's knowledge reaches all things, including the future free choices of His creatures. Part 03 names who holds each view.

Then come the questions. "If God is for us, who can be against us?" (Romans 8:31). "Who will bring any charge against God’s elect? It is God who justifies" (Romans 8:33). The judge has already ruled. "Christ Jesus, who died, and more than that was raised to life, is at the right hand of God—and He is interceding for us" (Romans 8:34). *Interceding* means speaking to God on someone's behalf.

> For I am convinced that neither death nor life, neither angels nor principalities, neither the present nor the future, nor any powers, neither height nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God that is in Christ Jesus our Lord.

Romans 8:38–39 · BSB

**What it does not say.** This is not a promise of an easy life. Paul quotes Psalm 44: "For Your sake we face death all day long" (Romans 8:36). The comfort is that suffering, however real, cannot cut a believer off from God's love. Notice too that "anything else in all creation" includes you.

**Consider:** Which of the five links in verses 29–30 depends on you holding on? Which dangers does Paul name in verses 35–39, and what does he say about each?

**Reflect:** What are you afraid could separate you from God? Find where it fits in Paul's list.

## 03 Mercy that does not depend on us

**Main readings:** Romans 9:6–24

*Hook:* Paul grieves for his own people, then asks whether God's word has failed.

**Setting.** This chapter comes out of grief. Paul, a Jew, says he has "great sorrow and unceasing anguish" for his own people, Israel, most of whom had not received Jesus as their Messiah (Romans 9:1–5). In Jewish Scripture and prayer, Israel is the people God chose and bound Himself to by **covenant**, a binding promise between God and His people. So Paul faces a hard question: has God's promise to Israel failed? His answer runs through Romans 9 to 11 and ends in hope for Israel (Romans 11). Nothing in this chapter blames the Jewish people, and it must never be read that way. Jesus and His first followers were Jews, and Paul writes as one who loves his kin.

"It is not as though God’s word has failed. For not all who are descended from Israel are Israel" (Romans 9:6). Paul gives examples from Israel's own story. The line of promise ran through Isaac, not through every son of Abraham (Romans 9:7–9). Then came the twins:

> Yet before the twins were born or had done anything good or bad, in order that God’s plan of election might stand, not by works but by Him who calls, she was told, “The older will serve the younger.”

Romans 9:11–12 · BSB

Paul quotes Malachi: "Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated" (Romans 9:13). Many readers explain "hated" as a Hebrew way of saying "chose the one and not the other". Paul then answers an objection, "Is God unjust? Certainly not!" (Romans 9:14), from God's words to Moses: "I will have mercy on whom I have mercy" (Romans 9:15). "So then, it does not depend on man’s desire or effort, but on God’s mercy" (Romans 9:16). He speaks of Pharaoh, and then he hears the objection again: "Then why does God still find fault? For who can resist His will?" (Romans 9:19). He answers with a potter and his clay (Romans 9:20–21).

### Two Baptist readings

On this chapter, and on election as a whole, Southern Baptists hold two main readings. Both stand within article V. The confession calls election "the gracious purpose of God, according to which He regenerates, justifies, sanctifies, and glorifies sinners", says it "is consistent with the free agency of man", and says it "excludes boasting and promotes humility". (*Regenerates* means gives new spiritual life; *sanctifies* means makes holy over time.) The confession does not settle the details. This site does not choose between the two.

**The Calvinist reading** is named after John Calvin, the sixteenth-century Reformer in Geneva. On this reading God chose particular people for salvation before the world began, out of mercy alone and not because He foresaw anything in them. Romans 9:11–16 is read as being about individuals and their salvation. God's choice comes first, and faith is His gift to those He chose. The Abstract of Principles (1858), the founding confession of the Southern Baptist Theological Seminary, puts it this way: "Election is God's eternal choice of some persons unto everlasting life—not because of foreseen merit in them, but of His mere mercy in Christ" [check: wording against the site's copy of the Abstract of Principles]. Southern Baptists who hold this reading today include R. Albert Mohler Jr., president of that seminary; Thomas R. Schreiner, whose commentary on Romans argues it at length [check: series and year, Baker Exegetical Commentary, 1998]; and Tom Ascol of Founders Ministries.

**The non-Calvinist reading** takes several forms. Some who hold it call it the "traditional" Southern Baptist view. On this reading God's choosing is in Christ, and people come to share in it through faith, which God foresees and enables but does not compel. Romans 9 is read as God's freedom to choose the line through which He works in history (Isaac, Jacob, even Pharaoh) and His freedom to set the terms of mercy. Those terms are faith, not ancestry or effort, as Paul says at the chapter's end (Romans 9:30–32). Readers on this side point out that Genesis speaks of Jacob and Esau as "two nations" (Genesis 25:23), and that Malachi speaks of Edom, the people descended from Esau. Southern Baptists who hold this reading include Eric Hankins, chief drafter of "A Statement of the Traditional Southern Baptist Understanding of God's Plan of Salvation" (2012); David L. Allen and Steve W. Lemke, editors of *Whosoever Will* [check: publisher and year, B&H Academic, 2010]; Kenneth Keathley, author of *Salvation and Sovereignty* [check: year, 2010]; and, from an earlier generation, Herschel H. Hobbs, who chaired the committee that wrote the 1963 confession [check: that Hobbs is fairly described as non-Calvinist].

**What both readings hold.** Salvation is by grace, through faith, in Christ alone. No one earns it. The gospel is to be offered freely to everyone, and article XI (evangelism and missions) calls every believer to make disciples of all nations. True believers are kept to the end. In 2013 a committee appointed by the convention's Executive Committee asked Southern Baptists on both sides to hold these things together in trust [check: the report's title, "Truth, Trust, and Testimony in a Time of Tension", and who appointed the committee].

### Two questions readers ask

**Does God choose some people for hell?** Read verse 22 closely: "What if God … bore with great patience the vessels of His wrath, prepared for destruction?" Paul frames it as a "what if", and he speaks of God's "great patience". Some readers note that the vessels of mercy are ones God "prepared in advance for glory" (Romans 9:23), while verse 22 does not say who prepared the others. Exodus says that God hardened Pharaoh's heart, and also that Pharaoh hardened his own heart (Exodus 8:15). Article V speaks of election as a purpose of grace. It does not speak of a decree to condemn. Baptists explain verse 22 in more than one way, and this page does not settle it. What it does say is that no one who comes to Christ is turned away (part 04).

**If God chooses, why preach or pray?** Article V says election "comprehends all the means in connection with the end". In other words, God's purpose includes the preaching and the hearing by which people come to faith. The Ephesians were chosen, and they still "heard and believed" (Ephesians 1:13). Both Baptist readings preach the gospel to everyone.

**Consider:** What question is Paul answering in verse 6? Whose mercy does verse 16 say salvation depends on?

**Reflect:** If your salvation does not depend on your "desire or effort", what does that free you from? What does it ask of you?

## 04 Kept in two hands

**Main readings:** John 6:35–44; John 10:27–30

*Hook:* "I will never drive away." "No one can snatch." Jesus speaks two promises in two different crowds.

**Setting.** John 6 follows the feeding of the five thousand in Galilee. The crowd wants more bread, and Jesus offers Himself: "I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to Me will never hunger" (John 6:35). When John writes that "the Jews began to grumble" (John 6:41), he means that crowd, in that place. Jesus and His disciples were Jews too. The verse is not a statement about a whole people.

> Everyone the Father gives Me will come to Me, and the one who comes to Me I will never drive away.

John 6:37 · BSB

The verse has two halves. The first looks from God's side: those the Father gives will come. The second looks from yours: anyone who comes will never be turned away. Then Jesus says what the Father wants: "that I shall lose none of those He has given Me, but raise them up at the last day" (John 6:39), and "that everyone who looks to the Son and believes in Him shall have eternal life" (John 6:40). Losing even one would mean the Son failed to do the Father's will.

"No one can come to Me unless the Father who sent Me draws him" (John 6:44). Calvinist Baptists read this drawing as God's work that surely brings a person to Christ. Non-Calvinist Baptists read it alongside Jesus' later words, "I … will draw all men to Myself" (John 12:32), as God's drawing of everyone, which a person can resist. Both agree that no one comes to Christ by their own power alone.

In John 10 Jesus calls Himself the Shepherd:

> My sheep listen to My voice; I know them, and they follow Me. I give them eternal life, and they will never perish. No one can snatch them out of My hand. My Father who has given them to Me is greater than all. No one can snatch them out of My Father’s hand. I and the Father are one.

John 10:27–30 · BSB

The believer is held in the Son's hand, and that hand is inside the Father's hand. "Never perish" is as strong a word as Jesus could choose. "I and the Father are one" belongs to *Who do you say that I am?*, which reads what this verse says about who Jesus is.

**What the confession says.** Here the site speaks plainly, because article V does. "All true believers endure to the end. Those whom God has accepted in Christ, and sanctified by His Spirit, will never fall away from the state of grace, but shall persevere to the end." Older Baptists said the same. The Second London Baptist Confession (1677/1689), chapter 17, says such believers "can neither totally nor finally fall from the state of grace" [check: spelling against the site's copy]. Paul's own confidence was this:

> being confident of this, that He who began a good work in you will carry it on to completion until the day of Christ Jesus.

Philippians 1:6 · BSB

**What it does not say.** Being kept does not mean never sinning. Article V adds that believers "may fall into sin through neglect and temptation", grieving the Spirit and losing comfort, "yet they shall be kept by the power of God through faith unto salvation". Peter denied Jesus three times, and Jesus restored him (John 21:15–19). Notice also that the sheep "listen" and "follow". That is not a fee they pay to stay in the hand. It describes what sheep in that hand do.

**Consider:** In John 6:37, what does Jesus promise the one who comes? In John 10:28–29, whose hands hold the sheep, and who is able to take them out?

**Reflect:** Do you think of your salvation more as your grip on God or as God's grip on you? Which do these verses describe?

## 05 Can I lose my salvation?

**Main readings:** Hebrews 6:4–12; quoted, 1 John 2:19

*Hook:* The sternest warning in the New Testament ends with the words "we are convinced of better things in your case".

**The site's answer.** No. With article V, this site holds that those God has truly accepted in Christ will never finally fall away. The reasons are the promises in parts 02 and 04. *A new creation* links here for this question.

That answer has to face the hardest text honestly.

> It is impossible for those who have once been enlightened, who have tasted the heavenly gift, who have shared in the Holy Spirit, who have tasted the goodness of the word of God and the powers of the coming age—and then have fallen away—to be restored to repentance, because they themselves are crucifying the Son of God all over again and subjecting Him to open shame.

Hebrews 6:4–6 · BSB

**Setting.** Hebrews was written to believers under pressure. They had suffered for their faith (Hebrews 10:32–34) and were tempted to drift back from Christ. *Repentance* means turning from sin to God, a change of mind and of direction. The writer pictures two fields, both rained on. One bears a useful crop. The other bears only thorns (Hebrews 6:7–8).

Christians read these verses in four main ways:

1. **People near Christ, but never truly joined to Him.** They tasted, shared and saw, as a field drinks rain, but never bore the fruit of new life. Verse 9 sets them apart from "things that accompany salvation". John Calvin, in his commentary on Hebrews, and the Puritan John Owen, in his long exposition of the letter, both read the passage along these lines [check: confirm both commentaries' treatment of 6:4–6]. Many Calvinist Baptists follow them.
2. **A real warning that God uses to keep His people.** On this reading the warning addresses true believers. It is one of the means by which God makes sure they do not fall. Thomas R. Schreiner and Ardel B. Caneday argue this in *The Race Set Before Us* [check: publisher and year, InterVarsity Press, 2001].
3. **A warning about what would follow if apostasy were possible.** *Apostasy* means a deliberate, final abandoning of the faith. Some read the passage as saying: if someone could fall away, there would be no second sacrifice to return to. They take it as describing what that would mean, not as saying it happens.
4. **A real fall by a real believer.** This is the view of Christians in the Wesleyan and Arminian tradition. Arminian is named after Jacobus Arminius, a Dutch theologian who died in 1609. Wesleyan is named after John Wesley, the eighteenth-century founder of Methodism. In their own words, from the Methodist Articles of Religion, article 12: "Not every sin willingly committed after justification is the sin against the Holy Ghost, and unpardonable. … After we have received the Holy Ghost, we may depart from grace given, and fall into sin, and, by the grace of God, rise again and amend our lives" [check: wording and punctuation against a current Methodist Book of Discipline]. Free Will Baptists, who are outside the Southern Baptist Convention, also hold that a true believer can finally fall away. Many of them say it happens through a turning from faith, not through sin as such, and read Hebrews 6 as saying such a fall cannot be undone, which differs from the Methodist "rise again" [check: against the Free Will Baptist Treatise of the Faith and Practices, and Robert Picirilli, *Grace, Faith, Free Will*, 2002]. These are their views, shown as theirs. They are not the view of the confession this site writes under.

The first two readings are the ones most common among Southern Baptists, and the passage itself gives them weight. It goes straight on: "Even though we speak like this, beloved, we are convinced of better things in your case" (Hebrews 6:9). It then gives a reason: "God is not unjust. He will not forget your work and the love you have shown" (Hebrews 6:10).

John speaks of people who once sat in his churches and then left:

> They went out from us, but they did not belong to us. For if they had belonged to us, they would have remained with us. But their departure made it clear that none of them belonged to us.

1 John 2:19 · BSB

Read this way, a final falling away does not show that a believer lost salvation. It shows that someone who looked like a believer never truly had it.

**What this does not license.** "Once saved, always saved" is true when it means that God keeps those who are truly His. It is false when it is used to mean that how you live does not matter. Hebrews asks each reader "to show this same diligence to the very end, in order to make your hope sure" (Hebrews 6:11). Being kept by God and keeping on go together.

[care] If you fear you have fallen away and you want to come back, look at the people verse 6 describes. They are "crucifying the Son of God all over again". They are not grieving over their sin and longing for Christ. A desire to return is not what this passage describes. The promise in John 6:37 still stands: "the one who comes to Me I will never drive away." A pastor can talk this through with you.

**Consider:** What contrast do the two fields in verses 7–8 draw? What does the writer say in verse 9 about his readers?

**Reflect:** Is your fear of falling away driving you toward Christ or away from Him? What would diligence "to the very end" look like for you this week?

## 06 So that you may know

**Main readings:** 1 John 5:1–13

*Hook:* John says why he wrote his letter, and the reason is so that you may know.

**Setting.** John writes to churches shaken by people who had left them (1 John 2:19). He has given his readers tests to tell true faith from false: believing that Jesus is the Christ, loving God's children, keeping God's commands (1 John 5:1–3). Read as checklists, these tests can turn into fear. Ask what John wrote them *to do*. He tells you in verse 13: "so that you may know that you have eternal life."

**Assurance rests first on what God has said.**

> And this is that testimony: God has given us eternal life, and this life is in His Son. Whoever has the Son has life; whoever does not have the Son of God does not have life.

1 John 5:11–12 · BSB

"The testimony of God is greater" than any human witness (1 John 5:9). Assurance does not begin with how strong your feelings are, or how good last week was. It begins with God's word about His Son. Eternal life is not a thing you hold apart from Jesus. It "is in His Son". So the question is not "How much do I feel?" but "Do I have the Son?"

**The Spirit and a changed life confirm it.** "It is the Spirit who testifies" (1 John 5:6). "Whoever believes in the Son of God has this testimony within him" (1 John 5:10). Love for God's children and keeping His commands are signs of new birth (1 John 5:1–2). They are not the price of it. And "His commandments are not burdensome" (1 John 5:3).

"Water and blood" in verse 6 is read in more than one way. Many readers take it as Jesus' baptism at the start of His ministry and His death on the cross: He truly came as Son of God and truly died. This study does not depend on settling it. The Spirit, the water and the blood all point to the same Son.

**What it does not promise.** Assurance is not the same as never doubting. Article V says believers who fall into sin can "impair their graces and comforts", which means they can lose their sense of peace for a time without losing God. Assurance also does not rest on a past decision alone. The verses speak in the present tense: "believes", "has the Son". If you are unsure, the answer is not to dig up a moment from years ago. It is to come to Christ now. *The good news* sets out how.

**Consider:** What does verse 13 say John's purpose was? According to verses 11–12, where is eternal life found?

**Reflect:** When you doubt, where do you look first: at your feelings, at your record, or at what God has said about His Son?

## 07 Have I committed the unforgivable sin?

**Main readings:** Mark 3:28–30

[care] If this question is keeping you awake, or if fear and guilt have grown so heavy that you have thought of harming yourself, please tell someone today. In the United States you can call or text 988, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline [check before publishing]. In Spain, call 024 [check before publishing]. In Mexico, call the Línea de la Vida, 800 911 2000 [check before publishing]. In other Spanish-speaking countries, call your local emergency number or national crisis line [check before publishing: add a line for each country the site serves]. In Brazil, call the CVV on 188 [check before publishing]. These feelings, however strong, do pass, and people come through them. Talking to a pastor you trust, and to a doctor or counsellor, is wise, and it does not contradict anything in this passage.

*Hook:* Jesus' first word here is not about the one sin never forgiven. It is about all the sins that will be.

> Truly I tell you, the sons of men will be forgiven all sins and blasphemies, as many as they utter. But whoever blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will never be forgiven; he is guilty of eternal sin.” Jesus made this statement because they were saying, “He has an unclean spirit.”

Mark 3:28–30 · BSB

**Setting.** *Blasphemy* means speaking against God, insulting or slandering Him. Teachers of the law had come down from Jerusalem. They watched Jesus set people free by the power of God's Spirit, and they said He did it by the prince of demons (Mark 3:22). Mark tells you why Jesus spoke these words: "because they were saying, 'He has an unclean spirit.'" These men saw the Holy Spirit's work in front of them and called it the devil's work.

**Read the first sentence first.** "All sins and blasphemies, as many as they utter" will be forgiven. Peter denied Jesus with oaths, and he was restored (John 21:15–19). Paul calls himself a former blasphemer, and he was shown mercy (1 Timothy 1:13). Whatever this one sin is, it is not a word you said in anger, a doubt you once had, or a sin you are ashamed of.

**How it is read.** Christians understand this sin in two main ways. Many read it as a settled, knowing rejection of the Spirit's clear witness to Jesus, the way these men looked at His works and called them evil. Others, following Augustine of Hippo (died AD 430), understand it as unbelief and refusal to repent that a person keeps up until death [check: Augustine's treatment, usually cited from his Sermon 71]. On both readings the sin is unforgivable because it refuses the only One who forgives. It is not a sin too big for Christ's blood. Even the Methodist Articles of Religion, which differ from Baptists on falling away, insist that not every sin after a person believes is this sin (part 05).

**For the anxious believer.** Notice who Jesus was speaking to. The teachers of the law were not afraid they had sinned. They were sure they were right, and they were accusing Jesus. Many pastors have observed that the person who is in anguish over this question, and who wants Christ, is showing the very opposite of the hardness Jesus described. That is a pastoral observation, not a verse. The verse is John 6:37: "the one who comes to Me I will never drive away."

[care] Some people are troubled by blasphemous thoughts that seem to arrive on their own and horrify them. Thoughts that you hate and resist are not the settled verdict of Mark 3:30. If such thoughts keep returning and will not let you rest, tell a pastor, and consider seeing a doctor or counsellor as well. Repeated, unwanted thoughts are something people get help with and recover from.

**What it does not say.** The passage does not tell you to examine yourself until you feel certain. It does not say that a believer can stumble into this sin without knowing. And it gives no one the right to tell another person they have committed it.

**Consider:** What reason does verse 30 give for Jesus' words? What does verse 28 say about all other sins and blasphemies?

**Reflect:** Are you afraid of this verse, or of Christ? Read verse 28 aloud, and then John 6:37.

## Closing: Held, and knowing it

**The habit again.** Ask what each passage was written to do. Ephesians 1 was written to make you praise. Romans 8 was written so that you would know nothing can separate you from God's love. Romans 9 answers the charge that God is unfair, and it humbles everyone who reads it. John 6 and 10 were spoken so that sheep would know they are safe. Hebrews 6 warns drifters so that they stay. First John was written "so that you may know". Mark 3 shows that the One who warns of a single unforgiven sin also promises forgiveness for all the rest. When you read a passage for what it was written to do, a song stays a song, a warning stays a warning, and a promise is something you can rest on.

**If you have not yet trusted Christ.** None of this starts with working out whether you are chosen. It starts with Him. Article IV (salvation) says: "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord." Turn from your sin and trust Him. He says that whoever comes to Him He will never drive away. *The good news* explains how a person is made right with God, and *Saved by grace* shows how God forgives and changes us.
