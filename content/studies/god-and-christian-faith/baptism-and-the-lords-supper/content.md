# Baptism and the Lord's Supper: the two things Jesus told His church to do

Concepts: Water · Bread · Cup · What comes first

Motto: Water once. Bread again and again. / Signs that point past themselves.

Lead: Jesus left His followers very few instructions about what to do with their hands. Christians in every century have kept two of them: go under the water once, and share bread and a cup together again and again. This study teaches one habit for every passage about them. Ask what the sign points to, and ask what came before it.

> Therefore go and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit,

Matthew 28:19 · BSB

Words: A few words come up again and again here.

- **Baptism** comes from a Greek word whose ordinary sense is to dip or plunge. It means being put into water in Jesus' name.
- **The Lord's Supper** is the meal of bread and cup that Jesus gave on the night before He died. Christians also call it communion, the breaking of bread, or the Eucharist, from a Greek word for giving thanks.
- **Church** here means a gathered people who belong to Christ. It does not mean a building.
- **Gospel** means good news: that Jesus died for sinners and rose again, and that God forgives everyone who trusts Him.
- **Grace** is God's kindness given to people who have not earned it.
- **Ordinance** is the Baptist word for these two acts. It means something ordered, a thing Christ commanded His church to do.
- **Sacrament** is the word many other churches use. It comes from Latin and names a holy act through which, they teach, God gives grace.

Frame: Water and bread are ordinary things. In these two acts they are not the point. A sign is useful because it points past itself. So each part of this study asks two questions. What is this sign pointing at? And what does the passage say came before it? In baptism the answer will keep being faith in Christ. In the Supper it will keep being His death "for you". If you hold on to those two questions, the harder passages become easier to read.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. The study walks the passages it was given to own: every baptism Acts tells, Colossians 2:11–12, 1 Peter 3:18–22, the meal in Mark 14, and Paul's two passages on the Supper in 1 Corinthians. It quotes, but does not walk, passages that other studies on this site own, and it links to them. Where other Christian traditions teach differently, it gives each one a short paragraph in its own words, labels it as theirs, and then gives the Baptist answer from Scripture. The site's own view is the one in article VII (baptism and the Lord's Supper) of the Baptist Faith and Message 2000, the Southern Baptist Convention's statement of faith.

## 01 Two things He told us to do

**Main readings:** Matthew 3:13–17 and Matthew 28:16–20 (quoted); 1 Corinthians 11:23–26

Hook: Jesus did not ask anyone to do something He would not do. He went into the Jordan first.

Before He sent anyone to baptize, Jesus was baptized Himself. John tried to stop Him: "I need to be baptized by You, and do You come to me?" (Matthew 3:14). John's baptism was a baptism of turning from sin, and Jesus had no sin to turn from. He answered, "It is fitting for us to fulfill all righteousness in this way" (Matthew 3:15). Then:

> As soon as Jesus was baptized, He went up out of the water. Suddenly the heavens were opened, and He saw the Spirit of God descending like a dove and resting on Him.

Matthew 3:16 · BSB

The Father speaks and the Spirit descends, and both mark out the Son. Read more in *Who do you say that I am? Who Jesus really is*.

At the end of the same Gospel, the risen Jesus sends His followers to make disciples, "baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, and teaching them to obey all that I have commanded you" (Matthew 28:19–20). Baptism sits inside a larger task. Disciples are made, then baptized, then taught. Read more in *Witness and the nations: telling others about Jesus*.

The second command came on "the night He was betrayed". Over the bread and over the cup, Jesus said, "do this in remembrance of Me" (1 Corinthians 11:23–25). Part 06 reads that passage.

[read] Baptists hold that Christ gave His church two ordinances. Both are commanded by Him for every believer, and both act out the gospel. Baptism happens once, at the start. The Supper is repeated for the rest of a Christian's life. Article VII calls baptism "a church ordinance", and that name says what Baptists believe these acts are: obedience to a command, not a channel that makes grace flow by the act itself.

[other] Roman Catholic and Orthodox churches count seven sacraments: baptism, confirmation (in the Orthodox churches, chrismation), the Eucharist, penance, the anointing of the sick, holy orders and marriage. They teach that God gives grace through each. Lutheran and Reformed churches count two, but understand them differently from Baptists, as parts 02 and 06 show.

Limit: These two acts do not save anyone, and part 02 shows why Baptists read Scripture that way. They are not optional extras either. Jesus told His people to do them.

**Consider:** What did John's objection show about the difference between Jesus' baptism and ours? Where does baptism sit in the command of Matthew 28:19–20?

**Reflect:** Is there a command of Jesus that you have treated as a nice idea rather than a command?

## 02 Buried and raised: what baptism pictures

**Main readings:** Colossians 2:11–12; 1 Peter 3:18–22; Romans 6:3–4 (quoted)

Hook: The water closes over a person's head like a grave. Then they come up.

### A burial you can see

Paul writes to a church that was being pressed to add rules to Christ, circumcision among them. Circumcision is the cutting of the foreskin that God gave Abraham's family as the sign of His covenant with them. A **covenant** is a binding relationship that God makes and promises to keep. Paul answers that in Christ they already have what they were told they lacked:

> In Him you were also circumcised, in the putting off of your sinful nature, with the circumcision performed by Christ and not by human hands. And having been buried with Him in baptism, you were raised with Him through your faith in the power of God, who raised Him from the dead.

Colossians 2:11–12 · BSB

Look at what verse 11 calls this circumcision. It is "performed by Christ and not by human hands", and it is "the putting off of your sinful nature". This is an inward change that only Christ can make. Verse 12 then describes the outward sign, baptism, as a burial and a rising. It also says how the rising happens: "through your faith in the power of God".

Romans 6 uses the same picture: "all of us who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into His death... We were therefore buried with Him through baptism into death, in order that, just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, we too may walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:3–4). Read more in *A new creation: what changes when you believe*.

[read] This is why Baptists immerse, that is, put the whole person under the water. Article VII calls baptism "an act of obedience symbolizing the believer's faith in a crucified, buried, and risen Saviour, the believer's death to sin, the burial of the old life, and the resurrection to walk in newness of life in Christ Jesus." A burial pictured by a few drops loses the picture the apostle drew. The article adds that baptism is also "a testimony to his faith in the final resurrection of the dead". Going under the water and coming up looks forward to a grave that will open.

### "The baptism that now saves you"

Peter writes to Christians who were suffering for doing right. He points them to Christ, who "suffered for sins once for all, the righteous for the unrighteous, to bring you to God" (1 Peter 3:18). Then he turns to Noah: "In the ark a few people, only eight souls, were saved through water" (1 Peter 3:20). Then comes the sentence readers argue over:

> And this water symbolizes the baptism that now saves you also—not the removal of dirt from the body, but the pledge of a clear conscience toward God—through the resurrection of Jesus Christ,

1 Peter 3:21 · BSB

Read the whole sentence. Peter stops himself straight away to say what he does not mean: "not the removal of dirt from the body". The water's washing is not what saves. Then he says what he does mean: "the pledge of a clear conscience toward God", and "through the resurrection of Jesus Christ". Noah was not saved by the flood. He was saved by being in the ark, which carried him through it.

[read] Baptists read the verse this way. The water pictures salvation, and baptism is the believer's pledge before God. What saves is the risen Christ, received by faith. Salvation is by grace through faith (Ephesians 2:8–9, not quoted here), and the dying thief was promised Paradise with no baptism recorded (Luke 23:43). Read more in *Saved by grace: how God forgives and changes us*.

### Does baptism wash away sin?

Peter's sermon at Pentecost is the hardest verse for the Baptist reading, so here it is in full: "Repent and be baptized, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit" (Acts 2:38). To **repent** is to turn from sin to God, a change of mind that shows in a change of life. Read more in *The good news: how a person is made right with God*.

[other] Roman Catholic teaching is that "Through Baptism we are freed from sin and reborn as sons of God" (Catechism of the Catholic Church, paragraph 1213). Luther's Small Catechism, a Lutheran teaching, says that baptism "works forgiveness of sins, delivers from death and the devil, and gives eternal salvation to all who believe this" [check: the exact wording of the English translation used]. Orthodox churches also teach that in baptism sin is washed away and the person is born again. All three read Acts 2:38 as giving the water a real part in forgiveness. These are their own teachings, held seriously and from Scripture as they read it.

[read] Baptists answer kindly, and from Acts itself. Eight chapters later, while Peter was still speaking, "the Holy Spirit fell upon all who heard his message". Only then did Peter ask, "Can anyone withhold the water to baptize these people? They have received the Holy Spirit just as we have!" (Acts 10:44, 47). These people had received the Spirit before any water touched them. So Baptists read Acts 2:38 as speaking of faith and its first public act together. In Acts the two came close together, on the same day and often in the same hour.

[open] Who are "the spirits in prison" in 1 Peter 3:19? Christians have long disagreed. Some hold that Christ, by His Spirit, preached through Noah to the people of Noah's day; Augustine argued this [check: Augustine, Letter 164, to Evodius], and Wayne Grudem defends it today [check: Grudem, *1 Peter*, Tyndale New Testament Commentaries, appendix]. Others hold that the risen Christ announced His victory to fallen spirits; William Dalton argued this at length [check: Dalton, *Christ's Proclamation to the Spirits*]. The confession does not settle it, and neither does this study. Peter's point about baptism does not depend on the answer. Read more in *Angels, demons and spiritual powers*.

**Consider:** In Colossians 2:12, what does Paul say the believer was raised "through"? What does Peter say the saving baptism is not?

**Reflect:** If your baptism was a pledge, what did you pledge? If you have not been baptized, what would you be saying if you were?

## 03 What came before the water: every baptism in Acts

**Main readings:** Acts 8:12; 9:17–18; 10:44–48; 16:13–15; 16:25–34; 18:8; 19:1–7; with Acts 2:41 and 8:36–38 (quoted)

Hook: Ten times Acts says someone was baptized. Look at what happened just before.

PanelLead: Acts tells of ten baptisms. This counts each time Acts says that a person or a group was baptized in Jesus' name. It does not count commands, John's baptism, or Paul's later retelling of his own story. Read down the water. On the left is what the passage says came first. On the right is the baptism.

Panel:
1. Acts 2:41. "Those who embraced his message" → "were baptized, and about three thousand were added".
2. Acts 8:12. "when they believed Philip as he preached" → "they were baptized, both men and women".
3. Acts 8:13. Simon, Acts says, believed (not quoted here) → he was baptized.
4. Acts 8:36–38. Philip had told the eunuch the good news about Jesus (8:35, not quoted here). The eunuch asks, "What is there to prevent me from being baptized?" → "both Philip and the eunuch went down into the water, and Philip baptized him".
5. Acts 9:17–18. The Lord Jesus had appeared to Saul, and Ananias speaks His message → "He got up and was baptized".
6. Acts 10:44–48. "the Holy Spirit fell upon all who heard his message" → "he ordered that they be baptized in the name of Jesus Christ".
7. Acts 16:13–15. Lydia was listening; "The Lord opened her heart to respond" → "she and her household had been baptized".
8. Acts 16:25–34. "Believe in the Lord Jesus"; the word spoken "to everyone in his house" → "he and all his household were baptized".
9. Acts 18:8. "many of the Corinthians who heard the message believed" → "and were baptized".
10. Acts 19:1–7. Paul explains that John pointed to Jesus; "On hearing this" → "they were baptized into the name of the Lord Jesus".

PanelReads: In every one of the ten, the passage names something heard, believed or received before the water, for those it says were baptized. For Lydia's household, Acts says nothing about anyone's faith or age; it names only Lydia's opened heart. For the jailer's household and for Crispus' household, it says they believed. No infant is named, and none is ruled out. This pattern is one reason Baptists baptize those who have believed.

PanelCare: Baptism follows faith, but it does not prove faith. Acts says Simon believed and was baptized, and a few verses later Peter tells him his heart is not right before God (Acts 8:13, 21). The Berean has no verse 37 between Acts 8:36 and 8:38, which follows the oldest manuscripts, so that passage is shown in two parts. Choosing which words count as "what came first" is this study's judgement.

### A jailer at midnight

In other Spanish-speaking countries, call your local emergency number or crisis line [check before publishing: lines for each country the site serves]. Paul and Silas had been beaten and locked in stocks. At midnight they were "praying and singing hymns to God", and the other prisoners were listening (Acts 16:25). An earthquake threw the doors open. The jailer woke, thought the prisoners had escaped, and was about to take his own life. A jailer could be held responsible with his own life for lost prisoners. The first words he heard came from the men he had locked up:

> But Paul called out in a loud voice, "Do not harm yourself! We are all here!"

Acts 16:28 · BSB

The gospel reaches the jailer as care for his life before it reaches him as an answer. Then he asks the question, "Sirs, what must I do to be saved?" The answer is short: "Believe in the Lord Jesus and you will be saved, you and your household" (Acts 16:30–31).

Watch the order in the next verses. "Paul and Paul and Silas spoke the word of the Lord to him and to everyone in his house" (16:32) comes first. Then the man who had fastened their feet in stocks "washed their wounds" (16:33). Then "without delay, he and all his household were baptized" (16:33). Then a meal and joy: "he and all his household rejoiced that they had come to believe in God" (16:34).

[read] Two things stand out. The word was spoken to everyone in the house before anyone was baptized, and the joy at the end belongs to everyone who was baptized: "all his household" believed. The household is not baptized on the jailer's faith alone; the passage says they heard and believed. Notice too that it all happened "without delay", in the middle of the night. In Acts, baptism was not a reward for having grown mature. It was a believer's first step.

**Consider:** In the jailer's story, what comes before the water, and what comes after it? What does Acts say about Lydia's household, and what does it leave unsaid?

**Reflect:** The jailer washed wounds he had helped to cause, in the same hour he believed. Is there someone whose hurt you should tend as part of following Christ?

## 04 Who should be baptized, and how?

**Main readings:** Colossians 2:11–12 and the households of Acts 16 (from parts 02 and 03)

Hook: Christians who agree on almost everything else still part at the water's edge.

[read] The site holds with article VII that "Christian baptism is the immersion of a believer in water in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit." There are three reasons. The command in Matthew 28:19 puts making disciples first. Acts shows the pattern traced in part 03. And Romans 6 and Colossians 2 describe baptism as a burial and a rising. Older Baptists said the same. The Second London Baptist Confession (1677) says that those who "do actually profess repentance towards God, faith in, and obedience to, our Lord Jesus, are the only proper subjects of this ordinance", and that immersion "is necessary to the due administration of this ordinance" (chapter 29, sections 2 and 4) [check: exact wording and spelling of the 1677 text]. The New Hampshire Confession (1833) calls baptism "the immersion in water of a believer".

[other] Presbyterian and Reformed churches teach otherwise, from a covenant argument. The Westminster Confession says that "Not only those that do actually profess faith in and obedience unto Christ, but also the infants of one or both believing parents, are to be baptized" (chapter 28, section 4). It also says that "Dipping of the person into the water is not necessary; but Baptism is rightly administered by pouring or sprinkling water upon the person" (28.3). They reason this way. God gave circumcision to Abraham's children as infants. Colossians 2:11–12 sets circumcision and baptism side by side. So the children of believers should receive the covenant sign now, as they did then. Colossians 2:11–12 is among the Bible verses cited for 28.4 [check: the proof-texts printed under 28.4]. Some commentators from these churches also read the households of Acts this way. Albert Barnes, a Presbyterian, finds in Lydia's household "a strong presumptive proof that this was an instance of household or infant baptism" (on Acts 16:15). Jamieson, Fausset and Brown, also not Baptists, say on the same verse that whether children were present "is not explicitly stated", and that "the question of infant baptism must be determined on other grounds".

[read] The Baptist answer comes from the same verses. The circumcision Colossians speaks of is "performed by Christ and not by human hands" (2:11). What baptism answers to is that inward change, and the rising is "through your faith" (2:12). Circumcision marked birth into a family line. The new covenant people are marked by new birth and faith, and that is what baptism is said to show. As for the households, Acts says the jailer's household "had come to believe" and that Crispus "and his whole household believed" (Acts 16:34; 18:8). Lydia's verse says nothing either way. These verses cannot settle the question for either side on their own, so Baptists rest it on the command and the pattern.

[other] The Orthodox churches baptize infants, and they baptize by immersing the child three times, once at each name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit [check: an Orthodox source to quote on triple immersion]. So Baptists and Orthodox agree that baptism means going under the water. They differ on who is to be baptized, not how.

### "Do I need to be baptized again?"

Many people come to a Baptist church having been baptized as babies, often by parents who loved them and loved Christ. A Baptist church will ask such a person to be baptized as a believer. It does this because it holds that infant baptism is not the baptism Scripture describes. It never does so because it doubts the faith of the parents who brought the child. Most Baptists would not call this "re-baptism" at all. In their eyes, it is the first time the person receives the baptism Christ commanded. If your parents brought you to be baptized, you can honour their love and their prayers, and still take the step yourself.

### "When is a child ready?"

The confession sets no age. Baptist churches usually talk with a child who asks, to hear in the child's own words what they believe about Jesus. Read more in *Children and the home*.

**Consider:** In Colossians 2:11–12, what kind of circumcision does baptism answer to? Why can neither side settle the question from the household verses alone?

**Reflect:** If you were baptized as a believer, can you say in a sentence what you were confessing? If not yet, what holds you back?

## 05 The Passover and the upper room

**Main readings:** Mark 14:12–26; Exodus 12:1–14, 24–27 (quoted); Luke 22:19–20 (quoted)

Hook: The first Lord's Supper was set at a Jewish feast, kept by Jews, remembering a Jewish rescue.

### The Passover in Jewish terms

The Passover, Pesach in Hebrew, is the feast at which the Jewish people remember how God brought Israel out of slavery in Egypt. In Exodus, each household was to kill a lamb and put its blood on the doorframes. They were to eat it "roasted over the fire, along with unleavened bread and bitter herbs", dressed for travel (Exodus 12:7–11). God said, "when I see the blood, I will pass over you" (12:13). And:

> And this day will be a memorial for you, and you are to celebrate it as a feast to the LORD, as a permanent statute for the generations to come.

Exodus 12:14 · BSB

The feast was built to be explained. "When your children ask you, 'What does this service mean to you?'", the answer was to be the story itself: the LORD "passed over the houses of the Israelites in Egypt when He struck down the Egyptians and spared our homes" (Exodus 12:26–27).

Jewish families still keep the Passover. At the evening meal, called the seder ("order"), they read from the Haggadah ("telling"). They eat matzah, the unleavened bread, and bitter herbs, and drink cups of wine. A child asks why this night is different from all other nights, and the story of the exodus is told in answer. Since the Temple in Jerusalem was destroyed in AD 70, no Passover lamb has been sacrificed, and a roasted bone on the seder plate recalls it. The Mishnah, the early collection of Jewish teaching, says that in every generation a person should regard himself as if he himself had come out of Egypt (Mishnah Pesachim 10:5) [check: wording and reference]. To remember, in this sense, is not only to recall a past event. It is to take one's place inside the story.

How much of today's seder was already practised in Jesus' day is something historians discuss, and this study does not assume it all was.

### "This is My body"

Mark says the disciples prepared the meal "On the first day of the Feast of Unleavened Bread, when the Passover lamb was to be sacrificed" (Mark 14:12). Jesus and the Twelve were Jews keeping the feast of their people. During the meal Jesus said, "one of you who is eating with Me will betray Me", and each asked, "Surely not I?" (14:18–19). The first Supper was eaten by people who did not trust themselves.

Then Jesus did something new with the old meal:

> While they were eating, Jesus took bread, spoke a blessing and broke it, and gave it to the disciples, saying, "Take it; this is My body." Then He took the cup, gave thanks, and gave it to them, and they all drank from it. He said to them, "This is My blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many."

Mark 14:22–24 · BSB

"Blood of the covenant" echoes Exodus 24, where Moses sealed God's covenant with Israel with blood. Luke records the words as "This cup is the new covenant in My blood, which is poured out for you" (Luke 22:20). Read more in *Covenants and promises*.

Jesus then looked forward: "I will no longer drink of the fruit of the vine until that day when I drink it anew in the kingdom of God" (Mark 14:25). The meal points back to His death and forward to a feast still to come. "And when they had sung a hymn, they went out to the Mount of Olives" (14:26). Many commentators take this hymn to be the Psalms of praise (Psalms 113–118) sung at Passover, though Mark does not say which.

[read] Christians read the Passover as one of the places where the Old Testament points to Christ. Paul calls Christ "our Passover lamb" (1 Corinthians 5:7, not quoted here). As the lamb's blood marked the houses that judgement passed over, Christ's death is the ground on which God forgives. This is a Christian reading, and it is offered as one. It takes nothing from the Passover as Jewish people keep it, and it lays the guilt for Jesus' death on no people. Scripture says He went to the cross for sinners, and His followers count their own sins among the reasons. Read more in *Christ in the Old Testament*.

[open] Mark, Matthew and Luke present the Last Supper as a Passover meal. John's Gospel seems to place Jesus' death at the hour the Passover lambs were killed. Careful readers explain this in more than one way, and this study does not settle it [check: whether the cross study or the Gospels material covers this].

**Consider:** What question did the Passover expect children to ask, and what was the answer? What two directions does Mark 14:24–25 look in?

**Reflect:** The disciples each asked "Surely not I?" What would it mean to come to the Lord's table without pretending you are stronger than you are?

## 06 Do this in remembrance of Me

**Main readings:** 1 Corinthians 11:23–34; 1 Corinthians 10:16–17

Hook: Paul passes on Jesus' words to fix a dinner party gone wrong.

### What Paul received and passed on

At Corinth the church's shared meal had become a place where the well-fed ate first and the poor went hungry. Read more on that in *The church and its calling*. Paul's answer is to tell them again what the meal is:

> For I received from the Lord what I also passed on to you: The Lord Jesus, on the night He was betrayed, took bread, and when He had given thanks, He broke it and said, "This is My body, which is for you; do this in remembrance of Me."

1 Corinthians 11:23–24 · BSB

The cup follows: "This cup is the new covenant in My blood; do this, as often as you drink it, in remembrance of Me" (11:25). Then Paul says what the meal does:

> For as often as you eat this bread and drink this cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until He comes.

1 Corinthians 11:26 · BSB

The Supper is a sermon without words. It looks back to "the Lord's death" and forward to "until He comes".

### Eating "in an unworthy manner"

Paul warns that "whoever eats the bread or drinks the cup of the Lord in an unworthy manner will be guilty of sinning against the body and blood of the Lord. Each one must examine himself" (11:27–28). Read the warning beside the conduct Paul is confronting. The "unworthy manner" is the scene of the previous verses: a meal about Christ's self-giving, eaten in a way that shamed the poor. The meal is not a test of whether you feel good enough. It is a meal of people who deserved nothing. Paul's remedy is practical: "when you come together to eat, wait for one another" (11:33).

"For anyone who eats and drinks without recognizing the body eats and drinks judgment on himself" (11:29). Albert Barnes reads "the body" as failing to tell the Supper apart from any other meal, "between the bread which is used on this occasion, and common and ordinary food" (on 1 Corinthians 11:29). This study also hears the church in that word, because of the hungry being shamed in this very chapter, and because of 1 Corinthians 10:17, read below. That is this study's reading; no writer holding it was read for this page. Both may be true together.

[care] Paul says, "That is why many among you are weak and sick, and a number of you have fallen asleep" (11:30). That was a word through an apostle about a particular church and a particular sin. It does not mean that anyone who is ill is being punished, and no one may use it to explain another person's illness. Even here Paul calls it discipline "so that we will not be condemned with the world" (11:32). If you are ill, seeking a doctor's care is right. Read more in *What Scripture promises the sick*.

### One loaf

A chapter earlier, Paul had used the meal to warn against feasts in idols' temples:

> Is not the cup of blessing that we bless a participation in the blood of Christ? And is not the bread that we break a participation in the body of Christ? Because there is one loaf, we who are many are one body; for we all partake of the one loaf.

1 Corinthians 10:16–17 · BSB

"Cup of blessing" was a Jewish name for a cup at a festive meal over which God was blessed. The Supper joins those who share it to Christ and to one another.

### What happens at the table?

[read] The site holds with article VII that "The Lord's Supper is a symbolic act of obedience whereby members of the church, through partaking of the bread and the fruit of the vine, memorialize the death of the Redeemer and anticipate His second coming." There are reasons in the text. Jesus says "do this in remembrance of Me" twice. And after His words, Paul still calls it "this bread" (11:26). Jesus was at the table in His body when He spoke, and His sacrifice was "once for all" (1 Peter 3:18). Some Baptists, reading "participation" in 10:16, speak of the Lord's nearness to those who come in faith, and the Second London Confession used language close to Westminster's below [check: Second London Confession, chapter 30, section 7]. Others hold to remembrance alone. Article VII says the Supper memorializes and anticipates, and says no more. Neither does this page.

[other] The Council of Trent, a Roman Catholic council, in its thirteenth session (1551), teaches that after the consecration Christ "is truly, really, and substantially contained" under the appearances of bread and wine. It calls the change of the whole substance of the bread and wine into His body and blood "Transubstantiation", meaning a change of substance.

[other] Lutheran teaching holds that Christ's true body and blood are present "in, with and under" the bread and wine [check: the wording in the Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration VII]. Luther's Small Catechism calls the Supper "the true body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ, under the bread and wine". Lutherans reject the label "consubstantiation" that others have given this view [check: a Lutheran church source stating this].

[other] Reformed churches follow John Calvin in teaching a spiritual presence. The Westminster Confession says worthy receivers "spiritually, receive and feed upon Christ crucified", and that His body and blood are "not corporally or carnally in, with, or under the bread and wine; yet as really, but spiritually, present to the faith of believers" (chapter 29, section 7).

[other] Orthodox churches confess that the bread and wine become Christ's body and blood. Many Orthodox writers stress that the Church declines to define how this happens [check: an Orthodox source; note that the Confession of Dositheus, 1672, used the Greek word metousiosis].

[read] Baptists answer these traditions with respect, from the passages themselves. The words are "remembrance" and "proclaim". The bread is still bread when it is eaten. And the death it remembers was finished once and needs no repeating. The meal's power lies in what it points to: Christ, who gave His body "for you".

**Consider:** What two directions does 1 Corinthians 11:26 look in? Whose conduct was Paul's warning about "an unworthy manner" aimed at?

**Reflect:** Paul says to "wait for one another". Who at your table might you be leaving behind?

## 07 Who comes to the table?

**Main readings:** 1 Corinthians 11:28, 33; 1 Corinthians 10:17 (from part 06)

Hook: The table belongs to the Lord. Churches still have to decide who comes to it.

[read] Article VII says that baptism, "Being a church ordinance... is prerequisite to the privileges of church membership and to the Lord's Supper." Prerequisite means it comes first. The site says so plainly. In Acts, faith led to baptism, and the baptized were "added" to the believers (Acts 2:41). The Supper is the meal of that gathered people. So the order is faith, then baptism, then the table.

[open] Baptist churches who hold this line still differ on one point, and the confession leaves room. Some practise **closed** communion: the table is for members of that local church. Others practise **close** communion: the table is for baptized believers of like faith, including those from other churches. This study does not choose between them.

[other] Some churches practise **open** communion, inviting every believer to the table, baptized or not. Many who do this love Christ and want no believer turned away. The site says kindly and plainly that the confession's line is its own: baptism comes before the Supper, because that is the order the New Testament shows.

[care] If you are a believer and have not been baptized, this is not a closed door. It is an invitation to take the first step. Speak to a pastor about being baptized, and then come. If you have been baptized and stay away because you feel unworthy, read 1 Corinthians 11 again. Paul asks you to examine yourself (11:28), and examining yourself is meant to lead you to the table, not away from it. The meal is for sinners who trust Christ. Read more in *Chosen and kept: can I be sure?* and *Life together: belonging to a church*.

**Consider:** What does article VII say must come before the Supper, and why? What is the difference between closed and close communion?

**Reflect:** Is there anything keeping you from either the water or the table that Scripture itself does not put there?

## Close: Signs that point past themselves

Way: Here is the habit to carry to the next passage. Ask what the sign points to, and ask what came before it. The water points to a burial and a rising, Christ's and yours. Before it, every time, came hearing and believing. The bread and cup point to a body given and blood poured out "for you", and forward to "until He comes". Before them came the night He was betrayed. Neither sign is the saving thing. Each points to the One who saves.

Invite: If you have not yet trusted Christ, none of this begins with water or bread. It begins with Him. The jailer asked, "What must I do to be saved?", and the answer has not changed: "Believe in the Lord Jesus" (Acts 16:30–31). Turn from your sin and trust Him as Lord. Then find a church, and ask to be baptized.

Question: What is your next step: the water, the table, or coming back to one you have neglected?
