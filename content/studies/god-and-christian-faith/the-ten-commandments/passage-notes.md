# Passage notes: The Ten Commandments (61)

## Exodus 20:1–17 (owned)

**Context.** Israel has reached the Wilderness of Sinai in the third month after leaving Egypt (Exodus 19:1). The LORD has reminded them of the rescue and offered a covenant (19:4–6), and the people have answered "We will do everything that the LORD has spoken" (19:8). The ten words come between that offer and the sealing of the covenant with blood and a meal (24:1–11). The frame (19:1–8, 20:1–2, 24:1–11) is owned by *Covenants and promises* (26); this study owns the commandments themselves. Verses 18–21 (the people's fear) are not walked here.

**Main readings and who holds them.**
- *The opening sentence (20:2).* Jewish tradition counts it as the first word (Maimonides lists belief in God from it as the first commandment of the Torah). Most Protestants and the Orthodox treat it as a preface; the Baptist Catechism (1693/1695) calls it "the preface to the ten commandments". Catholics and Lutherans also treat it as a preface.
- *Numbering.* Jewish: preface counted, "no other gods" and "no idols" one word, coveting one. Philo, Josephus, the Orthodox, Reformed and most Protestant churches, including Baptists: "no other gods" and "no idols" separate, coveting one. Augustine, then Roman Catholic and Lutheran teaching: "no other gods" and "no idols" joined, coveting divided (Catholics follow Deuteronomy 5:21's order; Luther's Small Catechism follows Exodus 20:17's). This study follows the Protestant and Orthodox numbering, as the row directs.
- *Two tables.* Christian teaching (following Matthew 22:37–40) reads words 1–4 as love for God and 5–10 as love for neighbour. Jewish tradition pictures five words on each stone (Mekhilta), placing honouring parents with the duties toward God. Some scholars (Meredith Kline) suggest each stone carried a full copy, after the pattern of ancient treaties. The text does not say; the study presents the two-table picture as a teaching arrangement grounded in Jesus' summary.
- *20:5–6, visiting iniquity.* Targum Onkelos reads it as applying where children continue in their fathers' sin; Christian readers commonly read "of those who hate Me" the same way, with Deuteronomy 24:16 and Ezekiel 18:20. The contrast of three or four generations with a thousand is noted.
- *20:7.* Read broadly: false oaths, empty use of God's name, and claiming His name falsely.
- *20:8–11.* Creation as the ground of the Sabbath (with Genesis 2:2–3).

**Studies it links.** Covenants and promises (26) for the Sinai frame; God: who He is and what He does (21) for the divine name; Genesis 1–11 (50) for Genesis 2:2–3; Every life in God's image (62); Sexuality, singleness and divorce (43); Money, possessions and generosity (63); Children and the home (60); Following Jesus (16); Leviticus (47); The shape of Scripture (33) samples 20:1–3 and should link here.

## Deuteronomy 5:6–21 (owned)

**Context.** Forty years after Sinai, on the plains of Moab, Moses repeats the ten words to a new generation before they enter the land (Deuteronomy 5:1–5).

**Main readings and who holds them.**
- *Differences from Exodus.* "Observe" for "Remember" in the Sabbath command (5:12); the Sabbath grounded in the rescue from slavery (5:15) rather than creation, with the purpose "so that your manservant and maidservant may rest as you do" (5:14); "that it may go well with you" added to the parents' command (5:16); in the coveting command the wife named first, "field" added, and in Hebrew a second verb of desire for the goods (5:21). Jewish and Christian readers alike treat the two lists as one set of words given with different emphases; the Catholic division of coveting follows this order.
- The study reads the two reasons for the Sabbath (creation, rescue) together, as both a gift and a matter of justice.

**Studies it links.** Same as Exodus 20; Children and the home (60) owns Deuteronomy 6:4–9, which follows.

## Mark 2:23–28 (owned)

**Context.** The first of two Sabbath controversies in Galilee (2:23–3:6), part of a run of disputes in Mark 2:1–3:6. Mark 3:1–6, the healing in the synagogue, belongs to *Encounters with Jesus* (14); this study quotes Mark 3:4 only.

**Main readings and who holds them.**
- *2:27–28.* Jesus neither abolishes the Sabbath nor merely relaxes a rule; He states the day's purpose and claims lordship over it as the Son of Man (Daniel 7:13–14). Christians who keep the first day (the Baptist Faith and Message 2000, article VIII; the Second London Confession, ch. 22) and those who keep the seventh (Seventh Day Baptists; Seventh-day Adventists) all appeal to this passage, reading "Lord of the Sabbath" differently: as authority to fulfil and reshape the day, or as endorsement of the seventh day itself.
- *The Pharisees' objection.* Presented fairly as a debate among Jewish teachers over how to honour the day; plucking counted as reaping, one of the categories in Mishnah Shabbat 7:2. Jewish teaching that saving life overrides the Sabbath (Mishnah Yoma 8:6) is noted beside Mark 3:4.
- *2:26, Abiathar.* 1 Samuel 21:1–6 names Ahimelech. Explanations: the phrase may mean "in the passage about Abiathar" (compare Mark 12:26; associated with William Lane, to be checked); or "in the days of Abiathar", who was present and became the better-known high priest. The study presents this briefly, under article I.

**Studies it links.** Encounters with Jesus (14) for Mark 3:4; Who do you say that I am? (11) for "Son of Man" (not linked in text; editor may add); Christ in the Old Testament (41) for Colossians 2:16–17.
