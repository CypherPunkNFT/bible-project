# The Ten Commandments

Subtitle: The ten words, God's law of love

Concepts: Ten words · Two tables · One love

Motto: Two stones. One voice. / Rescue first, then the words.

Lead: The Ten Commandments are the best-known lines of the Bible's law, meaning the instructions God gave the people of Israel at Mount Sinai. Most people meet them as a list of rules on a wall. This study teaches one reading habit: before you read a command, read who is speaking and what He has already done. Then it walks the ten in two groups, love for God and love for neighbour, follows Jesus as He reads them, and stops at the one that Christians keep differently: the day of rest.

> I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery.

Exodus 20:2 · BSB

Frame: Scripture gives the commandments twice. The first time is at Mount Sinai, soon after Israel's escape from Egypt (Exodus 20). The second is forty years later, when Moses repeats them on the edge of the promised land (Deuteronomy 5). The two are almost word for word the same, and where they differ, the difference teaches something. One note on the page: where English Bibles print LORD in capitals, they stand for God's own personal name, which He made known to Moses (Exodus 3:14–15). Read more in *God: who He is and what He does*.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. This study walks three passages: the commandments in Exodus 20:1–17, their repetition in Deuteronomy 5:6–21, and one Sabbath day in Mark 2:23–28. It quotes a few verses from elsewhere and links the studies that walk them. It does not walk the scene at the mountain, which belongs to *Covenants and promises*, or the Sermon on the Mount, which belongs to *Following Jesus and the Sermon on the Mount*. The larger question of how the whole law of Moses applies to Christians belongs to *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice*; this study gives that study's answer in brief and links it. Marriage and property, which two of the commandments guard, have their own studies too: *Sexuality, singleness and divorce* and *Money, possessions and generosity*.

## 01 Ten words, and the first is a rescue

**Main readings:** Exodus 20:1–17; Deuteronomy 5:6–21

The passage opens plainly: "And God spoke all these words" (Exodus 20:1). Elsewhere the Hebrew calls them, literally, "the ten words" (Exodus 34:28; Deuteronomy 4:13; 10:4). English Bibles, the Berean among them, usually write "Ten Commandments" there. The old name Decalogue is simply Greek for "ten words".

Notice what comes first. It is not a command. It is a name and a rescue: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of slavery" (Exodus 20:2). The people hearing this are already free. They are camped at the mountain because God has brought them there, as He reminded them a few days before:

> ‘You have seen for yourselves what I did to Egypt, and how I carried you on eagles’ wings and brought you to Myself.

Exodus 19:4 · BSB

At Sinai God made a covenant with Israel. A covenant is a binding agreement in which God makes promises to a people and asks a faithful life of them in return. The ten words stand at its centre. Read more in *Covenants and promises*.

### How the ten are counted

Scripture says there are ten, but it does not number them. Three ways of counting are in use today. None of them adds a word or removes one; they read the same verses and draw the lines in different places.

**In Jewish tradition** the Ten are called the *Aseret ha-Dibrot*, "the ten utterances" or "ten words". The first word is the sentence you have just read, "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt" (Exodus 20:2). It is counted as a word in its own right, because it calls Israel to know and trust the God who saved them. Moses Maimonides, the twelfth-century Jewish teacher, lists believing in God, drawn from this verse, as the first of all the commandments of the Torah [check: Maimonides, *Sefer ha-Mitzvot*, positive commandment 1]. (The Torah is the Jewish name for the five books of Moses; the word means teaching or instruction.) The second word joins "no other gods" and "no idols" into one (Exodus 20:3–6), and the tenth is the whole command against coveting (Exodus 20:17). In the synagogue the Ten are read aloud in the yearly cycle of Torah readings, and on Shavuot, the festival that remembers the giving of the Torah at Sinai, and many congregations stand to hear them [check: customs of standing differ between communities]. In the days of the Temple they were recited each morning with the Shema, Israel's confession that the LORD is one (Mishnah, Tamid 5:1). The Talmud records that the daily recital was later stopped, so that no one could claim that only these ten were given at Sinai [check: Babylonian Talmud, Berakhot 12a].

**Most Protestants and the Orthodox churches** count the opening sentence as a preface, then take "no other gods" as the first commandment and "no idols" as the second, with the command against coveting kept whole as the tenth. This study follows that numbering. It is old: the first-century Jewish writers Philo and Josephus already divided the commandments this way [check: Philo, *On the Decalogue*; Josephus, *Antiquities* 3.91–92]. The Baptist Catechism, approved by Baptists in London in 1693, counts in the same way and calls the opening sentence "the preface to the ten commandments" [check: question number and wording in the site's copy].

**Roman Catholics and Lutherans** follow the way Augustine of Hippo counted in the early fifth century [check: Augustine, *Questions on the Heptateuch* 2.71]. "No other gods" and "no idols" make one commandment, so the name of God becomes the second and the Sabbath the third. The command against coveting is divided into two, to keep the count at ten. Catholic teaching follows the order of Deuteronomy 5:21, so the ninth is coveting a neighbour's wife and the tenth his goods [check: Catechism of the Catholic Church, paragraphs 2514–2557]. Luther's Small Catechism follows Exodus 20:17, so its ninth is coveting a neighbour's house and its tenth his wife, servants and animals.

What all three share matters more than where they differ. Each one begins from the rescue, and none of them reads the ten as the price of getting out of Egypt.

**Reflect:** If the ten began with "Do this" instead of "I brought you out", what would change in how you heard them? What has God already done for you before you have done anything?

## 02 The first table: love for God (words one to four)

**Main readings:** Exodus 20:3–11; Deuteronomy 5:7–15

Exodus says the words were written on two tablets of stone (Exodus 31:18; 32:15). An older word for tablet is "table", which is why Christians speak of the "two tables" of the law. Scripture does not say how the words were divided between the stones. Christian teaching has long read the first group as duties toward God and the second as duties toward a neighbour, because that is how Jesus summed them up (see part 04). Jewish tradition pictures five words on each stone, which puts honouring parents on God's side [check: Mekhilta de-Rabbi Ishmael, Bachodesh 8]. Some scholars suggest instead that each stone carried the whole text, as ancient treaties were written out in two copies, one for each party [check: Meredith Kline, *Treaty of the Great King*, 1963]. The two-table picture is a way of teaching, not a sentence from Exodus. It is a good one all the same, because it follows Jesus' own reading.

### No other gods

"You shall have no other gods before Me" (Exodus 20:3). This is spoken to people who have just watched the gods of Egypt fail to keep them. The command asks for undivided loyalty to the One who saved them.

[source:luther-small] Luther's Small Catechism, a Lutheran teaching, explains this commandment in one line: "We should fear, love, and trust in God above all things." [check: wording against the site's copy of the Small Catechism]

### No idol

The second word forbids making an image in order to "bow down to them or worship them" (Exodus 20:5). It is not a ban on all art: a few chapters later God Himself orders carved cherubim for the ark (Exodus 25:18). It forbids shaping God into something we can make and manage. Then God gives the reason: "for I, the LORD your God, am a jealous God" (Exodus 20:5). His jealousy is the rightful jealousy of a faithful husband, a love that will not share its beloved with a rival. It is not the petty envy the word can mean in ordinary speech.

### Does God punish children for their parents' sins?

Readers stumble here, and fairly. The verse speaks of God "visiting the iniquity of the fathers on their children to the third and fourth generations of those who hate Me" (Exodus 20:5). Iniquity means wrongdoing.

Read the whole sentence. The children in view are "those who hate Me", and the next verse sets the other side against it:

> but showing loving devotion to a thousand generations of those who love Me and keep My commandments.

Exodus 20:6 · BSB

The ancient Jewish Aramaic paraphrase known as Targum Onkelos reads the warning this way, adding that it applies when the children go on sinning as their fathers did [check: Targum Onkelos on Exodus 20:5]. The rest of Scripture agrees that each person answers for their own sin (Deuteronomy 24:16; Ezekiel 18:20). So this verse is a warning that a family's turning from God can run down the years, and that mercy runs much further: three or four generations against a thousand. "Loving devotion" translates the Hebrew *hesed*, God's faithful covenant love.

[open] The apologetics answer on whether God punishes children for their parents' sins goes further into this question [check: whether this answer exists; its exact title and link].

### The name

"You shall not take the name of the LORD your God in vain" (Exodus 20:7). The Hebrew pictures lifting or carrying God's name for nothing, for an empty purpose. That certainly includes careless swearing. It also includes making a false promise in His name, and calling yourself His while living as though He did not matter.

### The Sabbath

The fourth word, the day of rest, is the longest of the ten and the one Christians keep in different ways. It has a part of its own (part 05).

**Consider:** Who is the first table about? In Exodus 20:5–6, how far does the warning reach, and how far does the mercy reach?

**Reflect:** What do you trust in more readily than God when you are afraid? What would it look like to carry His name well this week?

## 03 The second table: love for neighbour (words five to ten)

**Main readings:** Exodus 20:12–17; Deuteronomy 5:16–21

The second table turns from God to the people around you, and it moves in a clear order: family, life, marriage, property, truth, and at last the heart.

### Father and mother

"Honor your father and mother, so that your days may be long in the land that the LORD your God is giving you" (Exodus 20:12). Deuteronomy adds "and that it may go well with you" (Deuteronomy 5:16). To honour is to give weight: respect, care, and in childhood obedience. It is the first command of the ten with a promise attached. Read more in *Children and the home*.

[care] If a parent harmed you, this command does not ask you to stay where you are not safe, to keep a secret, or to pretend nothing happened. If you are in danger now, contact local emergency services. In Spain, call 024; in Mexico, the Línea de la Vida at 800 911 2000; in other Spanish-speaking countries, your local emergency number [check before publishing]. In Brazil, call CVV at 188 [check before publishing]. Feelings this heavy do pass, and people do recover. What follows is this site's own word, not a quotation: honouring a parent who has done harm may mean praying for them, speaking truthfully, and keeping a safe distance. Scripture never asks anyone to obey a call to sin, and getting help is not dishonour.

### Life

"You shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13). The Hebrew word speaks of the unlawful taking of a human life, which is why the Berean writes "murder" and not "kill". Human life is guarded because people are made in God's image (Genesis 9:6). Read more in *Every life in God's image*.

[source:luther-small] Luther's Small Catechism, a Lutheran teaching, turns the prohibition outward into care: "We should fear and love God that we may not hurt nor harm our neighbor in his body, but help and befriend him in every bodily need." [check: wording against the site's copy]

### Marriage and property

"You shall not commit adultery" (Exodus 20:14) guards the promise of marriage. "You shall not steal" (Exodus 20:15) guards what belongs to another. Both have their own studies, *Sexuality, singleness and divorce* and *Money, possessions and generosity*, and this one does not repeat them.

### Truth

"You shall not bear false witness against your neighbor" (Exodus 20:16). The words come from the courtroom, where a lie could cost a person their land, their freedom or their life. Christians have long read them more widely, as a guard on a neighbour's good name.

[source:luther-small] Luther's explanation ends on a phrase worth carrying: we should "defend him, think and speak well of him, and put the best construction on everything." [check: wording against the site's copy]

### The heart

The last word goes where no court can follow. "You shall not covet your neighbor’s house" (Exodus 20:17). To covet is to want what belongs to someone else so badly that you would take it if you could. Every other command can be checked from outside. This one cannot, because coveting happens inside you.

Compare the two lists. Exodus names the house first and then the wife. Deuteronomy names the wife first, and adds "field" to the house (Deuteronomy 5:21). In Hebrew, Deuteronomy also uses a second verb for the house and what follows, a word for desire, where the Berean writes "covet" both times [check: Hebrew of Deuteronomy 5:21, *tachmod* and *titavveh*]. The point is the same in both lists, but Deuteronomy sets the wife apart from the goods.

**Consider:** In what order does the second table move, from the family to the heart? Which of the ten could no judge ever enforce?

**Reflect:** Which neighbour's good name is in your hands this week? What do you want badly enough that it has begun to shape how you treat someone?

## 04 Jesus reads the ten, and sums them in love

**Main readings:** Exodus 20:1–17, read with Matthew 5:21–30, Matthew 22:37–40 and Romans 13:8–10

### The commands reach the heart

In the Sermon on the Mount Jesus takes two of the ten and follows them inward:

> You have heard that it was said to the ancients, ‘Do not murder’ and ‘Anyone who murders will be subject to judgment.’ But I tell you that anyone who is angry with his brother will be subject to judgment.

Matthew 5:21–22 · BSB

> You have heard that it was said, ‘Do not commit adultery.’ But I tell you that anyone who looks at a woman to lust after her has already committed adultery with her in his heart.

Matthew 5:27–28 · BSB

Jesus is not adding new rules. He shows where the old ones were always aimed. The tenth word had already said as much. Read more in *Following Jesus and the Sermon on the Mount*.

### Two loves, two tables

Asked which commandment is greatest, Jesus answers from Moses (Deuteronomy 6:5; Leviticus 19:18):

> Jesus declared, “‘Love the Lord your God with all your heart and with all your soul and with all your mind.’ This is the first and greatest commandment. And the second is like it: ‘Love your neighbor as yourself.’ All the Law and the Prophets hang on these two commandments.”

Matthew 22:37–40 · BSB

Here are the two tables, in Jesus' own words. Mark tells the same exchange, and *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice* walks that account. Paul reads the second table the same way:

> The commandments “Do not commit adultery,” “Do not murder,” “Do not steal,” “Do not covet,” and any other commandments, are summed up in this one decree: “Love your neighbor as yourself.” Love does no wrong to its neighbor. Therefore love is the fulfillment of the law.

Romans 13:9–10 · BSB

Love does not replace the commandments. The commandments show what love does and does not do. Read more in *Faith in daily life*.

### Do the commandments make a person right with God?

No. Go back to the order of Exodus 20:2: the rescue comes before the words. The New Testament says the same thing about everyone. Being made right with God (what the Bible calls justification) is a gift of grace, God's kindness that no one earns, received by faith in Christ. Paul says the law shows us our sin. He would not have known coveting if the law had not said "Do not covet" (Romans 7:7). The confession this site writes under puts it plainly: "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord" (article IV, salvation). Read more in *The good news: how a person is made right with God* and *Saved by grace: how God forgives and changes us*.

### How do the ten apply to Christians now?

This question belongs to *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice*, and here is its answer in brief. The Baptist Faith and Message does not say how the law of Moses applies to a Christian. Baptists hold three main readings. The first divides the law into moral, ceremonial and civil parts, with the moral law binding still; the Second London Baptist Confession of 1677 (reissued in 1689) teaches this. The second holds that the whole law is fulfilled in Christ and comes to Christians through His teaching and His apostles; this is known as new covenant theology or progressive covenantalism. The third is the dispensational reading. [check: quote the Leviticus study's own summary sentence here once that study is final]

All three agree that no one is saved by keeping the law, and that the commands against murder, adultery, theft, false witness and coveting still describe how love treats a neighbour, since Romans 13:9 names them. They differ most over the fourth word. Read more in *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice*.

**Consider:** Where does Jesus say the commands against murder and adultery reach? Which of the ten does Paul name in Romans 13:9?

**Reflect:** Is there anger or desire in you that you have excused because it never became an act? What would loving your neighbour as yourself ask of you in one relationship this week?

## 05 The first day: the Lord's Day and the Sabbath

**Main readings:** Exodus 20:8–11; Deuteronomy 5:12–15; Mark 2:23–28

The word Sabbath comes from a Hebrew word that means to stop or to rest. In the Bible it is the seventh day of the week, which runs from sunset on Friday to sunset on Saturday. The Lord's Day is the name Christians give to the first day, Sunday. This part asks how the two belong together.

### Two reasons for one day

Exodus grounds the day in creation:

> For in six days the LORD made the heavens and the earth and the sea and all that is in them, but on the seventh day He rested. Therefore the LORD blessed the Sabbath day and set it apart as holy.

Exodus 20:11 · BSB

This looks back to the first week: "Then God blessed the seventh day and sanctified it, because on that day He rested from all the work of creation that He had accomplished" (Genesis 2:3). To sanctify is to set something apart as holy, belonging to God. Read more in *Genesis 1–11: the questions readers bring*.

Deuteronomy gives a second reason, the rescue:

> Remember that you were a slave in the land of Egypt, and that the LORD your God brought you out of there with a mighty hand and an outstretched arm. That is why the LORD your God has commanded you to keep the Sabbath day.

Deuteronomy 5:15 · BSB

Both lists widen the rest beyond the householder, to children, servants, animals and "the foreigner within your gates", and Deuteronomy says why: "so that your manservant and maidservant may rest as you do" (Deuteronomy 5:14). People who had been slaves were not to make others work without stopping. The day is a gift, and it is also a matter of justice.

### Shabbat in Jewish practice

For Jewish families the Sabbath, Shabbat, is kept as a gift and a joy. It begins at sundown on Friday, when candles are lit and a blessing called kiddush is said over wine. The household rests from the kinds of work the rabbis gathered into thirty-nine categories (Mishnah, Shabbat 7:2), and it ends at nightfall on Saturday with a short ceremony of separation called havdalah. The prophet called the day "a delight" (Isaiah 58:13). The Jewish teacher Abraham Joshua Heschel called it "a palace in time" [check: Heschel, *The Sabbath*, 1951, page].

### Lord of the Sabbath

Mark tells of one Sabbath in Galilee. The disciples were picking heads of grain as they walked (Mark 2:23). Some Pharisees objected (Mark 2:24). The Pharisees were a Jewish movement known for careful keeping of the law of Moses and of the traditions that applied it to daily life, and picking grain could count as reaping, one of the kinds of work set aside on the day. This was a debate among Jewish teachers about how to honour a day they all loved.

Jesus answers from Israel's own Scriptures. When David and his men were hungry, they ate the consecrated bread from the house of God, which was lawful only for the priests (Mark 2:25–26; 1 Samuel 21:1–6). Then He gives His ruling:

> Then Jesus declared, “The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath. Therefore, the Son of Man is Lord even of the Sabbath.”

Mark 2:27–28 · BSB

"Son of Man" is the name Jesus most often uses for Himself, drawn from Daniel's vision of one given everlasting rule (Daniel 7:13–14). He does not abolish the day. He claims it as His own and says what it is for: the good of people. In the next scene He asks, "Which is lawful on the Sabbath: to do good or to do evil, to save life or to destroy it?" (Mark 3:4). Read more in *Encounters with Jesus*. Later Jewish teaching says plainly that saving a life sets the Sabbath aside (Mishnah, Yoma 8:6).

[care] Mark says David's meal happened "During the high priesthood of Abiathar" (Mark 2:26), while 1 Samuel 21 names Ahimelech, Abiathar's father, as the priest who gave the bread. Abiathar was alive at the time, escaped to David soon afterwards (1 Samuel 22:20) and became the better-known high priest of David's reign. Some readers note that Mark's phrasing can mean "in the passage about Abiathar", the way Mark 12:26 speaks of "the passage about the bush" [check: confirm that William Lane, *The Gospel according to Mark*, 1974, takes this view, and the page]. The confession this site writes under holds Scripture to be "truth, without any mixture of error" (article I, the Scriptures), and this site reads the two passages together.

### The first day

The New Testament shows the churches gathering on a new day. "On the first day of the week we came together to break bread" (Acts 20:7). "On the first day of every week, each of you should set aside a portion of his income" (1 Corinthians 16:2). John writes, "On the Lord’s day I was in the Spirit" (Revelation 1:10). The first day is the day Jesus rose from the dead. Paul also tells believers not to let others judge them over the old day:

> Therefore let no one judge you by what you eat or drink, or with regard to a feast, a New Moon, or a Sabbath. These are a shadow of the things to come, but the body that casts it belongs to Christ.

Colossians 2:16–17 · BSB

Read more in *Christ in the Old Testament*.

So, in the site's own voice and with the Baptist Faith and Message: the first day of the week is the Lord's Day, "a Christian institution for regular observance". It "commemorates the resurrection of Christ from the dead" and is a day for worship, both public and private. Its activities should be "commensurate with the Christian's conscience under the Lordship of Jesus Christ" (article VIII, the Lord's Day).

### The range among Baptists, and beyond

Within that line, Southern Baptists have not always asked the same thing. The 1963 Baptist Faith and Message called for "resting from secular employments", works of necessity and mercy excepted, and the 1925 confession had asked the same [check: the exact wording of the 1925 article]. The Second London Baptist Confession of 1677, reissued in 1689, goes further. It teaches that the Sabbath was changed at the resurrection to the first day, to be kept "as the Christian Sabbath", with rest from ordinary work and the whole day given to worship (chapter 22, paragraphs 7–8) [check: spelling against the site's copy]. The 2000 confession leaves how strictly ordinary work and leisure are set aside to conscience, and this site does not choose between those who keep Sunday strictly and those who keep it more freely (Romans 14:5–6).

[other] Seventh Day Baptists are Baptists who baptize believers and govern their churches by congregation, but keep the seventh day as the Sabbath. They have churches in England from the seventeenth century and in America from 1671, and they are not part of the Southern Baptist Convention [check: dates, and the name of their present general conference]. They are fellow Baptists who differ on this point.

[other] Seventh-day Adventists are a separate tradition. Their own statement, Fundamental Belief 20, "The Sabbath", begins: "The beneficent Creator, after the six days of Creation, rested on the seventh day and instituted the Sabbath for all people as a memorial of Creation." [check: wording and number against adventist.org] They teach that the fourth commandment binds Christians to keep the seventh day. That is their teaching, not this site's.

### Must Christians keep Saturday?

This site answers no. The reasons are Jesus' claim to be Lord of the Sabbath (Mark 2:28), the churches gathering on the first day (Acts 20:7; Revelation 1:10), and Paul's word that the Sabbath is "a shadow" whose body "belongs to Christ" (Colossians 2:17). The gift beneath the command still stands, though: rest from work, time given to God, and rest for others too. A Christian who keeps Saturday out of conscience is not to be judged for it (Colossians 2:16; Romans 14:5–6), and neither is the one who does not.

## Rescue first, then the words

Close: You have counted the ten three ways, read them in two tables, heard Jesus take them to the heart, and followed one of them from the seventh day to the first. Through all of it the opening sentence has stood: "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out" (Exodus 20:2).

Way: Here is the habit to carry to the next command you read, in either Testament. Before you ask what it requires, ask who is speaking and what He has already done. Then read the command as the way a rescued people lives with the God who rescued them, and with each other. The order matters: commands that come after the rescue are a life to live, not a price to pay.

Invite: This is this site's own word to you, not a quotation. If you have read the ten and found that you have not kept them, the commandments have done what Paul said they do. They show the need. They do not meet it. The good news, which is what the word gospel means, is that Jesus Christ kept the law, died for those who broke it, and rose again on the first day. Repent, which means to turn from your sin to God, and trust Him as Lord. The One who brought Israel out of slavery can bring you out too.

Question: Which of the ten do you most often treat as a rule to keep, and how would it sound if you heard it after "I brought you out"?
