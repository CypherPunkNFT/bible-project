# Humanity, sin and renewal

Concepts: Made · Fallen · Renewed · Waiting

Motto: Dust and image. / Both are true of you.

Lead: Every person you will ever meet was made in God's image, has gone wrong, and can be made new only by Christ. Most of us hold one of those truths and drop another: we flatter people, or we write them off. This study walks six passages, from the garden to the redemption of the body, and teaches you to read any person with three questions: what does this passage honour, what will it not excuse, and where does it point them?

> So God created man in His own image; in the image of God He created him; male and female He created them.

Genesis 1:27 · BSB

Frame: The line goes by what each passage is mostly about: the wrong, or God's answer. Its height is not anyone's worth. It starts with God's blessing, goes down through the garden to every mouth silenced, turns at one sentence about one man, and goes on toward a body not yet redeemed. Choose any point on the line. Each passage opens with its words, and then keeps apart what the text says, what this study reads in it, and what it asks of you.

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. These six passages carry the image, the fall, universal sin, Adam and Christ, renewal and the body. Named and not walked here: the making of the woman (Genesis 2:18–25), the flood (Genesis 6:5–8), Paul's struggle in Romans 7 (Romans 7:14–25), and the verse on head coverings that also says image (1 Corinthians 11:7). Other accounts of this doctrine divide it differently. How long ago and by what steps God made the world is not handled here: the Baptist Faith and Message does not fix a timeline, and Baptists differ.

## 01 In the image of God

Slug: image

Hook: Who is made in God's image? Before anyone has done anything, the answer is: them.

Line: God blesses male and female alike, and James says that likeness still stops your tongue.

Read: Genesis 1:26–31; James 3:8–12

Setting: The sixth day, and then a letter to believers whose same mouths bless and curse (James 3:10).

Honours: Every person, before any achievement: "in the image of God He created him; male and female He created them" (Genesis 1:27).

Refuses: Contempt that sounds religious: "With the tongue we bless our Lord and Father, and with it we curse men" (James 3:9).

Points to: The Maker, whose verdict comes first: "indeed, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31).

### Male and female He created them

God says, "Let Us make man in Our image, after Our likeness" (Genesis 1:26), and the next verse says who that means.

The word covers male and female, and the blessing and the charge that follow are spoken to them together: "God blessed them and said to them" (Genesis 1:28). Nothing in the passage ties the image to rank, wealth, strength or usefulness. Paul tells Athens that "From one man He made every nation of men" (Acts 17:26).

[read] So the image is given before you could earn it, and no one's opinion of you can take it away. Baptists confess that "every person of every race possesses full dignity and is worthy of respect and Christian love" (Article 3), and contend for "the sanctity of all human life from conception to natural death" (Article 15).

To rule the creatures is a charge given inside a world God calls good. It is no licence to despise what He made (Genesis 1:26–31). The Baptist Faith and Message adds that "The gift of gender is thus part of the goodness of God's creation".

### Made in God's likeness

James is not writing about the garden. He is writing about ordinary speech. "With the tongue we bless our Lord and Father, and with it we curse men, who have been made in God's likeness" (James 3:9). The people being cursed are fallen people, and James still calls them made in God's likeness. Genesis does the same after the flood: "for in His own image God has made mankind" (Genesis 9:6).

"Out of the same mouth come blessing and cursing. My brothers, this should not be!" (James 3:10).

[read] So sin does not cancel the reason to honour someone, and worship does not excuse contempt.

[care] Honouring a person does not mean approving what they do. James names cursing as evil in the same breath, and Genesis 9:6 is a verse about murder. Both reasons stand together.

Look closely: Who does Genesis apply the image to? Why does James bring that likeness into a paragraph about the tongue?

Your response: Think of someone you find easy to dismiss. What would change in the way you speak of them if James 3:9 were in the room?

## 02 I was afraid, so I hid

Slug: hid

Hook: God's first words to the man after the fall are not a sentence. They are a question.

Line: A question about God's word, a taking, a hiding, and God still coming to look for them.

Read: Genesis 3:1–24

Setting: A garden where "they were not ashamed" (Genesis 2:25), and one command with a warning (Genesis 2:16–17).

Honours: They are still worth seeking: "But the LORD God called out to the man" (Genesis 3:9).

Refuses: Passing it on: "The woman whom You gave me" and "The serpent deceived me" are recorded, and neither one ends the matter (Genesis 3:12–13).

Points to: The God who still provides for them: "He clothed them" (Genesis 3:21).

### Did God really say

The serpent begins with a question about God's word: "Did God really say" (Genesis 3:1). Then a flat denial: "You will not surely die" (Genesis 3:4). The woman sees the tree "good for food and pleasing to the eyes" and "desirable for obtaining wisdom", and she takes and eats, and gives to her husband "who was with her" (Genesis 3:6).

[read] So the sin begins as distrust of what God said, before anyone's hand moves.

Their eyes do open, as promised, and what they see is that they are naked. They make their own covering, "fig leaves" (Genesis 3:7), and then they hide.

> "I heard Your voice in the garden," he replied, "and I was afraid because I was naked; so I hid myself."

Genesis 3:10 · BSB

### Dust you are

Then the order of the chapter turns around. God questions the man, then the woman, and each points on. He does not question the serpent. He speaks to the serpent first, then the woman, then the man (Genesis 3:9–19). The figure here sets the two orders one above the other. The consequences reach pain, the marriage, the ground, work and death: "For dust you are, and to dust you shall return" (Genesis 3:19).

The same chapter has both of these: God clothes them (Genesis 3:21), and He guards the way to the tree of life (Genesis 3:24). Neither should be cut to make the story simpler.

[care] "he will rule over you" (Genesis 3:16) is said as part of what the fall brought. It gives no one a licence for cruelty, and it is not a picture of what a marriage is for. Article 18 of the Baptist Faith and Message and the study Faith in daily life take up marriage itself. The text does not name the fruit or call it an apple, and it does not say why God used skin. This study builds nothing on the fruit or the skin. It does notice that God is the one who clothes them, and that clothes come back later in this study. That link is this study's picture.

[care] "He will crush your head" (Genesis 3:15) is not quoted outright in the New Testament, though Paul uses the same picture: "The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet" (Romans 16:20). This study reads Genesis 3:15 as the first promise of the One to come, and that is a reading, not something the verse spells out.

[source:henry-gen] Matthew Henry reads it so: "A gracious promise is here made of Christ, as the deliverer of fallen man from the power of Satan".

Look closely: What does the serpent promise, and what happens instead? Whose name does each person give when asked?

Your response: Where are you explaining the influences behind something you did, so that you do not have to say you did it? What would answering "Where are you?" (Genesis 3:9) sound like?

## 03 All under sin

Slug: under-sin

Hook: Paul's readers are ready to agree about someone else. Then he reads them a list that starts at the throat.

Line: Jews and Greeks alike, every mouth silenced, and then the first words of the answer: But now.

Read: Romans 3:9–26

Setting: Paul has been working through Gentile and Jew and asks, "Are we any better?" (Romans 3:9).

Honours: The sinner, as someone God is willing to justify: "justified freely by His grace" (Romans 3:24).

Refuses: Any standing outside the accusation: "Not at all" (Romans 3:9), and "there is no one who does good, not even one" (Romans 3:12).

Points to: Christ: "this righteousness from God comes through faith in Jesus Christ to all who believe" (Romans 3:22).

### Every mouth silenced

Paul answers his own question by quoting Scripture, mostly the Psalms and Isaiah, one line after another (not always word for word). Near the end the lines name parts of the body, and the figure called One body, two uses sets them out. The point of the chain comes in the next sentence: "so that every mouth may be silenced and the whole world held accountable to God" (Romans 3:19). The law "merely brings awareness of sin" (Romans 3:20).

> for all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God,

Romans 3:23 · BSB

[read] So this passage is a poor foundation for feeling better than some other group. You are inside the sentence.

[care] Paul does write "they have together become worthless" (Romans 3:12), which can sound as if a person has no worth. Psalm 14 says "corrupt" at that point (Psalm 14:3). The line describes what people turned away from God have made of their lives. It does not give God's estimate of what they are worth to Him: "While we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8). Nor does the list say every person commits every wrong to the same degree. It says no one stands clear.

### But now

Next comes "But now, apart from the law, the righteousness of God has been revealed" (Romans 3:21). The verdict and the rescue are in one paragraph. The verdict falls on all, and the gift reaches "all who believe" (Romans 3:22). They "are justified freely by His grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus" (Romans 3:24). How that works belongs to the studies Grace, faith and salvation and What the cross accomplishes. Here, notice only that Paul does not stop at the diagnosis.

[read] So hold the passages together. Genesis gives the ground for dignity. James rules out contempt. Romans confronts the whole world with sin. You do not have to pretend someone has done no wrong in order to honour them, and you do not have to deny their dignity in order to name the wrong.

Look closely: What does Paul's opening question do to the way you hear the quotations? Where does the argument go once every mouth is silenced?

Your response: When you read Romans 3, do you hear a verdict on other people first, or a reason you need grace yourself?

## 04 The obedience of the one man

Slug: one-man

Hook: Paul traces sin and death to one man, and grace to One Man. Then he says it is not an even match.

Line: Adam and Christ, set side by side, and Paul says more than once that the two are not equal.

Read: Romans 5:12–21; 1 Corinthians 15:22

Setting: Paul has just said "While we were still sinners, Christ died for us" (Romans 5:8), and he now sets out how far that reaches.

Honours: One human family, from one man: "From one man He made every nation of men" (Acts 17:26).

Refuses: The idea that sin is a set of separate accidents: "sin entered the world through one man, and death through sin" (Romans 5:12).

Points to: The One whose gift is larger: "where sin increased, grace increased all the more" (Romans 5:20).

### Death reigned

Paul traces sin and death to "one man", and he says death "reigned from Adam until Moses" (Romans 5:12–14). He adds "because all sinned" (Romans 5:12), and he calls Adam "a pattern of the One to come" (Romans 5:14).

[read] So humanity's trouble is bigger than a list of private mistakes. Paul treats Adam as one man with a place in history, in the same sentence as Moses, and this site reads him so.

> For just as through the disobedience of the one man the many were made sinners, so also through the obedience of the one man the many will be made righteous.

Romans 5:19 · BSB

### How much more

Paul sets this out so carefully that the figure on this page can simply lay it side by side. Twice he says "how much more", and at the end "all the more" (Romans 5:15–20). The comparison exists to serve what Christ accomplishes, so do not stop reading it before it gets there. It ends on "eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord" (Romans 5:21).

[care] "justification and life for all men" (Romans 5:18) does not mean everyone is saved whatever they do. Just before it the gift is for "those who receive an abundance of grace" (Romans 5:17), and Baptists confess that "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord". This study does not settle how "all" and "many" (Romans 5:19) fit together beyond that, and it does not rest anything on the disputed wording at the end of Romans 5:12.

Look closely: Which contrasts does Paul repeat? Where does he say the two sides are not equal?

Your response: When you think about what is wrong with people, how much room does the gift take up in your account? Does it take up as much as it does in Paul's?

## 05 Renewed in the image

Slug: renewed

Hook: Take off, put on. Paul describes the new life the way you would describe getting dressed.

Line: Raised with Christ, so take off the old and put on the new, in ordinary rooms with ordinary people.

Read: Colossians 3:1–17

Setting: A letter to believers who are told, "you died, and your life is now hidden with Christ in God" (Colossians 3:3).

Honours: The renewed person, in terms taken from creation: "being renewed in knowledge in the image of its Creator" (Colossians 3:10).

Refuses: Calling the old ways small: "Because of these, the wrath of God is coming" (Colossians 3:6).

Points to: Christ as the whole of it: "Christ is all and is in all" (Colossians 3:11).

### Take it off

The commands come after the identity. "Therefore, since you have been raised with Christ" (Colossians 3:1) comes first, and "Put to death" comes after (Colossians 3:5). Paul lists "sexual immorality, impurity, lust, evil desires, and greed" (Colossians 3:5), then "anger, rage, malice, slander, and filthy language" (Colossians 3:8). And he adds one reason in the language of clothes: "you have taken off the old self" (Colossians 3:9).

> and have put on the new self, which is being renewed in knowledge in the image of its Creator.

Colossians 3:10 · BSB

[care] "Put to death" (Colossians 3:5) means an end to these practices. It is not a call to hurt your body, and your body is not the enemy, as the next passage says. If you are thinking of harming yourself, tell someone today, and contact emergency services or a crisis line.

### Put it on

Paul's next word is "clothe yourselves with hearts of compassion, kindness, humility, gentleness, and patience" (Colossians 3:12), and elsewhere "clothe yourselves with the Lord Jesus Christ" (Romans 13:14).

[read] This study's picture: the first clothes in the Bible were home-made, "they sewed together fig leaves" (Genesis 3:7), and the next were God's, "He clothed them" (Genesis 3:21). The likeness is in the Berean's English only, a similar English word, and Paul does not cite Genesis here.

"Forgive as the Lord forgave you" (Colossians 3:13). "And over all these virtues put on love" (Colossians 3:14). Paul writes "there is no Greek or Jew" (Colossians 3:11), and asks this of whatever you do next, "in word or deed" (Colossians 3:17).

[read] So renewal happens among other people. Paul does not pair each thing off with one thing on. That is a reader's work, and the response below asks you to do it.

Look closely: What comes first in the passage, the commands or the identity? What are the readers told to wear?

Your response: Choose one thing Paul says to take off, and one thing he says to put on in its place. Where, this week, is the room you would do that in?

## 06 The redemption of our bodies

Slug: bodies

Hook: You have the Spirit, and you still groan. Paul says that is what it looks like to be waiting.

Line: Your body belongs to the Lord now, and it is promised a future.

Read: 1 Corinthians 6:12–20; Romans 8:18–25

Setting: A church saying "Everything is permissible for me" (1 Corinthians 6:12), and a letter written to believers under "present sufferings" (Romans 8:18).

Honours: The body itself: "your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit" (1 Corinthians 6:19).

Refuses: Treating the body as if it did not matter: "You are not your own; you were bought at a price" (1 Corinthians 6:19–20).

Points to: The hope ahead: "we wait eagerly for our adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies" (Romans 8:23).

### Glorify God with your body

Corinth had a slogan, and Paul answers it from the resurrection: "By His power God raised the Lord from the dead, and He will raise us also" (1 Corinthians 6:14). His argument against sexual immorality rests on belonging: "your bodies are members of Christ" (1 Corinthians 6:15). "Therefore glorify God with your body" (1 Corinthians 6:20).

[read] So since the body has a future, what you do with it now matters. Paul does not treat the body as a bad container to be got rid of, and nothing here makes looks or ability the measure of anyone's worth.

[care] This passage is about what a person chooses to do. If someone has done something to your body against your will, that is their sin against you, not yours, and this passage does not call you defiled. Getting to safety and telling someone you trust does not go against anything here.

### Waiting in hope

"The creation waits in eager expectation" (Romans 8:19). It is "groaning together", but in the "pains of childbirth" (Romans 8:22), so the groaning is going somewhere. And it is not only the creation.

> Not only that, but we ourselves, who have the firstfruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait eagerly for our adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies.

Romans 8:23 · BSB

[read] Having the Spirit does not mean the suffering has stopped. Hope and grief, weakness and unfinished change, can live in one person.

Paul's word for the meantime is "we wait for it patiently" (Romans 8:25). Then he names where it ends: those God foreknew He "predestined to be conformed to the image of His Son" (Romans 8:29). And to the Corinthians: "so also shall we bear the likeness of the heavenly man" (1 Corinthians 15:49).

[care] An illness or a disability is no evidence that someone has sinned more, or has less of God's grace. Asked about a man born blind, Jesus says "Neither this man nor his parents sinned" (John 9:3). The fuller account of the resurrection and the new creation is in the study The hope to come.

Look closely: Why does Paul bring the resurrection into an argument about conduct? What do believers who have the Spirit still wait for?

Your response: Who is weak or in pain near you, or is it you? How would hope for the body change the way you care for that person this week?

## Part: reach | How does it reach us? | Baptists differ.

Lead: Paul says the many were made sinners through one man's disobedience (Romans 5:19). He does not spell out how that passes from Adam to you. Here are the answers given by the sources read for this page, all of them from people who agree that every person sins and that only Christ saves.

### What the confession says

Article 3 of the Baptist Faith and Message says Adam's descendants "inherit a nature and an environment inclined toward sin", and that "as soon as they are capable of moral action, they become transgressors and are under condemnation". It says "Only the grace of God can bring man into His holy fellowship". This is what this site holds. It does not say whether Adam's own guilt is counted as ours. On that, Baptists differ, and this study does not choose.

### Three positions, four voices

[source:boyce] Adam's guilt counts as ours because he stood for us. James P. Boyce, first president of the Southern Baptist seminary, says the race "sinned in him, not consciously, but representatively". He argues it from the parallel with Christ in Romans 5.

[source:london] The Second London Confession, a Baptist confession of 1677, says much the same: "the guilt of the Sin was imputed, and corrupted nature conveyed". Imputed means counted as theirs, and conveyed means passed down.

[source:abstract] Corruption, and condemnation with it. The Abstract of Principles of 1858, the founding confession of the same seminary, says Adam's posterity "inherit a nature corrupt and wholly opposed to God and His law, are under condemnation", and it adds "as soon as they are capable of moral action, become actual transgressors". It does not use the word imputed.

[own:traditional] Guilt only for your own sin. A 2012 statement by a group of Southern Baptist pastors and professors, never adopted by the convention, says that everyone inherits a nature and an environment inclined toward sin and that all who can act morally will sin, but it denies that anyone is guilty before they have personally sinned. Only an extract of it was seen for this page, so it is told here in this site's words.

[open] The confession does not go on to say what becomes of those who die before they are "capable of moral action". Neither does this study.

### Other traditions, labelled as theirs

[other:westminster] Westminster (Presbyterian, 1647): "the guilt of this sin was imputed, and the same death in sin and corrupted nature conveyed to all their posterity".

[other:trent] The Council of Trent (Roman Catholic, 1546): Adam's sin is "transfused into all by propagation, not by imitation", and Trent ties its remedy to baptism, infants included. Baptists disagree on that last point. Baptism is for those who already believe, and it does not take away sin: it pictures what Christ has done (Romans 6:4).

[other:orthodox] The Longer Catechism of the Orthodox Church (1839): "As from an infected source there naturally flows an infected stream". The stress falls on death and infection passed down, "and all sin themselves".

## Part: image | What is the image? | Scripture says who before it says what.

Lead: No verse defines the image of God, and Genesis names who bears it before anything else. So two questions stay on the table, and this page reports the answers it read.

### What is it?

The Baptist Faith and Message says man is "made in His own image", and does not define it further. Two answers read for this page follow, and they do not wholly exclude each other.

[source:boyce] A spiritual nature. Boyce: "That image and likeness consists in the possession of a spiritual nature", meaning a person who knows, chooses, and can say ought. He says "There is certainly no reference to the bodily form of man", and he takes the rule over creatures as "an office conferred upon the man made in God's image".

[source:calvin-gen] Chiefly righteousness and holiness. Calvin, reading from what is restored in Colossians 3:10: "we may judge from its restoration what it originally had been". He calls ruling the creatures "some portion, though very small, of the image of God". He is Reformed, not Baptist.

[open] Genesis 1:26 joins the image and the charge in one sentence: "to rule over the fish of the sea". Boyce and Calvin both set that join below the image itself. Whether it should count for more is a question this page leaves open. No writer who argues that it should was read for it.

### Was it lost?

Scripture says both things, and each needs saying. The image is still the ground for honouring people after the fall (Genesis 9:6; James 3:9). And it is "being renewed" (Colossians 3:10), which means something needs renewing.

[source:boyce] Boyce: "That the whole image was not destroyed by the sin of Adam". He says original righteousness was lost and is restored in Christ.

[source:calvin-gen] Calvin uses a stronger word on Genesis 1, "the image of God had been destroyed in us by the fall". But on Genesis 9:6 he says "there yet exists some remnant of it, so that man is possessed of no small dignity".

This site says this much and no more. No person has ever lost the dignity of being made in God's image, and no person can restore the likeness by their own strength.

## Close: Read people with three questions

Close: This study began with God blessing male and female, and it ends with a body not yet redeemed. It runs through a garden and a silenced mouth, and it turns on one man's obedience.

Way: Take the three questions with you to the next person you read about, in Scripture or the news or your own family. What does the passage honour in them? What will it not excuse? Where does it point them? Then ask the same of yourself.

Invite: If you have never come to Christ, this is where the study points you too. You were made in God's image, you have sinned and fall short, and you cannot make yourself new. "if you confess with your mouth" that Jesus is Lord "and believe in your heart that God raised Him from the dead, you will be saved" (Romans 10:9).

Question: Who do you find it easiest to write off, and who do you find it easiest to excuse? What would it take to honour the one and tell the truth to the other?

Again: Read the passage again before you answer.
