# Humanity sin and renewal

**Study 29 · God and Christian Faith · First content draft · 2026-10-13**

Scripture speaks of people made in God's image, accountable for wrongdoing, and in need of the grace revealed in Christ. These claims belong together. Human dignity does not make sin harmless, and the reality of sin does not give us permission to treat another person as worthless.

The readings begin with creation and the disobedience in the garden, then follow Paul's account of sin and the contrast between Adam and Christ. They lead to renewal in the Creator's image and the hope of bodily redemption. The question is both what has gone wrong and what kind of life God's saving work restores.

## 01 In the image of God

**Main readings:** Genesis 1:26–31; James 3:8–12.

### Male and female created he them

Genesis describes God's purpose to make humanity in his image and likeness, with responsibility over other living creatures. The account then states:

> So God created man in his own image, in the image of God created he him; male and female created he them.

Genesis 1:27 · KJV

The image language includes male and female. The blessing and commission that follow are addressed to them together. Human life appears within the creation God declares very good, with food, fruitfulness, and responsibility for life on the earth.

The passage does not make the image depend on rank, wealth, strength, or usefulness to another person. It describes humanity's relation to its Creator. That provides a reason to recognise dignity before measuring someone's achievements or deciding whether we agree with them.

Dominion is given within a world that belongs to God and is described as good. The responsibility should be read in relation to that setting, rather than as a reason to despise what God has made.

### Made after the similitude of God

James brings God's likeness into an argument about speech. The same tongue blesses God and curses people made after his likeness. James says this should not be so, illustrating the contradiction through a fountain producing different kinds of water.

His appeal concerns people in the world his readers inhabit, not only humanity before disobedience. The likeness of God remains a reason to reject contemptuous speech. Acknowledging human sin does not remove that reason.

The connection is demanding: speech directed toward God and speech directed toward people cannot be separated into unrelated moral compartments. Worship does not excuse treating God's creatures with contempt.

**Consider:** To whom does Genesis apply the image language? Why does James bring that likeness into his teaching about the tongue?

**Reflect:** Consider someone you find easy to dismiss. What would change in your speech if you kept James's stated reason in view?

## 02 I was afraid and I hid myself

**Main reading:** Genesis 3:1–24.

### Hath God said

The serpent questions God's instruction and contradicts the warning of death. The woman sees the fruit as desirable for food, sight, and wisdom; she takes and eats, gives to her husband, and he eats. The story identifies an act of disobedience within a conversation about God's word and trustworthiness.

The opening of their eyes leads to awareness of nakedness and an attempt to cover themselves. When they hear God, they hide. Adam explains his response:

> And he said, I heard thy voice in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked; and I hid myself.

Genesis 3:10 · KJV

The hoped-for gain is followed by shame and fear. The questioning then exposes what they have done. Adam points to the woman God gave him; the woman points to the serpent's deception. The narrative records the explanations without making the disobedience disappear.

### Unto dust shalt thou return

God's words address the serpent, the woman, and the man. The account describes enmity, pain, strained relationship, a cursed ground, toil, and return to dust. The consequences reach the conditions of human life rather than ending with a private feeling of regret.

God also clothes the man and woman before they are sent from the garden. The story closes with the way to the tree of life guarded. Judgment and God's provision of clothing both remain part of the account; neither should be removed to make the story simpler.

The chapter gives a narrative of transgression and its consequences. Questions about how that condition relates to every descendant receive further treatment in later Scripture and Christian doctrine. Begin by observing the movement here: mistrust, disobedience, hiding, questioning, and exclusion.

**Consider:** How does the result differ from the serpent's assurance? What do the responses to God's questions reveal?

**Reflect:** Consider whether explaining the influences behind a wrong action has become a way of avoiding responsibility for the action itself.

## 03 All under sin

**Main reading:** Romans 3:9–26.

### Every mouth may be stopped

Paul asks whether one group is better than another and answers that Jews and Gentiles are all under sin. His chain of scriptural descriptions names failures of understanding, speech, conduct, and the fear of God. Sin reaches across the person's life.

The argument brings the whole world under accountability before God. The law gives knowledge of sin; it does not provide grounds for a person to pronounce themselves righteous through comparison with someone else.

> For all have sinned, and come short of the glory of God;

Romans 3:23 · KJV

Paul's conclusion takes away the advantage of standing outside the accusation and directing it only toward others. The reader is included in the need being described. The passage is therefore a poor foundation for a claim of personal or group superiority.

### Justified freely by his grace

The argument turns toward God's righteousness, redemption in Christ, and justification by grace. The declaration of universal sin belongs to a passage that also announces God's saving action. Paul does not leave the reader with wrongdoing as the final word about what God can do.

Accountability and grace are not alternatives in this account. The need for grace is made clear by the reality of sin; the gift of grace prevents that diagnosis from becoming a claim that renewal is impossible.

Read the different biblical claims together. Genesis gives a basis for dignity, James rules out contempt, and Romans confronts the whole world with sin. Recognising another person's dignity does not require pretending they have done no wrong. Naming wrongdoing does not require denying that dignity.

**Consider:** How does Paul's opening question shape the use of his quotations? Where does the argument move after establishing human accountability?

**Reflect:** Consider whether you hear the passage first as a verdict on other people or as a reason for your own dependence on grace.

## 04 By the obedience of one

**Main reading:** Romans 5:12–21.

### Sin and death

Paul connects the entry of sin and death with one man, then describes death reigning from Adam to Moses. He calls Adam a figure of the one to come and develops a comparison with Jesus Christ.

The comparison includes offence and gift, condemnation and justification, disobedience and obedience. It also contains repeated contrasts: the gift is not simply equal to the offence, and grace is described as abounding.

> For as by one man’s disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous.

Romans 5:19 · KJV

The account is larger than a collection of unrelated individual mistakes. It connects humanity's condition with Adam while locating righteousness and life in Christ. The paragraph's movement is toward the reign of grace through righteousness unto eternal life.

### Much more the grace of God

Christ's action is the centre of the hope Paul describes. The passage speaks of receiving abundant grace and the gift of righteousness. Its treatment of Adam serves the explanation of what Christ accomplishes; the comparison should not be stopped before it reaches that purpose.

Christian accounts differ over inherited sin and its relation to human freedom. Westminster describes Adam's guilt as imputed to his descendants and corruption conveyed to them; it says fallen people cannot convert themselves by their own strength and require God's renewing grace. See [chapters 6 and 9](https://opc.org/wcf.html).

The Catholic Catechism describes original sin as an inherited deprivation of original holiness, not a personally committed fault in Adam's descendants. It describes human nature as wounded and inclined to sin, and rejects the claim that people can live rightly without necessary divine grace. See [paragraphs 404–406](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_one/article_1/paragraph_7_the_fall.index.html).

These formulations differ and do not exhaust Christian interpretation. They should not be reduced to one side taking sin seriously and another denying the need for grace. The first task in Romans remains following Paul's comparison and its announcement of life through Christ.

**Consider:** Which contrasts does Paul repeat? Why does the paragraph say much more when describing grace?

**Reflect:** Consider whether your understanding of humanity's need gives equal attention to the gift Paul announces in Christ.

## 05 Renewed in knowledge

**Main reading:** Colossians 3:1–17.

### After the image of him that created him

Colossians begins with life in relation to the risen Christ. The readers are directed to seek what is above, where Christ is, and are told that their life is hidden with him in God. That identity leads into commands about their present conduct.

They are to put away sexual wrongdoing, covetousness, anger, malice, harmful speech, and lying. The instruction about lying is linked to putting off the old man with his deeds and putting on the new:

> And have put on the new man, which is renewed in knowledge after the image of him that created him:

Colossians 3:10 · KJV

Renewal is described in relation to the Creator's image. It has visible consequences for what people do and say to one another. The call to seek things above does not lead away from responsibility in ordinary relationships; it leads directly into them.

### Put on therefore

The passage proceeds from what must be put away to mercy, kindness, humility, meekness, patience, and forgiveness. Love binds these together. Christ's forgiveness supplies the pattern for forgiving one another.

The community is called to peace as one body, thankfulness, and the word of Christ dwelling richly among them. Teaching, admonishing, singing, and doing everything in Jesus' name belong to this account of renewed life.

The verse naming Greeks and Jews, circumcision and uncircumcision, slaves and free people refuses to make those distinctions the basis of superior standing in the new humanity. Christ is central to its identity. Renewal includes the way people who differ are taught to live together.

**Consider:** How does the opening description of life with Christ lead into the commands? What replaces the practices the readers are told to put away?

**Reflect:** Choose one harmful pattern named in the passage and the positive practice that should take its place. Consider a concrete occasion for that change.

## 06 The redemption of our body

**Main readings:** 1 Corinthians 6:12–20; Romans 8:18–25.

### The body is for the Lord

First Corinthians addresses bodily conduct in relation to belonging to Christ. Paul refuses the conclusion that a claim of liberty makes every action helpful or that he should be mastered by anything. He then connects the body with the Lord and with resurrection.

The argument against sexual immorality appeals to the body as a member of Christ and a temple of the Holy Ghost. Believers are described as bought with a price and called to glorify God in their bodies. Their embodied life matters to discipleship.

This reasoning does not treat the material body as an evil container to be discarded. Nor does it make physical appearance or ability the measure of spiritual worth. It directs attention to belonging, conduct, and God's purpose to raise his people.

### Waiting in hope

Romans describes creation groaning and waiting for deliverance from corruption. Believers who have the firstfruits of the Spirit also wait:

> And not only they, but ourselves also, which have the firstfruits of the Spirit, even we ourselves groan within ourselves, waiting for the adoption, to wit, the redemption of our body.

Romans 8:23 · KJV

Possessing the Spirit does not mean the present experience of suffering has already ended. The passage gives language for both genuine renewal and continuing groaning. Its hope concerns redemption of the body, not indifference to it.

Paul follows with patient waiting for what is not yet seen. Hope can therefore coexist with grief, weakness, and unfinished change. Those experiences are not, by themselves, evidence that the person has no share in God's grace.

The readings hold creation, accountability, renewal, and bodily hope together. Humanity is neither self-sufficient nor beyond God's restoring work. The life renewed in Christ is called to honour the Creator now while awaiting the redemption still promised.

**Consider:** Why does Paul bring resurrection into an argument about bodily conduct? What do believers in Romans still await despite having the Spirit?

**Reflect:** Consider how hope for bodily redemption might shape patient care for yourself or another person experiencing weakness.
