# What the cross accomplishes

**Study 25 · God and Christian Faith · First content draft · 2026-10-13**

Christ gave his life a ransom for many. He bore sins, reconciled people to God, and triumphed over principalities and powers. The New Testament describes his death through several connected pictures, each showing something about the need he meets and the life that follows.

These readings follow six of those descriptions. Sacrifice concerns sin, cleansing, and access to God. Redemption speaks of costly deliverance. Reconciliation addresses estrangement. Bearing sins joins Christ's suffering to our righteousness. Victory concerns the powers and bondage he overcomes. Love gives both the source of his coming and the shape of life for those he saves.

## 01 One sacrifice for sins

**Main reading:** Hebrews 9:1–10:25. **Compare:** Romans 3:21–26.

### He offered himself

Hebrews begins its comparison with a sanctuary, priests, blood, and repeated offerings. Its concern includes the worshipper's conscience and the way into God's presence. The old arrangements form the setting in which the writer explains Christ's priesthood and offering.

Christ enters by his own blood and offers himself without spot to God through the eternal Spirit. The offering is personal: the priest does not bring an animal separate from himself. Hebrews connects his blood with a conscience cleansed from dead works to serve the living God, and with the promised eternal inheritance.

The argument then contrasts repeated offerings with Christ's single offering:

> But this man, after he had offered one sacrifice for sins for ever, sat down on the right hand of God;

Hebrews 10:12 · KJV

The contrast concerns what the offerings accomplish. The writer describes Christ as putting away sin by the sacrifice of himself, and says that where sins are remitted there is no further offering for sin. Forgiveness rests on his offering rather than an endless attempt to make another sufficient payment.

### Let us draw near

The next movement is an invitation to approach. Because there is access through Jesus' blood and a high priest over God's house, believers are urged to draw near, hold fast their profession, and encourage one another toward love and good works. The conclusion reaches both worship and shared life.

Romans 3 places sacrifice-related language within an argument about righteousness: God sets forth Christ as a propitiation through faith in his blood, demonstrating his righteousness while justifying the believer. The passage holds God's justice and gracious forgiveness together. Its vocabulary deserves attention within that argument rather than being treated as a self-contained definition of every aspect of the cross.

**Consider:** What does Hebrews say the repeated sacrifices could not accomplish? What invitations follow its description of Christ's offering?

**Reflect:** Consider how access grounded in Christ's offering changes the way you approach God and encourage another believer.

## 02 A ransom for many

**Main readings:** Mark 10:35–45; 1 Peter 1:13–21.

### To give his life

James and John ask for places beside Jesus in glory. Their request leads to teaching about the cup he will drink, the other disciples' indignation, and the difference between exercising lordship and serving. Jesus then explains his own mission:

> For even the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many.

Mark 10:45 · KJV

His life is given for others. The ransom language adds something to the example of service: his death accomplishes a deliverance that benefits the many. At the same time, the setting makes his action the answer to the disciples' contest over greatness.

The sentence does not name a recipient of the ransom. Supplying one requires an argument beyond this verse. Its stated emphasis is the Son of man's purpose, the giving of his life, and those for whom it is given.

### Redeemed with the precious blood of Christ

First Peter contrasts silver and gold with Christ's precious blood, describing him as a lamb without blemish or spot. Here redemption concerns release from the vain manner of life inherited from the readers' fathers. The cost and the former life both matter to the exhortation.

The surrounding instructions call readers to sober hope, obedience, and holiness. Redemption is therefore connected with a changed manner of living. The passage goes on to name God as the one who raised Christ and gave him glory, so that the readers' faith and hope might be in God.

The ransom saying and Peter's appeal connect saving cost with service and holiness. A person cannot follow the argument merely by calculating the value of a payment while leaving the former life and the desire for superiority untouched.

**Consider:** How does Jesus' statement answer the dispute about greatness? From what does Peter say the readers were redeemed?

**Reflect:** Identify a pattern you have treated as inevitable because it is familiar or inherited. Read Peter's call to holiness alongside his description of redemption.

## 03 Reconciled to God

**Main readings:** 2 Corinthians 5:14–21; Ephesians 2:11–22.

### God was in Christ

Paul describes Christ's love as the force directing his ministry. Christ died and rose so that those who live should live for him. The passage moves through new creation to reconciliation, naming God as the one who reconciles through Jesus Christ and entrusts the message to his servants.

> To wit, that God was in Christ, reconciling the world unto himself, not imputing their trespasses unto them; and hath committed unto us the word of reconciliation.

2 Corinthians 5:19 · KJV

God's action addresses trespasses and estrangement. The appeal that follows is to be reconciled to God. The ministry announces and urges a response to God's action; it does not manufacture the action through the messenger's persuasiveness.

Verse 21 places the sinless Christ and our sins at the centre of the explanation. Paul says that God made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin, so that we might become God's righteousness in him. Both statements belong together: the language about sin does not turn Christ into a wrongdoer.

### Both have access

Ephesians addresses Gentiles who had been far off and says they have been brought near by Christ's blood. Its account of peace concerns Jews and Gentiles made one, as well as their reconciliation to God. Christ reconciles both in one body through the cross.

The outcome includes shared access by one Spirit to the Father. Those once described as strangers become fellow citizens and members of God's household. The cross therefore has consequences for the community's boundaries and its understanding of belonging.

This peace cannot be reduced to people becoming more agreeable while remaining estranged from God. Nor can reconciliation to God be treated as permission to rebuild contempt between the people who share access to him. Ephesians locates both changes in Christ's action.

**Consider:** Who initiates reconciliation in Paul's account? What changes for the people addressed in Ephesians?

**Reflect:** Consider whether you treat another believer as an outsider on grounds that conflict with the shared access described here.

## 04 He bare our sins

**Main reading:** 1 Peter 2:18–25. **Compare:** Isaiah 53:1–12.

### In his own body on the tree

Peter addresses servants enduring wrongful suffering. He points to Christ's innocence, refusal to answer reviling with reviling, and trust in the one who judges righteously. Within that account of his example, Peter describes what Christ accomplished:

> Who his own self bare our sins in his own body on the tree, that we, being dead to sins, should live unto righteousness: by whose stripes ye were healed.

1 Peter 2:24 · KJV

The passage echoes Isaiah's account of suffering, wounds, straying sheep, and sins borne by the servant. Peter applies that language to Christ and to the readers' return to the Shepherd and Bishop of their souls. The stated purpose includes living unto righteousness.

The healing language here stands within an argument about sins and returning to the shepherd. It should not be turned into a promise that every reader's physical illness must disappear immediately. Likewise, Christ's endurance does not make the injustice inflicted on him righteous or give a wrongdoer permission to demand another person's suffering.

### Leaving us an example

Peter joins example and sin-bearing in one paragraph. Following Christ's steps concerns the response to wrongful treatment; his bearing our sins concerns what he does for us. The reader is called to follow him without being made the redeemer of someone else.

Later Christian accounts bring these passages together in different ways. The Westminster Confession connects Christ's obedience and sacrifice with satisfying divine justice and securing reconciliation and inheritance for those given him by the Father. See [chapter 8.5](https://opc.org/wcf.html). The Catholic Catechism describes his death as sacrifice, the substitution of his obedience for our disobedience, and satisfaction for sins, grounding its saving value in his love. See [paragraphs 613–616](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_one/section_two/chapter_two/article_4/paragraph_2_jesus_died_crucified.html).

These formulations share several terms while differing in their wider accounts of salvation. Their overlap cautions against assigning sacrifice, substitution, or love exclusively to one Christian tradition. Peter's own paragraph keeps saving action, righteous living, and example together.

**Consider:** What does Christ do for the readers, and what does Peter call them to do? How does the shepherd language complete the passage?

**Reflect:** Consider how refusing retaliation differs from declaring that injustice does not matter.

## 05 Triumphing over them

**Main readings:** Colossians 2:8–15; Hebrews 2:9–18.

### Nailing it to his cross

Colossians warns against teaching that draws people away from Christ. It describes his fullness and headship over principality and power, then speaks of burial and resurrection with him, being made alive, and forgiveness of trespasses.

The account of forgiveness leads into the removal of the handwriting of ordinances that stood against us, pictured as nailed to the cross. Then comes a public triumph:

> And having spoiled principalities and powers, he made a shew of them openly, triumphing over them in it.

Colossians 2:15 · KJV

The cross appears as the occasion of victory within the same movement that describes forgiveness. The defeat of hostile powers and the removal of what stands against sinners are connected in the paragraph. Victory is not presented as a separate rescue that leaves sins unaddressed.

### Through death

Hebrews explains that Christ shared flesh and blood so that through death he might destroy the devil, described as having the power of death, and deliver those held in bondage by its fear. He enters the condition of those he saves.

The passage goes on to describe a merciful and faithful high priest who makes reconciliation for sins and helps those who are tempted. Triumph, priesthood, and compassionate help therefore belong in the same account. His suffering is connected with his ability to help his people in theirs.

These words offer grounds for confidence without pretending believers no longer encounter temptation, grief, or death. The letter speaks to people who still need help. Its answer is the one who has shared their condition and overcome the bondage it describes.

**Consider:** Which actions surround the triumph in Colossians? Why does Hebrews emphasise Christ's sharing flesh and blood?

**Reflect:** Consider how confidence in Christ's victory might change a fear without requiring you to pretend the struggle has already vanished.

## 06 Herein is love

**Main readings:** Romans 5:6–11; 1 John 4:7–12. **Return to:** 2 Corinthians 5:14–15.

### While we were yet sinners

Romans describes those for whom Christ died as without strength, ungodly, sinners, and enemies. God's love is shown in that condition rather than as a reward after they have become worthy of it.

> But God commendeth his love toward us, in that, while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us.

Romans 5:8 · KJV

The following verses speak of justification by his blood, salvation from wrath, reconciliation through his death, and salvation by his life. Love belongs within the saving action these terms describe. The paragraph gives no basis for portraying the Son as loving while the Father first has to be persuaded to care: Paul names the love demonstrated as God's.

First John likewise begins with God's love in sending his Son so that we might live through him. It calls the Son the propitiation for our sins. God's initiative, love, and dealing with sins are held together.

### We ought also to love one another

John draws a direct conclusion from God's love: believers ought to love one another. Paul similarly says that those who live should live for the one who died and rose for them. The cross changes the direction of life as well as the standing and condition of those saved.

This response does not replace sacrifice, redemption, reconciliation, sin-bearing, or victory. It grows out of what Christ has done. His love calls forth love, his service challenges ambition, and his mercy gives people a reason to live beyond themselves.

Read the six descriptions together while allowing each its own emphasis. Hebrews asks how sinners draw near; Peter names redemption from a former life; Paul describes peace, forgiveness, and triumph. Their shared centre is Christ's self-giving death, inseparable from the risen life to which these writers also direct their readers.

**Consider:** What condition were people in when God's love was demonstrated? What response does John draw from God's action?

**Reflect:** Name one way to love another person that follows from receiving mercy rather than trying to earn it.
