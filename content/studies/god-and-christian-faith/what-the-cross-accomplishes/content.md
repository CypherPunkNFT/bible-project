# The cross, the empty tomb and the throne

*What Jesus' death and rising did*

Concepts: Sacrifice · Ransom · Peace · In our place · Victory · Love · Risen · Reigning

Motto: One cross. Six pictures. / An empty tomb. A throne.

Lead: The New Testament never explains Jesus' death with only one picture. It borrows from the temple, the slave market, a broken friendship, a law court, a battlefield and a family. Then it says something that is not a picture at all: he was raised, and people saw him. This study walks six pictures of the cross, then the witnesses of the empty tomb, then the throne where the risen Christ now reigns and prays.

> For what I received I passed on to you as of first importance: that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that He was buried, that He was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures,

1 Corinthians 15:3–4 · BSB

Frame: The cross was a Roman way of executing people. The condemned person was fixed to a wooden beam and left to die in public. The first Christians called that death the heart of their *gospel*, which means "good news". This study teaches one reading habit for it. **Every picture of the cross has a home.** When a writer calls Jesus' death a sacrifice, a ransom or a victory, ask three questions. What world does the word come from? What does it say Christ did? Where does the writer stop pressing it? Each picture tells the truth. None of them tells all of it. When the study reaches the empty tomb, the habit changes, because the resurrection is not a picture. It is an event that people reported. There the question becomes: who saw what, and what did they say?

Selection: This is a selection, not a census. The New Testament has more pictures of the cross than six. Among them are the Passover lamb, the cleansing of the temple and the curtain torn in two. These six were chosen because each has a paragraph of its own in the letters or the Gospels. The story of Jesus' last meal, his prayer in Gethsemane and his trial is told in the site's Gospel harmony and is not retold here [check: link to the Gospel harmony's Passion section]. The meaning of the Lord's Supper belongs to the baptism and Lord's Supper study [check: that study's final title]. The resurrection body and the end of death are read in *The hope to come*.

## 01 One sacrifice, once for all

**Main readings:** Hebrews 9:15–28. **Quoted:** Romans 3:25–26.

*Every year the high priest went in again. Hebrews says Christ went in once.*

A *sacrifice* in the Bible is something given to God. Often it was an animal killed and offered at the altar, in the worship God gave Israel. Hebrews writes as though its readers knew that worship well, and that is where it starts.

### The Day of Atonement, in Jewish terms

In the Torah, the first five books of the Hebrew Bible, God gave Israel one day each year called the Day of Atonement, *Yom Kippur* in Hebrew (Leviticus 16). On that day the high priest went alone into the innermost room of the sanctuary, the Most Holy Place, carrying blood. There he made atonement for himself and for the people. *Atonement* means dealing with sin so that people can be at one with God again.

The temple in Jerusalem was destroyed in AD 70, and no sacrifices have been offered since. Jewish people keep Yom Kippur today as a solemn day of fasting, of prayer in the synagogue, and of *teshuvah*, "returning" to God in repentance. A prayer said on the High Holy Days speaks of repentance, prayer and charity [check: the wording of the *Unetaneh Tokef* prayer before publishing]. That is the day as Jewish life keeps it. What follows is how Hebrews, a Christian letter, reads it. Read more in *Leviticus: holiness and sacrifice*.

### Not again and again

Hebrews calls Christ "the mediator of a new covenant" (Hebrews 9:15). A *mediator* is a go-between who brings two parties together. A *covenant* is a binding promise between God and his people, sealed by a solemn act. The Greek word for covenant can also mean a last will, and Hebrews uses both meanings. A will "does not take effect until the one who made it has died" (Hebrews 9:17). At Sinai, Moses sealed the first covenant with blood (Hebrews 9:19–20, recalling Exodus 24). Read more in *Covenants and promises*.

Then the writer sets out the contrast. The high priest went in "every year with blood that is not his own" (Hebrews 9:25). Christ entered "heaven itself, now to appear on our behalf in the presence of God" (Hebrews 9:24).

> But now He has appeared once for all at the end of the ages to do away with sin by the sacrifice of Himself.

Hebrews 9:26 · BSB

In this sacrifice the priest and the offering are the same person. And it is offered once: "Christ was offered once to bear the sins of many; and He will appear a second time, not to bear sin, but to bring salvation to those who eagerly await Him" (Hebrews 9:28).

Hebrews also says "without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness" (Hebrews 9:22). Notice the honest word just before it: "*nearly* everything must be purified with blood". The law allowed a very poor person to bring flour instead (Leviticus 5:11). The point is not that blood works like a formula. The point is that sin costs a life, and forgiveness is never cheap.

Paul makes the same point in a law-court setting:

> God presented Him as the atoning sacrifice through faith in His blood, in order to demonstrate His righteousness, because in His forbearance He had passed over the sins committed beforehand. He did this to demonstrate His righteousness at the present time, so as to be just and to justify the one who has faith in Jesus.

Romans 3:25–26 · BSB

*Faith* here means trust. To *justify* is to declare a person in the right. The cross shows how God can do this and still be just. Read more in *Grace, faith and salvation*.

**Where the picture stops.** Hebrews does not describe what the heavenly sanctuary looks like, and it does not call Israel's worship meaningless. It calls that worship God-given "copies" (Hebrews 9:23), pointing past themselves.

**Consider:** What does Hebrews say happened "every year", and what happened "once for all"? Why does the writer put "nearly" before "everything"?

**Reflect:** If your sin has been dealt with once for all, what are you still trying to offer God again and again?

## 02 A ransom for many

**Main readings:** Mark 10:35–45. **Quoted:** Galatians 3:13.

*Two brothers ask for the best seats. Jesus answers with the price of a slave.*

A *ransom* was the price paid to set a captive or a slave free. To *redeem* means to buy back, to free someone at a cost.

### Seats beside him

James and John want places of honour: "Grant that one of us may sit at Your right hand and the other at Your left in Your glory" (Mark 10:37). Jesus asks whether they can drink his cup and "be baptized with the baptism I will undergo" (Mark 10:38). Here "baptism" is a picture of being plunged into suffering, not of baptism in water. The other ten are angry, and they are angry for the same reason the brothers asked. Everyone in the room wants to be first.

Jesus contrasts the rulers who "lord it over" others with his own people: "whoever wants to be first must be the slave of all" (Mark 10:42–44). Then he gives his reason.

> For even the Son of Man did not come to be served, but to serve, and to give His life as a ransom for many.

Mark 10:45 · BSB

"Son of Man" is the title Jesus most often used for himself. In this one sentence his death is both an example of service and the price of other people's freedom. A reader should not keep the first and drop the second. Example alone cannot free anyone. And the ransom is not paid so that his followers can go back to competing for seats.

Paul uses the same market word for the law's curse:

> Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us. For it is written: "Cursed is everyone who is hung on a tree."

Galatians 3:13 · BSB

Read more in *Covenants and promises*.

**Where the picture stops.** Mark does not say to whom the ransom was paid. Some writers in the early church pressed the picture until it named the devil as the one who was paid. The verse does not say that, and this study does not supply a recipient. What the verse does say is enough: the price was his life, and it was paid for many.

**Consider:** What were the disciples arguing about when Jesus spoke of the ransom? What does Mark say the Son of Man came to do, and what did he not come to do?

**Reflect:** Where in your life are you still asking for the seat on the right? What would serving look like there this week?

## 03 Be reconciled to God

**Main readings:** 2 Corinthians 5:18–21. **Quoted:** Ephesians 2:14–16.

*The one who was wronged makes the first move.*

To *reconcile* is to end a broken relationship and make peace. In our quarrels the guilty party usually has to go first. Paul turns that around.

### All this is from God

"All this is from God, who reconciled us to Himself through Christ" (2 Corinthians 5:18). God was the one wronged, and God acts first. He does it "not counting men's trespasses against them" (2 Corinthians 5:19). *Trespasses* are wrongs, crossing a line God drew. The verses just before this one, about new creation, are read in *A new creation*.

The message is then handed to people. "Therefore we are ambassadors for Christ, as though God were making His appeal through us" (2 Corinthians 5:20). An ambassador speaks for a king. He does not make the peace himself. He announces it and urges people to accept it: "Be reconciled to God."

> God made Him who knew no sin to be sin on our behalf, so that in Him we might become the righteousness of God.

2 Corinthians 5:21 · BSB

*Righteousness* means being in the right with God. This verse describes an exchange. Christ, who had no sin, is treated as sin. Those who trust him are counted as right with God. The verse does not make Christ a sinner; Paul says plainly that he "knew no sin". It means he stood where sinners stand.

### Peace between people too

Ephesians shows the same peace reaching across the deepest divide of its day, the one between Jews and Gentiles (people who were not Jewish):

> For He Himself is our peace, who has made the two one and has torn down the dividing wall of hostility by abolishing in His flesh the law of commandments and decrees. He did this to create in Himself one new man out of the two, thus making peace and reconciling both of them to God in one body through the cross, by which He extinguished their hostility.

Ephesians 2:14–16 · BSB

Read more in *The church and its calling*.

**Where the picture stops.** Reconciliation is not God simply deciding the quarrel no longer matters. Verse 21 shows what it cost. And peace with God gives no one permission to rebuild walls against other people.

**Consider:** Who acts first in 2 Corinthians 5:18–19? What is an ambassador given to do, and what is he not given to do?

**Reflect:** Is there someone you are waiting to hear from first? What would it look like to take the first step, as God did?

## 04 He bore our sins: Christ in our place

**Main readings:** 1 Peter 2:21–25 with Isaiah 53:4–6.

*He took what was ours so that we could be given what was his.*

[care] Peter wrote these verses to household slaves who were being treated unjustly (1 Peter 2:18). He tells them about Christ's patience. He does not tell anyone who is being harmed at home or at work to stay and bear it. If you are being abused, getting to safety and telling someone is not disobeying this passage. In the United States, call 911, or the National Domestic Violence Hotline at 1-800-799-7233 [check before publishing]. In Spanish-speaking countries, call your country's emergency number: 911 in Mexico and many others, 112 in Spain, and 016 in Spain for violence against women [check before publishing]. In Brazil, call 190 for the police, or Ligue 180 for violence against women [check before publishing]. What has happened to you can be told, and help is real. Read more in *Faith under pressure*.

### How Jewish readers hear Isaiah

Peter's words come from Isaiah 53, so begin with Isaiah, and with how Jewish readers hear it. Isaiah calls Israel God's "servant" more than once (Isaiah 41:8; 49:3). Many Jewish interpreters, among them the medieval commentator Rashi [check: Rashi on Isaiah 52:13–53:12], read the suffering servant as the people of Israel, bruised among the nations. They read the "we" of these verses as the kings of the nations, who find they had misjudged Israel (Isaiah 52:15). That reading takes the chapter's setting seriously, and Jewish readers hold it today.

> Surely He took on our infirmities and carried our sorrows; yet we considered Him stricken by God, struck down and afflicted. But He was pierced for our transgressions, He was crushed for our iniquities; the punishment that brought us peace was upon Him, and by His stripes we are healed. We all like sheep have gone astray, each one has turned to his own way; and the LORD has laid upon Him the iniquity of us all.

Isaiah 53:4–6 · BSB

Christians read the servant as Jesus, because the New Testament does (Acts 8:32–35; 1 Peter 2). They also note things in Isaiah itself: the servant is sent to bring Israel back (Isaiah 49:5–6), and he suffers without "deceit" (Isaiah 53:9). Read more in *Christ in the Old Testament*.

Look at who carries the guilt in verse 6: "us all". Scripture lays the weight of this death on everyone's sin, the reader's included. It gives no ground for blaming the Jewish people for Christ's death, then or now.

### In his body on the tree

Peter quotes Isaiah 53:9 for Christ's innocence: "He committed no sin, and no deceit was found in His mouth" (1 Peter 2:22). Then he joins two things together.

> He Himself bore our sins in His body on the tree, so that we might die to sin and live to righteousness. "By His stripes you are healed."

1 Peter 2:24 · BSB

Christ's patience is "an example" to follow (1 Peter 2:21). His bearing of sins is something no follower can copy. This is the centre of the cross, and the site says it plainly. Christ died *in our place*. He bore the punishment our sins deserved: "the punishment that brought us peace was upon Him." Theologians call this *substitution*. The Baptist Faith and Message 2000, article II (God), in its paragraph on God the Son, speaks of "His substitutionary death on the cross". The older Baptist confession of 1677/1689, chapter 8.5, says that by his sacrifice Christ "hath fully satisfied the justice of God" [check: exact wording and spelling against the site's creeds collection]. Anselm of Canterbury's *Why God Became Man* (*Cur Deus Homo*, about 1098) is a famous early defence of the idea that Christ made satisfaction for sin. Galatians 3:13 and 2 Corinthians 5:21, read above, say the same thing.

**A question readers ask: is it fair for an innocent person to be punished for the guilty?** The objection is serious. Some have pressed it hard, including the British pastor Steve Chalke in *The Lost Message of Jesus* (with Alan Mann, 2003) [check: that this is the book and its argument, before naming it]. The answer starts with who is on the cross. It is not a third party dragged in from outside. "God was reconciling the world to Himself in Christ" (2 Corinthians 5:19). The Judge himself, in the Son, bears what he could have demanded. And the Son goes willingly: he came "to give His life" (Mark 10:45). Read more in the site's short answer on this objection [check: title and link of the apologetics answer on the cross].

[care] "By His stripes you are healed" is about sin. Peter goes straight on to sheep going astray and returning to the Shepherd (1 Peter 2:25). Matthew applies Isaiah 53:4 to Jesus' healings of the sick (Matthew 8:17). Christians differ on how far bodily healing belongs to what the cross won. The Assemblies of God, a classical Pentecostal church, teaches that "Deliverance from sickness is provided for in the atonement" [check: current wording of its Statement of Fundamental Truths, section 12]. This site holds that Christ's death and rising will end all sickness at the resurrection, and that God may heal now. It does not promise that every believer will be healed in this life, because Scripture does not promise that. Read more in *What Scripture promises the sick*.

**Consider:** What in 1 Peter 2:21–25 can a follower copy, and what can only Christ do? In Isaiah 53:6, who has gone astray?

**Reflect:** Say verse 24 aloud with your own name in it. What changes if your sins were truly carried?

## 05 Triumphing by the cross

**Main readings:** Colossians 2:13–15; Hebrews 2:9–18.

*It looked like his defeat. Paul calls it a victory parade.*

### The debt nailed up

Colossians starts with the condition we were in: "dead in your trespasses". Then it says what God did: "God made you alive with Christ. He forgave us all our trespasses" (Colossians 2:13). The record of what we owed is pictured as a debt, cancelled and "nailing it to the cross!" (Colossians 2:14). Then comes the battlefield.

> And having disarmed the powers and authorities, He made a public spectacle of them, triumphing over them by the cross.

Colossians 2:15 · BSB

A Roman general who won a war paraded his captives through the city. Paul says the cross was that kind of parade. The "powers and authorities" are spiritual powers opposed to God. Read more in *Angels, demons and spiritual powers*.

Look at the order of the verses. The forgiveness comes first, and the victory comes in the same sentence as the cancelled debt. The Swedish Lutheran bishop Gustaf Aulén, in *Christus Victor* (1931), argued that victory was the early church's main way of describing the cross. The victory is real. But Colossians does not set it against Christ bearing our sins. It ties the two together, because the enemy's strongest charge against us was our debt, and that debt is gone.

### Through death

> Now since the children have flesh and blood, He too shared in their humanity, so that by His death He might destroy him who holds the power of death, that is, the devil, and free those who all their lives were held in slavery by their fear of death.

Hebrews 2:14–15 · BSB

The Son wins by entering our weakness, not by staying above it. He was "made a little lower than the angels" so that "by the grace of God He might taste death for everyone" (Hebrews 2:9). *Grace* means God's kindness to people who have not earned it. He is "not ashamed to call them brothers" (Hebrews 2:11). And because he had to be "made like His brothers in every way", he became "a merciful and faithful high priest". A *high priest* stood between God and the people, "to make atonement for the sins of the people" (Hebrews 2:17).

**Where the picture stops.** The victory does not mean that believers no longer face temptation, grief or death. Hebrews ends this passage by promising help, not escape: "Because He Himself suffered when He was tempted, He is able to help those who are being tempted" (Hebrews 2:18).

**Consider:** In Colossians 2:13–15, what comes before the triumph? According to Hebrews 2:14–15, what does the fear of death do to people?

**Reflect:** What fear has held you like a slave? What would it mean to face it as someone whose brother has already gone through death?

## 06 While we were still sinners

**Main readings:** Romans 5:6–11. **Quoted:** 1 John 4:9–10.

*No one dies for an enemy. God did.*

### Love that does not wait

Paul describes the people Christ died for, and each word is worse than the one before: "powerless", "the ungodly", "sinners", "enemies of God" (Romans 5:6–10). People sometimes die for someone they think worthy, but "Very rarely" (Romans 5:7).

> But God proves His love for us in this: While we were still sinners, Christ died for us.

Romans 5:8 · BSB

Notice whose love is proved: God's. The Father is not a reluctant judge whom a kinder Son has to win over. John says the same:

> This is how God's love was revealed among us: God sent His one and only Son into the world, so that we might live through Him. And love consists in this: not that we loved God, but that He loved us and sent His Son as the atoning sacrifice for our sins.

1 John 4:9–10 · BSB

Read more in *Knowing God*.

In both passages the love does something. It does more than display a feeling. "Since we have now been justified by His blood, how much more shall we be saved from wrath through Him!" (Romans 5:9). God's *wrath* is his settled, just opposition to evil. It is not a bad temper. The twelfth-century teacher Peter Abelard is remembered for stressing the cross as a display of love that wakes love in us [check: the place in Abelard's commentary on Romans before citing it]. That much is true: the cross does move us to love. But Paul's love rescues. A love that only inspired us, and left us under wrath, would not be the love of Romans 5.

The paragraph ends in joy: "we also rejoice in God through our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom we have now received reconciliation" (Romans 5:11). Read the verses before it in *Faith under pressure*. The verses after it, on Adam and Christ, are read in *Humanity, sin and renewal*.

[open] **For whom did Christ die?** Mark says "for many" (Mark 10:45). Hebrews says "for everyone" (Hebrews 2:9). Baptists who affirm the same confession read these verses differently. Calvinist Baptists hold *particular redemption*: Christ died to save, surely, those the Father gave him. They follow the 1677/1689 confession, chapter 8.5, which speaks of "all those whom the Father hath given unto him" [check: wording]. Non-Calvinist Baptists hold that Christ died for every person. The 2012 *Statement of the Traditional Southern Baptist Understanding of God's Plan of Salvation* is one statement of that view [check: its exact wording on the atonement before quoting]. Both affirm article IV (salvation), that salvation is "offered freely to all who accept Jesus Christ as Lord and Saviour" [check: wording], and article V (God's purpose of grace). This study does not choose. Read more in *Chosen and kept* [check: final title].

**Consider:** List Paul's four words for the people Christ died for. In 1 John 4:10, where does love begin?

**Reflect:** Do you live as though God will love you once you are better? Read Romans 5:8 again, slowly.

## 07 He is not here: the empty tomb

**Main readings:** Luke 24:1–12; Luke 24:36–43.

*The first people to hear the news were not believed.*

Now the reading habit changes. This is not a picture. It is a report, so ask: who saw what?

### Why look among the dead?

The women come early on the first day of the week, carrying spices for a body (Luke 24:1). They find the stone rolled away, and they "did not find the body of the Lord Jesus" (Luke 24:3). Two men in radiant clothes speak to them:

> "Why do you look for the living among the dead? He is not here; He has risen! Remember how He told you while He was still in Galilee: 'The Son of Man must be delivered into the hands of sinful men, and be crucified, and on the third day rise again.'"

Luke 24:5–7 · BSB

The empty tomb is explained by a word, and the word sends them back to a promise: "Then they remembered His words" (Luke 24:8). Luke names the witnesses: "Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary the mother of James, and the other women" (Luke 24:10). Then he tells something no one inventing a story would choose to tell: "their words seemed like nonsense to them" (Luke 24:11). Peter runs, sees "only the linen cloths", and goes away "wondering" (Luke 24:12). Luke does not polish the disciples' doubt away. Read Mary Magdalene's own meeting with Jesus in *Women of the Bible*.

### It is I myself

That evening Jesus stands among them and says, "Peace be with you" (Luke 24:36). They think they are seeing a spirit, a ghost.

> "Look at My hands and My feet. It is I Myself. Touch Me and see—for a spirit does not have flesh and bones, as you see I have."

Luke 24:39 · BSB

Then he eats "a piece of broiled fish" in front of them (Luke 24:42–43). *Resurrection* means this. It is not a ghost, and it is not a memory that lives on. Nor is it a corpse revived to die again. The same Jesus, with the same wounded hands, is raised bodily to a life that does not end. The walk to Emmaus and Jesus opening the Scriptures are read in *Scripture and its witness*. For the question "Did Jesus really rise?", see the site's short answers [check: titles and links of the apologetics answers on the resurrection].

**Where the account stops.** Luke tells of two men at the tomb. Mark tells of one young man (Mark 16:5). The Gospels each tell the morning in their own way. This study does not force them into one detailed timetable that none of them gives.

**Consider:** What did the women have to remember before they understood? What three things does Jesus do in verses 39–43 to show he is not a spirit?

**Reflect:** Jesus met their doubts with his hands, not with a scolding. What doubt would you bring to him?

## 08 What I received: the witnesses

**Main readings:** 1 Corinthians 15:1–11.

*Paul gives a list of names, and most of them could still be asked.*

### The gospel in four lines

Paul reminds the church in Corinth of "the gospel I preached to you, which you received, and in which you stand firm" (1 Corinthians 15:1). Then he gives it in four lines: Christ died for our sins, he was buried, he was raised on the third day, and he appeared (1 Corinthians 15:3–5). The words "received" and "passed on" are the language of handing down teaching. Paul did not make this up. It was given to him.

Two lines are marked "according to the Scriptures". The death and the rising fulfil what the Hebrew Scriptures had said. One phrase explains the death: "for our sins". Without that phrase, the cross would be only a tragedy.

### More than five hundred

> After that, He appeared to more than five hundred brothers at once, most of whom are still living, though some have fallen asleep.

1 Corinthians 15:6 · BSB

"Most of whom are still living": in effect, Paul is saying, go and ask them. The list goes on through Cephas (Peter), the Twelve, James and all the apostles, and ends with Paul himself, "as to one of untimely birth" (1 Corinthians 15:8). This is the witness list Paul gives. It is not a timeline of every appearance. It does not name the women, whom the Gospels put first, so read this list beside Luke 24, not instead of it.

Paul's own place in the list leads him to grace. "I persecuted the church of God. But by the grace of God I am what I am" (1 Corinthians 15:9–10). The man who once tried to destroy the church is himself evidence that the gospel is true.

"By this gospel you are saved, if you hold firmly to the word I preached to you" (1 Corinthians 15:2). Article V of the confession (God's purpose of grace) holds that true believers "will never fall away from the state of grace". The warning is one of the ways God keeps them holding on. Paul's argument about what the resurrection means for the dead, and about the body to come, follows in verses 12–58. Read more in *The hope to come*.

**Consider:** Which of the four lines are events, and which phrases explain them? Why does it matter that "most" were still living?

**Reflect:** Paul says "this is what you believed" (1 Corinthians 15:11). Could you say these four lines in your own words to a friend?

## 09 The throne: exalted and praying

**Main readings:** Philippians 2:9–11; Hebrews 4:14–16. **Quoted:** Acts 1:9–11; Ephesians 1:20–23; Hebrews 7:25.

*The story does not end at the tomb. It ends at the throne, with a prayer.*

A *throne* is a king's seat. To be *exalted* is to be raised to the highest place.

### Taken up

> After He had said this, they watched as He was taken up, and a cloud hid Him from their sight. They were looking intently into the sky as He was going, when suddenly two men dressed in white stood beside them. "Men of Galilee," they said, "why do you stand here looking into the sky? This same Jesus, who has been taken from you into heaven, will come back in the same way you have seen Him go into heaven."

Acts 1:9–11 · BSB

Read more in *The hope to come*. Paul says where Jesus went. God "raised Him from the dead and seated Him at His right hand in the heavenly realms, far above all rule and authority, power and dominion" (Ephesians 1:20–21). Read more in *Prayer in the letters*.

### Every knee

> Therefore God exalted Him to the highest place and gave Him the name above all names, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.

Philippians 2:9–11 · BSB

The "Therefore" points back to the Son humbling himself "to death—even death on a cross" (Philippians 2:5–8), which is read in *Who do you say that I am*. The cross leads to the throne. And here Scripture remembers Scripture. In Isaiah 45:23, God says every knee will bow to *him*. Paul gives that same honour to Jesus. To call him "Lord" is to give him the name God alone holds.

**Where the passage stops.** Every knee will bow, but these verses do not say that everyone will be saved. Some will bow in joy and some in defeat. The confession's article X (last things) teaches a judgement and two final destinies.

### He always lives to pray

Hebrews joins the throne to the cross by the word "priest".

> Therefore, since we have a great high priest who has passed through the heavens, Jesus the Son of God, let us hold firmly to what we profess. For we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but we have one who was tempted in every way that we are, yet was without sin. Let us then approach the throne of grace with confidence, so that we may receive mercy and find grace to help us in our time of need.

Hebrews 4:14–16 · BSB

Today the throne is a "throne of grace". A worshipper there finds not anger but help. And the risen Christ is not idle: "He is able to save completely those who draw near to God through Him, since He always lives to intercede for them" (Hebrews 7:25). To *intercede* is to pray on someone else's behalf. Read more in *Christ in the Old Testament*. The one who was "tempted in every way" (Hebrews 4:15) is the one praying for you now.

**Consider:** What does "Therefore" in Philippians 2:9 point back to? What two things does Hebrews 4:16 say we receive at the throne?

**Reflect:** What weakness do you hide from God because you think he would not understand it? Bring it to the throne of grace today, by name.

## Close: Read the pictures together

Way: Here is the habit to carry to the next passage. When a writer pictures the cross, ask where the picture comes from (the temple, the market, a broken friendship, a law court, a battlefield, a family), what it says Christ did, and where the writer stops pressing it. Then let the pictures sit side by side. Each tells the truth. None is the whole. At their centre stands one claim, taught here in the site's own voice: Christ died in our place, for our sins. When you reach the resurrection, change the question: who saw what, and what did they say? And do not stop at the tomb, because the story goes on to a throne and a prayer that has not ended.

Invite: If you have never trusted Christ, none of this asks you to earn anything. It asks you to *repent*, which means to turn from your sin to God, and to trust the one who died for sinners and was raised. "Be reconciled to God" (2 Corinthians 5:20). The confession puts it plainly: "There is no salvation apart from personal faith in Jesus Christ as Lord" (article IV). Then tell a Christian you know, and find a church.

Question: Which of the six pictures have you forgotten, and what would change if you believed that the risen Christ is praying for you today?
